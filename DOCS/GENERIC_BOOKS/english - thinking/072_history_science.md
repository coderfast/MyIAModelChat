Nicolaus Copernicus was born on February 19, 1473, in Torun, a city of the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland, and died on May 24, 1543, in Frombork, where he had served as a cathedral canon for much of his life. Johannes Kepler was born on December 27, 1571, in Weil der Stadt, Germany, and died on November 15, 1630, in Regensburg, being one of the most important mathematicians and astronomers in the history of science. The first law, published in 1609 in his work Astronomia Nova, states that planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one of the foci, breaking with the millennial tradition of perfect circular orbits. The Austrian philosopher Karl Popper (1902-1994) formulated one of the most influential criteria for distinguishing science from non-science: the criterion of falsifiability. According to Popper, a scientific theory must be falsifiable, that is, it must make predictions that can potentially be refuted by empirical evidence. If a theory is compatible with any possible result, then it is not scientific, because there is no experiment or observation that can demonstrate that it is incorrect. Popper proposed this criterion in response to the problem of induction, which refers to the logical difficulty of justifying generalization from particular observations: we can never observe all possible cases of a phenomenon, so we can never be completely certain that a theory is true. Instead of seeking the verification of theories, Popper proposed that science progresses through the elimination of falsified theories, a process he called "conjectures and refutations." This approach has important implications for scientific practice, as it implies that scientists should design experiments that attempt to refute their own hypotheses, rather than simply seeking confirmation. Falsifiability became one of the pillars of the philosophy of science in the 20th century and remains a fundamental criterion for evaluating the quality of scientific theories.
