## Chapter 11: Alfred Wegener and Continental Drift

### The Meteorologist Who Revolutionized Geology

Alfred Lothar Wegener was born on November 1, 1880, in Berlin, Germany, and died on November 12, 1930, on the ice cap of Greenland during a scientific expedition. Although trained as an astronomer and meteorologist, Wegener formulated one of the most revolutionary theories of twentieth-century geology: continental drift. In 1912, he first presented his hypothesis in lectures at the University of Frankfurt and the Geological Society of Berlin, and in 1915 published his masterwork "Die Entstehung der Kontinente und Ozeane" ("The Origin of Continents and Oceans").

Wegener proposed that all continents had once been united in a supercontinent called Pangaea (from the Greek "all lands") that had fragmented and slowly drifted over millions of years to reach their current positions. His hypothesis was based on five main lines of evidence: the morphological fit of the coastlines, especially the eastern coast of South America and the western coast of Africa, which fit together like puzzle pieces; the distribution of identical fossils on continents separated by oceans, such as the Mesosaurus (an aquatic reptile from the Permian) found in both Brazil and South Africa, the Glossopteris (an extinct plant) found in South America, Africa, India, Antarctica, and Australia, and the Lystrosaurus (a terrestrial reptile from the Triassic) found in Africa, India, and Antarctica; geological similarities between rock formations on separated continents, such as the Caledonian mountain chain that extends from Scandinavia through Scotland, Greenland, and North America; paleoclimatic evidence, such as the glaciations of the late Carboniferous and early Permian found in regions that currently have tropical climates, and the distribution of Carboniferous coal formations in regions that currently have cold climates; and paleomagnetic evidence, which shows that rocks from different continents record magnetic pole directions that are inconsistent with the current positions of the continents but consistent with a unified position.

Wegener's theory was rejected by most of the scientific community of his time, primarily because Wegener could not propose a convincing mechanism for the movement of the continents. Wegener suggested that the continents moved through the oceanic crust like a ship through water, but geophysicists calculated that the force needed to move the continents through the oceanic crust was too great to be produced by known forces. The scientific community also rejected Wegener's theory because he was a meteorologist and not a geologist, and because the theory contradicted the established geological paradigm of "contraction of the Earth," which held that the Earth had cooled and contracted gradually since its formation, creating mountains and oceanic trenches by contraction.

Wegener made four expeditions to Greenland between 1906 and 1930, studying the meteorology, glaciology, and geology of the island. During his fourth expedition, Wegener died on November 12, 1930, at the age of 50, probably from a myocardial infarction while transporting supplies between research stations. His body was found on April 23, 1931, by a rescue expedition. Despite the rejection of his theory during his lifetime, Wegener is now considered one of the most important scientists of the twentieth century, and his theory of continental drift laid the groundwork for the development of plate tectonics theory in the 1960s.

## Chapter 12: Harry Hess and Seafloor Spreading

### The Theory That Changed Geology

Harry Hammond Hess was born on May 24, 1906, in New York, and died on August 25, 1969, in Princeton, New Jersey. He was a geologist and officer in the United States Navy during World War II, and later a professor of geology at Princeton University. In 1962, he published a paper titled "History of Ocean Basins," which was published posthumously in 1963, in which he proposed the theory of seafloor spreading, one of the most important theories of twentieth-century geology.

Hess's theory proposes that oceanic crust is continuously created at mid-ocean ridges, where magma rises from the mantle and cools to form new oceanic crust, which moves away from the ridge as tectonic plates separate. Oceanic crust is continuously destroyed at subduction zones, where an oceanic plate subducts beneath another tectonic plate and melts in the mantle, completing a cycle of creation and destruction of oceanic crust that Hess called "the conveyor belt of the seafloor."

Hess proposed his theory based on several lines of scientific evidence: the distribution of seafloor sediments, which show that the youngest sediments are found near mid-ocean ridges and the oldest sediments are found far from the ridges; the distribution of magnetic anomalies on the seafloor, which show parallel bands of positive and negative magnetic anomalies that are symmetric with respect to the mid-ocean ridges; the age of the seafloor, which shows that the youngest oceanic crust is found at mid-ocean ridges and the oldest is found at oceanic trenches; and the topography of the seafloor, which shows submarine mountain chains (mid-ocean ridges) in the center of the oceans and oceanic trenches at the edges of the oceans.

Hess's theory was initially received with skepticism, but was confirmed by the magnetic evidence discovered by Frederick Vine and Drummond Matthews in 1963, and by the seismic evidence discovered by the United States Navy's network of seismic stations during the Cold War. The theory of seafloor spreading was a fundamental step toward the development of plate tectonics theory in the 1960s.

### Hess's Naval Career

Hess served in the United States Navy during World War II, where he commanded the USS Cayuga, a troop transport. During the war, Hess participated in amphibious operations in the Atlantic and Pacific, and had the opportunity to study the seafloor with sonar. Hess's observations of the seafloor during the war sparked his interest in marine geology, and after the war, he used the sonar data collected during the war to study seafloor topography.

Hess discovered seamounts (guyots) in the western Pacific, which were underwater mountains with flat tops that had been eroded by waves before sinking below sea level. These guyots provided evidence that the oceanic crust was subsiding as it moved away from mid-ocean ridges, which was consistent with his theory of seafloor spreading.

### The Mechanism of Spreading

The mechanism proposed by Hess for seafloor spreading is based on the convection of the Earth's mantle. According to Hess's theory, convection currents in the Earth's mantle rise toward the surface at mid-ocean ridges, creating tension that separates tectonic plates and allows magma to rise and cool to form new oceanic crust. The new oceanic crust moves away from the ridge as the plates separate, and the old oceanic crust sinks at oceanic trenches where it subducts beneath another tectonic plate and melts in the mantle.

The rate of seafloor spreading varies depending on the mid-ocean ridge. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at a rate of approximately 2.5 centimeters per year (1 cm/year per side), while the East Pacific Rise spreads at a rate of approximately 6 to 15 centimeters per year (3 to 7.5 cm/year per side). Slow-spreading ridges are associated with irregular topography and low magma volume, while fast-spreading ridges are associated with smooth topography and high magma volume.

Hess also proposed that the Earth's mantle was not a simple Newtonian fluid, but had viscoelastic properties that allowed slow long-term deformation but behaved as an elastic solid in the short term. This property of the Earth's mantle explained why tectonic plates could move slowly over the asthenosphere without causing continuous earthquakes.

The magnetic anomalies on the seafloor are parallel bands of oceanic rock with positive (normal) and negative (reversed) magnetization that are symmetric with respect to mid-ocean ridges. These magnetic anomalies form when magma rises from the mantle and cools at the mid-ocean ridge, recording the direction of the Earth's magnetic field at the time of its solidification. When the Earth's magnetic field has the same direction as the current magnetic field, the rocks record normal magnetization; when the Earth's magnetic field has the opposite direction, the rocks record reversed magnetization.

Vine and Matthews proposed that the magnetic anomalies on the seafloor are formed by seafloor spreading combined with reversals of the Earth's magnetic field. According to their hypothesis, when magma rises and cools at the mid-ocean ridge, it records the direction of the Earth's magnetic field at that time. As the oceanic crust moves away from the ridge, new crust forms at the ridge, recording the current direction of the Earth's magnetic field. If the Earth's magnetic field reverses during this process, the new rocks will record the opposite direction, creating alternating bands of magnetization that are symmetric with respect to the ridge.

The Vine-Matthews hypothesis was confirmed by geophysicist J. Tuzo Wilson in 1963, who published a paper showing that the magnetic anomalies on the seafloor were exactly those predicted by the Vine-Matthews hypothesis. Wilson also showed that the sequence of magnetic reversals on the seafloor was consistent with the sequence of magnetic reversals determined from potassium-argon dating of terrestrial volcanic rocks.

### Reversals of the Earth's Magnetic Field

The Earth's magnetic field reverses periodically, meaning the north and south magnetic poles swap positions. These magnetic reversals have occurred hundreds of times throughout Earth's history and have been recorded in magnetic rocks (rocks containing ferromagnetic minerals such as magnetite). The frequency of magnetic reversals varies, with periods of normal and reversed magnetic activity lasting from thousands of years to millions of years.

The chronology of magnetic reversals has been determined through potassium-argon dating of terrestrial volcanic rocks and uranium-lead dating of igneous rocks. The most widely used magnetic timescale is the magnetic timescale of geophysicist Allan Cox, first published in 1963 and revised several times since. The Cox timescale divides the magnetic history of the last 5 million years into periods of normal and reversed polarity, with each period designated by a letter and a number.

Magnetic reversals are caused by changes in the dynamics of the Earth's outer core, where the Earth's magnetic field is generated by the movement of liquid iron (the geomagnetic dynamo). Changes in convection currents in the outer core can cause magnetic reversals, but the exact mechanism of magnetic reversals is still not fully understood.

### Confirmation of Seafloor Spreading

The Vine-Matthews hypothesis provided the definitive confirmation of Hess's theory of seafloor spreading. The magnetic anomalies on the seafloor showed a pattern of symmetric bands that was exactly what was predicted by the combination of seafloor spreading and reversals of the Earth's magnetic field.

The rate of seafloor spreading could be determined by measuring the distance between magnetic anomalies and comparing it with the chronology of magnetic reversals. For example, the Mid-Atlantic Ridge has a spreading rate of approximately 2.5 centimeters per year (1 cm/year per side), meaning the oceanic crust moves away from the ridge at a rate of 1 cm/year on each side of the ridge.

The magnetic anomalies on the seafloor also provided evidence that oceanic crust is destroyed at subduction zones. The oldest oceanic crust is found at oceanic trenches, where the oceanic crust subducts beneath another tectonic plate and melts in the mantle, completing the cycle of creation and destruction of oceanic crust proposed by Hess.

## Chapter 14: Pangaea and Supercontinents

### The Supercontinent Cycle

Pangaea (from the Greek "all lands") was a supercontinent that existed during the late Paleozoic and early Mesozoic, approximately 335 to 175 million years ago. Pangaea formed through the collision of several continents and microcontinents during the late Carboniferous and early Permian, approximately 335 to 320 million years ago, and fragmented during the late Triassic and early Jurassic, approximately 200 to 175 million years ago.

Pangaea was formed by the union of the continents of Laurasia (which included North America, Europe, and Asia) and Gondwana (which included South America, Africa, India, Antarctica, and Australia), which in turn had formed through the collision of smaller continents during the Neoproterozoic and early Paleozoic. Pangaea extended from the North Pole to the South Pole and was surrounded by the superocean Panthalassa (from the Greek "all seas"), which covered approximately 70% of the Earth's surface.

The existence of Pangaea was postulated by Alfred Wegener in 1912, and was confirmed by geological, paleontological, paleoclimatic, and paleomagnetic evidence. Geological evidence includes the morphological fit of continental coastlines, similarities in continental rock formations, and the distribution of continental mountain chains. Paleontological evidence includes the distribution of identical fossils on continents separated by oceans. Paleoclimatic evidence includes the glaciations of the late Carboniferous and early Permian found in regions that currently have tropical climates. Paleomagnetic evidence includes the magnetic pole directions recorded in continental rocks that are inconsistent with current positions but consistent with a unified position.

### Supercontinents Before Pangaea

Pangaea was not the first supercontinent in Earth's history. Scientists have identified several supercontinents before Pangaea, including Rodinia (which existed approximately 1,100 to 750 million years ago during the Neoproterozoic), Columbia (or Nuna) (which existed approximately 1,800 to 1,300 million years ago during the Mesoproterozoic), Kenorland (which existed approximately 2,700 to 2,500 million years ago during the Paleoproterozoic), and Vaalbara (which existed approximately 3,600 to 2,800 million years ago during the Archean).

The existence of these supercontinents has been inferred from geological, paleomagnetic, and geochronological evidence. Rodinia, for example, has been reconstructed from the distribution of orogenic belts (fossil mountain chains), the ages of zircon crystals, and the magnetic pole directions recorded in continental rocks. The fragmentation of Rodinia approximately 750 million years ago was associated with a global glaciation known as "Snowball Earth," during which the oceans froze completely and the Earth's surface was covered by ice.

### The Wilson Cycle

The Wilson cycle is a geological cycle that describes the periodic formation and destruction of supercontinents throughout Earth's history. The Wilson cycle was proposed by Canadian geophysicist John Tuzo Wilson in 1966, and describes a complete cycle of opening and closing of ocean basins that lasts approximately 400 to 500 million years.

The Wilson cycle begins with the fragmentation of a supercontinent, which creates a new ocean basin that expands through the creation of new oceanic crust at mid-ocean ridges. As the ocean basin expands, the continents drift apart and the ocean becomes wider. Eventually, the expansion of the ocean stops and subduction of oceanic crust begins at the ocean margins, causing the ocean to narrow. Finally, the continents collide with each other, closing the ocean and forming a new supercontinent, completing the cycle.

The current Wilson cycle began with the fragmentation of Pangaea approximately 200 million years ago, and is in the phase of Atlantic Ocean expansion and Pacific Ocean contraction. The Atlantic Ocean is expanding at a rate of approximately 2.5 centimeters per year, while the Pacific Ocean is narrowing at a rate of approximately 3 to 5 centimeters per year. It is estimated that the next supercontinent, called "Pangaea Proxima" or "Novopangaea," will form within approximately 250 to 350 million years.

## Chapter 15: The Pacific Ring of Fire

### The Most Active Zone on the Planet

The Pacific Ring of Fire is a zone of intense volcanic and seismic activity that surrounds the Pacific Ocean in a horseshoe shape, with a length of approximately 40,000 kilometers and a width of up to 500 kilometers. The Pacific Ring of Fire hosts approximately 75% of the world's active volcanoes (about 452 of the 604 volcanoes identified as active) and 90% of the world's earthquakes, including the most powerful earthquakes ever recorded.

The Pacific Ring of Fire extends from the Kamchatka Peninsula in Russia, through the Kuril Islands, Japan, the Mariana Islands, the Philippine Islands, Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, the Solomon Islands, Fiji, Tonga, New Zealand, Antarctica, Chile, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, Central America, Mexico, California, Oregon, Washington, Alaska, the Aleutian Islands, and into eastern Siberia.

The volcanic and seismic activity of the Pacific Ring of Fire is caused primarily by the subduction of Pacific Ocean plates beneath the surrounding continental and oceanic plates. The Pacific Plate subducts beneath the North American Plate in the northeast, the Nazca Plate in the east, the Antarctic Plate in the southeast, the Indo-Australian Plate in the south, and the Eurasian Plate and Philippine Plate in the northwest.

### The Most Active Volcanoes of the Ring of Fire

The Pacific Ring of Fire is home to some of the world's most active and dangerous volcanoes. Mount Fuji (Fujisan) in Japan is a composite volcano with an altitude of 3,776 meters, the highest point in Japan. Fuji has erupted more than 100 times in the last 5,600 years, with the most recent eruption in 1707 (the Hōei eruption), which sent ash as far as Tokyo, 100 kilometers away. Mount Fuji is located at the junction of the Eurasian, Philippine, and North American plates, and is caused by the subduction of the Philippine Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate.

Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines is a composite volcano with an altitude of 1,486 meters before its 1991 eruption. The Pinatubo eruption on June 15, 1991, was the second-largest volcanic eruption of the twentieth century, with an eruptive column 34 kilometers high, ejection of 10 cubic kilometers of material, and ashfall over an area of 100,000 square kilometers. The eruption caused approximately 800 deaths and displaced more than 200,000 people, and cooled global temperatures by approximately 0.5 degrees Celsius over the following two years.

Mount Krakatoa (Krakatau) in Indonesia is a composite volcano located in the Sunda Strait, between the islands of Java and Sumatra. The Krakatoa eruption of August 26-27, 1883, was one of the most devastating volcanic eruptions in history, with an eruptive column 36 kilometers high, an explosion heard 4,800 kilometers away on Rodrigues Island in the Indian Ocean, and a tsunami up to 30 meters high that killed more than 36,000 people. The Krakatoa eruption removed two-thirds of the original island and caused a global temperature decrease of approximately 1.2 degrees Celsius over the following five years.

Mount Santa María in Guatemala is a composite volcano with an altitude of 3,772 meters. The Santa María eruption of October 24, 1902, was one of the largest volcanic eruptions of the twentieth century, with an eruptive column 28 kilometers high and ejection of 5 cubic kilometers of material. The eruption killed approximately 5,000 people and created the Santiaguito crater, which has been in continuous activity since 1922.

### The Most Powerful Earthquakes of the Ring of Fire

The Pacific Ring of Fire has recorded the most powerful earthquakes in history. The Valdivia earthquake (Chile) of May 22, 1960, with a magnitude of 9.5 on the Richter scale, is the most powerful earthquake ever recorded. The earthquake caused a tsunami up to 25 meters high that struck the coasts of Chile, Hawaii, Japan, the Philippines, New Zealand, and eastern Australia, killing between 1,000 and 6,000 people. The Alaska earthquake (Good Friday) of March 27, 1964, with a magnitude of 9.2, is the second most powerful earthquake ever recorded in North America.

The Indian Ocean earthquake of December 26, 2004, with a magnitude of 9.1, was caused by the subduction of the Indian Plate beneath the Burma Plate in the northern Indian Ocean. The earthquake generated a tsunami that struck the coasts of 14 countries, killing approximately 230,000 people, making it one of the deadliest natural disasters in history. The tsunami reached heights of up to 30 meters in Sumatra and traveled across the Indian Ocean to the coasts of East Africa, more than 5,000 kilometers away.

The Tōhoku earthquake (Japan) of March 11, 2011, with a magnitude of 9.1, was caused by the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North American Plate off the coast of Japan. The earthquake generated a tsunami up to 40 meters high that struck the coast of Japan, killing approximately 19,759 people, displacing more than 450,000 people, and causing the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster, the worst nuclear accident since Chernobyl in 1986.


## Chapter 16: Vesuvius - The Volcano That Buried Pompeii

### History and Geology of Vesuvius

Mount Vesuvius (Vesuvio in Italian, Monte Vesuvius in English) is a composite volcano (stratovolcano) located on the western coast of Italy, approximately 9 kilometers east of Naples and 25 kilometers southwest of Mount Somma. With an altitude of 1,281 meters above sea level, Vesuvius is the only active volcano in mainland Europe, and is considered one of the most dangerous volcanoes in the world due to the population density of its surroundings: more than 3 million people live in its immediate danger zone.

Vesuvius formed through the subduction of the African Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate, a process that began approximately 500,000 years ago and has created the volcanic chain of the Campanian arc in southern Italy. The Vesuvius volcanic complex consists of two overlapping volcanoes: Mount Somma, an older composite volcano that formed between 40,000 and 17,000 years ago, and the current Vesuvius cone, which formed inside the Mount Somma crater from eruptions that began approximately 17,000 years ago.

The most famous eruption of Vesuvius was the eruption of August 24, 79 AD, which destroyed the Roman cities of Pompeii, Herculaneum, Oplontis, and Stabiae. The eruption was documented by the Roman writer Pliny the Younger in two letters addressed to the historian Tacitus, which constitute the first detailed account of a volcanic eruption in the history of Western literature. Pliny the Younger described the eruptive column as having "the shape of a tall pine tree" (pinus pinea), a description that gave rise to the term "Plinian eruption" to designate large-magnitude volcanic eruptions with eruptive columns that reach the stratosphere.

### The Eruption of 79 AD

The Vesuvius eruption of August 24, 79 AD, began in the afternoon and lasted approximately 18 to 20 hours. The eruption was preceded by an earthquake with an estimated magnitude of 5 on the Richter scale, which caused minor damage in Pompeii and other nearby cities. The eruption was divided into two main phases: a pyroclastic phase, during which columns of hot gas and ash rose to a height of 20 to 30 kilometers and fell on the surrounding cities, and a pyroclastic flow phase, during which flows of hot gas, ash, and rock traveled at speeds of up to 700 kilometers per hour from the volcano to the surrounding cities, with temperatures of up to 700 degrees Celsius.

The first phase of the eruption lasted approximately 12 to 15 hours, during which the eruptive column rose to a height of 20 to 30 kilometers and generated a rain of pyroclasts (ash, lapilli, and volcanic bombs) over the surrounding cities. The pyroclastic rain was most intense in Herculaneum, located 7 kilometers from the volcano, where the ash layer reached a thickness of 20 meters. In Pompeii, located 10 kilometers from the volcano, the ash layer reached a thickness of 4 to 6 meters.

The second phase of the eruption was the most devastating. Approximately at midnight on August 25, the eruptive column collapsed, generating pyroclastic flows that traveled at speeds of up to 700 kilometers per hour from the volcano to the surrounding cities, with temperatures of up to 700 degrees Celsius. The pyroclastic flows reached Herculaneum at 1:00 AM, Pompeii at 6:30 AM, and Oplontis at 7:00 AM on August 25. The pyroclastic flows killed all the people in their path, including those who were attempting to flee the cities.

### The Death of Pliny the Elder

Pliny the Elder (Gaius Plinius Secundus) was a Roman writer and naturalist who died during the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 AD. Pliny the Elder was the commander of the Roman fleet at Misenum, and when he saw the eruption, he decided to approach the volcano by boat to investigate the phenomenon and rescue his friends. Pliny the Elder sailed from Misenum to Stabiae, 5 kilometers from the volcano, where he was caught by the pyroclastic flows and probably died from asphyxiation by volcanic gas. His nephew, Pliny the Younger, described his uncle's death in a letter addressed to the historian Tacitus, providing the first detailed account of a person's death by volcanic eruption.

### The Excavation of Pompeii

Pompeii was rediscovered in 1748 by the Spanish architect Joaquín de Alcubierre, who began excavations by order of King Charles III of Spain. The excavations of Pompeii continued during the eighteenth, nineteenth, and twentieth centuries, and have revealed an extraordinarily well-preserved Roman city, with buildings, frescoes, mosaics, inscriptions, artifacts, and human remains that provide a unique picture of daily life in a first-century AD Roman city.

Among the most important findings in Pompeii are the thermae (public baths), the forum (political and commercial center), the amphitheater (with a capacity for 20,000 spectators), the lupanars (brothels), the shops, the houses of wealthy citizens (such as the House of the Faun, the House of the Vettii, and the House of the Lovers), and the plaster casts of the eruption victims, which were created by pouring liquid plaster into the voids left by the victims' bodies in the solidified ash.

Herculaneum was first excavated in 1738, and the excavations have revealed an exceptionally well-preserved Roman city, with wooden buildings, frescoes, mosaics, papyri from the Villa of the Papyri, and human remains that provide a detailed picture of life in a first-century AD Roman city. Unlike Pompeii, which was buried by ash and lapilli, Herculaneum was buried by pyroclastic flows that solidified, creating a rock called "Herculaneum ignimbrite" that is extremely difficult to excavate.

## Chapter 17: Mount Etna - The Largest Active Volcano in Europe

### Geological Characteristics of Etna

Mount Etna (Mongibello in Italian, Gibel Mountar in Sicilian) is a composite volcano (stratovolcano) located on the eastern coast of Sicily, Italy, with an altitude that varies between 3,329 and 3,357 meters above sea level (the altitude varies due to eruptions that create and destroy the main cone). Etna is the tallest active volcano in mainland Europe and one of the largest active volcanoes in the world, with a basal perimeter of approximately 140 kilometers and a volume of approximately 500 cubic kilometers.

Etna formed through the subduction of the African Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate, a process that began approximately 500,000 years ago. Etna's volcanic activity has been continuous over the last 500,000 years, with more than 200 eruptions documented in the last 2,000 years. Etna has approximately 400 craters, including summit craters, secondary (parasitic) craters, and volcanic fissures.

The main cone of Etna is formed by layers of lava, ash, and pyroclasts from past eruptions, and is crowned by a complex of summit craters that change shape and position with each eruption. The current summit craters include the Central Crater, the Northeast Crater, the Southeast Crater, and the Bocca Nuova Crater, which formed in 1968. Etna also has several secondary (parasitic) craters on its flanks, including the Voragine Crater, the Lago Crater, the Filosofo Crater, and the Nerra Crater.

### History of Major Eruptions

Etna has had numerous major eruptions throughout its history. The 1669 eruption was one of the most devastating, with a lava flow that destroyed part of the city of Catania and killed approximately 20,000 people. The eruption began on March 11, 1669, with the opening of a fissure on the volcano's southern flank, and the lava flows advanced at a speed of approximately 1 meter per second, reaching the city of Catania on March 23 and destroying approximately 40 buildings, including the Catania Cathedral.

The 1928 eruption was the last eruption to cause fatalities on Etna, with 7 deaths. The eruption began on November 2, 1928, with the opening of a fissure on the volcano's northeastern flank, and the lava flows destroyed several towns, including Mascali, which was completely destroyed. The eruption lasted until December 2, 1928, and the lava flows covered an area of approximately 49 square kilometers.

The 1991-1993 eruption was the longest eruption of Etna in the last 300 years, lasting 473 days and producing approximately 500 million cubic meters of lava. The eruption began on December 14, 1991, with the opening of a fissure on the volcano's eastern flank, and the lava flows threatened the town of Zafferana Etnea, which was protected with containment barriers and redirection of the lava flows.

The 2002-2003 eruption was one of the most spectacular eruptions of Etna, with explosions that created an eruptive column 5 kilometers high, lava flows that reached 100 meters in width, and paroxysmal eruptions that lasted until January 28, 2003. The eruption caused disruption of air traffic at Catania airport and damage to the volcano's tourist infrastructure.

The 2021 eruption was the most recent eruption of Etna at the time of writing, with paroxysmal eruptions occurring regularly from February 2021 to October 2021. The paroxysmal eruptions created eruptive columns up to 10 kilometers high and lava flows that reached 3 kilometers in length.

### Etna as a World Heritage Site

Mount Etna was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2013, in recognition of its geological, scientific, educational, cultural, and historical importance. Etna is one of the best-studied volcanoes in the world, and has provided numerous contributions to volcanology, geology, geochemistry, and geophysics. Etna is also an important center of scientific research, with several volcanic observatories, including the Etna Volcanological Observatory (Osservatorio Etneo), which was founded in 1841 and is one of the oldest volcanic observatories in the world.

## Chapter 18: Krakatoa - The Eruption That Changed the World

### History and Geology of Krakatoa

Krakatoa (Krakatau in Indonesian) is a composite volcano (stratovolcano) located in the Sunda Strait, between the islands of Java and Sumatra, Indonesia. The original Krakatoa was an island approximately 5 kilometers in diameter and 813 meters above sea level, formed by volcanic eruptions over the last 2 million years. Krakatoa is located in a zone of extreme tectonic activity, where the Indian Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate, creating a chain of active volcanoes extending along the western coast of Sumatra and Java.

Before its catastrophic 1883 eruption, Krakatoa was a composite volcano with three main cones: Rakata (812 meters), Danan (447 meters), and Perboewatan (122 meters). The volcano had had several recorded eruptions, including an eruption in the seventeenth century that possibly destroyed much of the island.

### The 1883 Eruption

The Krakatoa eruption of August 26-27, 1883, was one of the most devastating volcanic eruptions in modern history. The eruption was preceded by several months of seismic and volcanic activity, including earthquakes and minor eruptions that began in May 1883. The main eruption began on August 26, 1883, at 1:00 PM (local time), with an eruptive column that reached 25 kilometers in height.

The catastrophic phase of the eruption began on August 27, 1883, at 10:02 AM (local time), when the volcano exploded with an estimated force of 200 megatons of TNT, equivalent to approximately 13,000 times the force of the Hiroshima atomic bomb. The explosion was heard 4,800 kilometers away, on Rodrigues Island in the Indian Ocean, making it the loudest sound ever recorded in history. The explosion also created an atmospheric pressure wave that circled the Earth at least three times, and generated a tsunami up to 30 meters high that struck the coasts of Java, Sumatra, and other islands in the Sunda Strait.

The Krakatoa eruption killed approximately 36,417 people, most of them from the tsunami that struck the coasts of Java and Sumatra. The eruption also destroyed approximately two-thirds of the original Krakatoa island, reducing its surface area from 75 square kilometers to 25 square kilometers. The resulting crater, the Krakatoa Caldera, has a diameter of approximately 6 kilometers and a depth of approximately 250 meters below sea level.

### The Krakatoa Tsunami

The tsunami generated by the Krakatoa eruption was one of the most devastating in history, with waves up to 30 meters high that struck the coasts of Java and Sumatra. The tsunami waves traveled across the Indian Ocean and reached the coasts of Sri Lanka, India, and even East Africa, more than 5,000 kilometers away. The cities of Anyer, Merak, Panimbang, Teluk Betung, and Pringsewu on Java were destroyed by the tsunami, and the coastal towns of Sumatra also suffered devastating damage.

The Krakatoa tsunami was recorded by tide gauges around the world, including in England, the United States, and Japan. The tide gauge measurements showed that the tsunami circled the Earth at least three times, and that the tsunami waves were detectable even on the coasts of Europe and America, more than 20,000 kilometers away.

### Anak Krakatoa

After the 1883 eruption, Krakatau remained relatively inactive for several decades, but in 1927 a new volcano began to emerge in the center of the submarine caldera. This new volcano, called Anak Krakatau ("Child of Krakatau"), has been in continuous activity since its formation, and has grown to an altitude of approximately 338 meters above sea level by 2023. Anak Krakatau has had several major eruptions, including a 2018 eruption that caused a tsunami that struck the coasts of Java and Sumatra, killing 437 people.

## Chapter 19: Pinatubo - The Eruption of the Twentieth Century

### The Discovery of Pinatubo

Mount Pinatubo is a composite volcano (stratovolcano) located on the island of Luzon, Philippines, with an altitude of 1,486 meters above sea level before its 1991 eruption. Pinatubo is located at the junction of the Eurasian Plate and the Philippine Plate, in a zone of extreme tectonic activity where the Eurasian Plate subducts beneath the Philippine Plate.

Before 1991, Pinatubo was virtually unknown to modern science, and was not known to be an active volcano because it had not erupted in the last 500 years. However, the volcano had had catastrophic eruptions in the past, including an eruption approximately 1,400 years ago that was one of the largest eruptions of the last 10,000 years, with an eruptive column 34 kilometers high, ejection of 10 cubic kilometers of material, and ashfall over an area of 100,000 square kilometers.

### The 1991 Eruption

The Pinatubo eruption of June 15, 1991, was the second-largest volcanic eruption of the twentieth century, after the Novarupta eruption in Alaska in 1912. The eruption was preceded by several months of seismic and volcanic activity, including earthquakes and minor eruptions that began in April 1991. The main eruption began on June 15, 1991, at 1:41 PM (local time), with an eruptive column that reached 34 kilometers high, penetrating the stratosphere.

The Pinatubo eruption was a Plinian eruption, with an eruptive column that rose to a height of 34 kilometers and generated pyroclastic flows that traveled at speeds of up to 600 kilometers per hour from the volcano to the surrounding valleys. The eruption ejected approximately 10 cubic kilometers of pyroclastic material, including ash, lapilli, volcanic bombs, and pumice, and generated pyroclastic flows that covered an area of approximately 400 square kilometers.

The Pinatubo eruption killed approximately 800 people, most from diseases related to ashfall and from the collapse of roofs covered in ash. The eruption also caused the displacement of more than 200,000 people, including 15,000 members of the United States Air Force from Clark Air Base, which was severely damaged by ashfall. Clark Air Base was subsequently closed and relocated.

### The Climatic Impact of Pinatubo

The Pinatubo eruption injected approximately 17 million tons of sulfur dioxide (SO2) into the stratosphere, causing a decrease in global temperatures of approximately 0.5 degrees Celsius over the following two years. The SO2 reacted with water vapor in the stratosphere to form sulfuric acid (H2SO4), which condensed into stratospheric aerosol particles that reflected solar radiation, causing global cooling.

The global cooling caused by the Pinatubo eruption was detected by weather stations around the world, and was used to validate climate models that predict the effect of volcanic eruptions on global climate. The Pinatubo eruption also caused a decrease in the stratospheric ozone layer, due to chemical reactions between SO2 and chlorine and bromine compounds in the stratosphere.

## Chapter 20: Mount Fuji - The Sacred Volcano of Japan

### Characteristics and Geology of Fuji

Mount Fuji (Fujisan) is a composite volcano (stratovolcano) located on the island of Honshu, Japan, 100 kilometers southwest of Tokyo. With an altitude of 3,776 meters, Fuji is the highest point in Japan and the tallest volcano in the country. Fuji is considered a perfect volcano for its symmetrical conical shape, which has inspired numerous works of art, including the famous woodblock prints by Katsushika Hokusai ("Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji").

Fuji formed through the subduction of the Philippine Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate, a process that began approximately 100,000 years ago. The current volcano was formed approximately 10,000 years ago by the merging of three earlier volcanoes: Komitake (an ancient volcano that formed approximately 100,000 years ago), Ko-Fuji ("Old Fuji," a volcano that formed approximately 100,000 years ago), and Shin-Fuji ("New Fuji," the current volcano that formed approximately 10,000 years ago).

### Eruption History

Fuji has erupted more than 100 times in the last 5,600 years, with eruptions that have produced lava flows, eruptive columns, and ashfall. The most recent eruptions include the Hōei eruption of 1707, which was the last significant eruption of Fuji and sent ash as far as Tokyo, 100 kilometers away. The Hōei eruption began on December 16, 1707, 49 days after the great Hōei earthquake (an earthquake with an estimated magnitude of 8.6 on the Richter scale), and lasted until January 24, 1708.

The Hōei eruption created a new crater on the southeastern flank of Fuji, called the Hōei Crater, which has a diameter of 1,140 meters and a depth of 300 meters. The eruption produced approximately 700 million cubic meters of pyroclastic material, including ash, lapilli, and volcanic bombs, which covered an area of approximately 1,700 square kilometers. The Hōei ash reached a depth of 2 to 4 centimeters in Tokyo and caused agricultural damage in the Kantō region.

Fuji has been quiet since the Hōei eruption in 1707, and scientists warn that the volcano could erupt again in the future. Seismic studies show that Fuji's magma chamber is filling with new magma, and that the pressure in the magma chamber is increasing. A 2004 study estimated that the probability of a significant eruption of Fuji in the next 100 years is approximately 5%.

### Fuji as Cultural Heritage

Mount Fuji was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2013, as Cultural Heritage (not Natural) in recognition of its cultural, artistic, and historical importance to the Japanese people. Fuji has been venerated as a sacred volcano in the Shinto religion for more than 1,000 years, and Mount Fuji has been the destination of religious pilgrimages since the seventh century. Climbing Fuji is a popular activity, with more than 300,000 people climbing the volcano each year during the climbing season, which runs from July to early September.

## Chapter 21: Kilauea - The Most Active Volcano in Hawaii

### Characteristics and Geology of Kilauea

Kīlauea is a shield volcano located on the island of Hawai'i, United States, with an altitude of 1,247 meters above sea level and a basal perimeter of 135 kilometers. Kīlauea is one of the five volcanoes that form the island of Hawai'i, and is considered the youngest active volcano of the Hawaiian volcanic chain. Kīlauea was formed by the activity of a hotspot in the Earth's mantle, which has created the Hawaiian island chain as the Pacific Plate moves northwestward over the hotspot.

Kīlauea has been in near-continuous activity since 1983, with eruptions that have produced more than 3.5 cubic kilometers of lava that have covered approximately 150 square kilometers of terrain. The most recent eruption before 2018 began in January 1983 with the opening of fissures on the volcano's East Rift, and continued for 35 years, until it ended in September 2018.

### The 2018 Eruption

The 2018 Kīlauea eruption was one of the most devastating eruptions of the volcano in recorded history. The eruption began on May 3, 2018, with the opening of fissures on the volcano's East Rift, in the Leilani Estates subdivision, in the Puna district of the island of Hawai'i. The volcanic fissures produced lava flows that destroyed more than 700 homes and covered an area of approximately 35.5 square kilometers of terrain.

The 2018 eruption also caused the collapse of the Halema'uma'u crater, an active crater at the summit of Kīlauea, which subsided approximately 500 meters, creating a new crater approximately 1.5 kilometers in diameter. The crater collapse generated earthquakes up to magnitude 6.9 on the Richter scale, which caused damage to the island's infrastructure.

Kīlauea's lava reached the ocean at several points, creating "lava entries" where the hot lava (at approximately 1,100 degrees Celsius) met the ocean water, generating steam clouds ("laze") that contained hydrochloric acid and volcanic glass particles. The lava entries created new terrain (called a "lava delta") that extended toward the ocean, but was unstable and could collapse suddenly into the ocean.

### Recent Activity

Kīlauea has had continuous activity since the 2018 eruption, with eruptions in the Halema'uma'u crater that have created a lava lake in the crater. In September 2023, Kīlauea erupted again, with paroxysmal eruptions that created eruptive columns up to 3 kilometers high and lava flows that covered the floor of the Halema'uma'u crater. The eruptive activity continued until December 2023, when the volcano entered a period of relative calm.

The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (HVO) monitors Kīlauea's activity continuously, using a network of seismographs, GPS, thermal cameras, and gas sampling. HVO issues eruption alerts and volcanic danger warnings for communities near the volcano, and provides the public with information about volcanic activity.

## Chapter 22: Types of Volcanoes

### Shield Volcanoes

Shield volcanoes are volcanoes with gentle slopes that form through eruptions of low-viscosity basaltic lava that flows easily over long distances from the eruption center. Shield volcanoes have a characteristic inverted shield shape, with slopes of 2 to 10 degrees, and can reach diameters of up to 600 kilometers and altitudes of up to 9,000 meters (from the ocean floor). Shield volcanoes are associated with hotspots in the Earth's mantle and with mid-ocean ridges, where low-viscosity basaltic lava flows easily to the surface.

Mauna Loa in Hawai'i is the largest shield volcano in the world, with an altitude of 4,169 meters above sea level and a volume of approximately 75,000 cubic kilometers. Mauna Loa was formed by the activity of a hotspot in the Earth's mantle, and has erupted more than 33 times in the last 200 years. The most recent eruption of Mauna Loa began on November 27, 2022, after 38 years of dormancy.

Mauna Kea in Hawai'i is another important shield volcano, with an altitude of 4,205 meters above sea level, the highest point in Hawai'i. Mauna Kea has been inactive for approximately 4,500 years, but is considered dormant, not extinct, because the Hawaiian hotspot activity continues.

Kīlauea in Hawai'i is an active shield volcano, with an altitude of 1,247 meters above sea level. Kīlauea has been in near-continuous activity since 1983, and is one of the most active volcanoes in the world.

### Composite Volcanoes (Stratovolcanoes)

Composite volcanoes (stratovolcanoes) are volcanoes with steep slopes that form through alternating eruptions of viscous (andesitic or rhyolitic) lava and pyroclastic material (ash, lapilli, and volcanic bombs). Composite volcanoes have a characteristic conical shape, with slopes of 30 to 35 degrees, and can reach altitudes of up to 7,000 meters. Composite volcanoes are associated with subduction zones, where oceanic crust subducts beneath another tectonic plate and melts in the mantle, creating viscous magma that erupts explosively.

Mount Fuji in Japan is a perfect composite volcano, with an altitude of 3,776 meters and a symmetrical conical shape. Fuji has erupted more than 100 times in the last 5,600 years, and the most recent eruption was the Hōei eruption in 1707.

Mount Vesuvius in Italy is a composite volcano, with an altitude of 1,281 meters. Vesuvius is famous for its 79 AD eruption that destroyed the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum.

Mount Santa María in Guatemala is a composite volcano, with an altitude of 3,772 meters. Santa María had a catastrophic eruption in 1902, which created the Santiaguito crater, which has been in continuous activity since 1922.

Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines is a composite volcano, with an altitude of 1,486 meters before its 1991 eruption. The Pinatubo eruption was the second-largest volcanic eruption of the twentieth century.

### Cinder Cones (Scoria Cones)

Cinder cones (scoria cones) are volcanoes with steep slopes that form through Strombolian eruptions of fragmented basaltic lava that accumulates around the crater. Cinder cones have a characteristic conical shape, with slopes of 30 to 40 degrees, and can reach altitudes of up to 300 meters. Cinder cones are associated with hotspots, mid-ocean ridges, and rift zones, where low-viscosity basaltic lava erupts explosively.

Paricutín in Mexico is a famous cinder cone that formed on February 20, 1943, in a cornfield in the state of Michoacán, Mexico. Paricutín grew rapidly, reaching an altitude of 424 meters in one year and a maximum altitude of 468 meters before becoming dormant in 1952. The Paricutín eruption lasted 9 years, and the lava flows destroyed the town of San Juan Parangaricutiro and covered an area of approximately 25 square kilometers.

Cerro Negro in Nicaragua is an active cinder cone, with an altitude of 488 meters. Cerro Negro formed on April 23, 1850, and has erupted more than 20 times since its formation. The most recent eruption was in 1999.

### Lava Domes

Lava dome volcanoes are volcanoes with very steep slopes that form through eruptions of extremely viscous rhyolitic lava that accumulates around the crater forming a lava dome. Lava dome volcanoes have a characteristic dome shape, with slopes of 40 to 50 degrees, and can reach altitudes of up to 500 meters above the surrounding terrain. Lava dome volcanoes are associated with subduction zones, where continental crust melts creating extremely viscous rhyolitic magma.

Mount St. Helens in the United States is a lava dome volcano that formed after the catastrophic eruption of May 18, 1980, which destroyed the volcano and created a crater 1.5 kilometers in diameter. After the 1980 eruption, a lava dome began forming in the crater, and has grown to reach an altitude of approximately 2,550 meters above sea level.

## Chapter 23: Types of Lava and Magma Chambers

### Types of Lava

Lava is magma that has reached the Earth's surface, and is classified into three main types based on its chemical composition, viscosity, and temperature: basaltic lava, andesitic lava, and rhyolitic lava.

Basaltic lava is the most common lava on Earth, and forms through partial melting of the Earth's mantle at mid-ocean ridges, hotspots, and rift zones. Basaltic lava has a composition of approximately 50% silica (SiO2), 10% alumina (Al2O3), 10% iron oxide (FeO), 15% magnesium (MgO), and 10% calcium (CaO). Basaltic lava has a low viscosity (101 to 103 Pa·s), a high temperature (1,100 to 1,200 degrees Celsius), and flows easily over long distances, creating gentle-sloping lava flows.

Andesitic lava has an intermediate composition between basaltic lava and rhyolitic lava, with approximately 60% silica (SiO2). Andesitic lava has a medium viscosity (103 to 106 Pa·s), a medium temperature (800 to 1,000 degrees Celsius), and flows shorter distances than basaltic lava, creating steep-sloping lava flows. Andesitic lava is the most common lava in subduction zones, where oceanic crust subducts beneath another tectonic plate and melts in the mantle.

Rhyolitic lava has a silica-rich composition (70% or more SiO2), and forms through the melting of continental crust in subduction zones. Rhyolitic lava has a high viscosity (106 to 1010 Pa·s), a low temperature (650 to 800 degrees Celsius), and flows very little, creating lava domes, volcanic domes, and very steep-sloping lava flows. Rhyolitic lava is responsible for the most explosive and destructive volcanic eruptions.

### Magma Chambers

Magma chambers are large reservoirs of magma that accumulate beneath volcanoes and feed volcanic eruptions. Magma chambers are found at depths of between 1 and 10 kilometers beneath volcanoes, and can have diameters of between 1 and 10 kilometers. Magma chambers form when magma rises from the mantle or from deep crust and accumulates in a zone of low pressure in the Earth's crust.

The temperature of magma chambers varies depending on the composition of the magma. Basaltic magma chambers have temperatures of 1,100 to 1,200 degrees Celsius, andesitic magma chambers have temperatures of 800 to 1,000 degrees Celsius, and rhyolitic magma chambers have temperatures of 650 to 800 degrees Celsius. The pressure in magma chambers increases when new magma rises from the mantle and accumulates in the chamber, and decreases when magma exits the chamber through eruptions or intrusions.

### Volcanic Eruptions

Volcanic eruptions are classified into several types based on the composition of the magma, the viscosity of the magma, the amount of dissolved gas in the magma, and the ascent rate of the magma. The most common types of volcanic eruptions include:

Hawaiian eruptions are effusive eruptions of low-viscosity basaltic lava that produces gentle-sloping lava flows that flow over long distances. Hawaiian eruptions are associated with shield volcanoes and hotspots, and are the least explosive type of eruption.

Strombolian eruptions are semi-explosive eruptions of fragmented basaltic lava that produces lava fountains, volcanic bombs, and short-distance lava flows. Strombolian eruptions are associated with cinder cones and composite volcanoes, and are moderately explosive.

Vulcanian eruptions are explosive eruptions of viscous andesitic lava that produces eruptive columns of ash, volcanic bombs, and pyroclastic flows. Vulcanian eruptions are associated with composite volcanoes in subduction zones, and are moderately to highly explosive.

Plinian eruptions are extremely explosive eruptions of extremely viscous rhyolitic lava that produces eruptive columns of ash that reach the stratosphere, pyroclastic flows that travel at speeds of up to 700 kilometers per hour, and ashfall that covers areas of up to 100,000 square kilometers. Plinian eruptions are associated with composite volcanoes in subduction zones, and are the most explosive and destructive type of eruption.

## Chapter 24: The Richter Scale and Seismology

### Measuring Earthquakes

The Richter scale (or local magnitude scale, ML) is a logarithmic scale that measures earthquake magnitude, developed by seismologists Charles Richter and Beno Gutenberg in 1935 at the California Institute of Technology (Caltech). The Richter scale was the first widely used earthquake magnitude scale, and although it has been partially replaced by the moment magnitude scale (Mw), it remains the most well-known scale to the general public.

The Richter scale measures the magnitude of an earthquake based on the maximum amplitude recorded by a Wood-Anderson seismograph, which is a torsion seismograph with a natural period of 0.8 seconds and a sensitivity of 2,800. Richter magnitude is calculated using the formula:

ML = log10(A) - log10(A0)

where A is the maximum amplitude recorded by the seismograph (in millimeters) and A0 is the amplitude of a reference earthquake at a distance of 100 kilometers.

The Richter scale is a logarithmic scale, meaning that each unit increase on the scale represents a 10-fold increase in seismic wave amplitude and an approximately 31.6-fold increase in energy released by the earthquake. For example, a magnitude 7 earthquake releases approximately 31.6 times more energy than a magnitude 6 earthquake, and approximately 1,000 times more energy than a magnitude 5 earthquake.

### Seismic Waves

Earthquakes generate two main types of seismic waves: body waves that propagate through the interior of the Earth, and surface waves that propagate along the Earth's surface.

Body waves are subdivided into two types: P waves (primary) and S waves (secondary). P waves are compression waves that propagate through the Earth at speeds of 5 to 8 kilometers per second, depending on the depth and composition of the rocks. P waves are the first to reach seismic stations, which is why they are called "primary." P waves can propagate through all media (solids, liquids, and gases), and are responsible for the initial shaking felt before an earthquake.

S waves are shear waves that propagate through the Earth at speeds of 3 to 5 kilometers per second. S waves are the second to reach seismic stations, which is why they are called "secondary." S waves can only propagate through solid media, and cannot propagate through liquids or gases, which was used to determine that the Earth's outer core is liquid.

Surface waves are subdivided into two types: Love waves (L waves) and Rayleigh waves (R waves). Love waves are horizontal shear waves that propagate along the Earth's surface at speeds of 3 to 5 kilometers per second. Rayleigh waves are orbital motion waves that propagate along the Earth's surface at speeds of 2 to 4 kilometers per second, producing an undulating motion similar to ocean waves.

### Seismographs

Seismographs are instruments that measure and record seismic waves generated by earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, explosions, and other sources of seismic energy. Modern seismographs are electronic instruments that use accelerometers or velocimeters to measure ground motion, and record data on digital computers.

The global seismic network consists of more than 4,000 seismic stations distributed around the world, which continuously record seismic waves and send data to processing centers in real time. The most important processing centers include the Pacific Earthquake Center (PDE) of the United States Geological Survey (USGS) in Golden, Colorado, and the Seismology Center of the Paris Geophysics Institute.

Modern digital seismographs have a sensitivity of up to 10-9 meters, allowing them to detect earthquakes of magnitude 1 or smaller. Seismographs can also detect earthquakes induced by human activities, such as mining, underground fluid injection, and underground nuclear tests.

### The Moment Magnitude Scale

The moment magnitude scale (Mw) is an earthquake magnitude scale that measures the magnitude of an earthquake based on the seismic moment, which is a measure of the total energy released by the earthquake. The moment magnitude scale was developed by seismologists Hiroo Kanamori and Thomas C. Hanks in 1979, and has replaced the Richter scale as the standard magnitude scale for earthquakes of magnitude 6 or greater.

The moment magnitude scale is calculated using the formula:

Mw = (2/3) * log10(M0) - 10.7

where M0 is the seismic moment in dyn·cm. The moment magnitude scale is a logarithmic scale, but unlike the Richter scale, it does not saturate for large-magnitude earthquakes, making it more accurate for earthquakes of magnitude 7 or greater.

The moment magnitude scale has a range of 0 to more than 10, and each unit increase on the scale represents an approximately 31.6-fold increase in energy released by the earthquake. The most powerful earthquakes ever recorded with the moment magnitude scale include the 1960 Valdivia earthquake (Chile) with a magnitude of 9.5, the 1964 Alaska (Good Friday) earthquake with a magnitude of 9.2, and the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake with a magnitude of 9.1.


## Chapter 25: The Most Destructive Earthquakes in History

### Ancient Earthquakes

Earthquakes have devastated civilizations throughout human history. The Aleppo earthquake of October 23, 1138, was one of the deadliest earthquakes in history, with an estimated magnitude of 7.1 on the Richter scale, killing approximately 230,000 people in the city of Aleppo (modern-day Syria) and its surroundings. The Shensi earthquake of January 22, 1556, in China was the deadliest earthquake in history, with an estimated magnitude of 8.0-8.3 on the Richter scale, killing approximately 830,000 people, mainly from the collapse of dwellings built in caves carved into loess (a soft sedimentary rock).

The Lisbon earthquake of November 1, 1755, was one of the most destructive earthquakes in European history, with an estimated magnitude of 8.5-9.0 on the Richter scale, killing between 30,000 and 100,000 people and destroying most of the city of Lisbon, Portugal. The earthquake was followed by a tsunami up to 6 meters high that struck the Portuguese coast, and fires that lasted five days and destroyed the buildings that had survived the earthquake and tsunami. The Lisbon earthquake had a significant impact on European Enlightenment thought, and prompted the development of modern seismology by the Marquis of Pombal, Prime Minister of Portugal, who organized a systematic survey of the earthquake's damage.

### Twentieth-Century Earthquakes

The San Francisco earthquake of April 18, 1906, with an estimated magnitude of 7.9 on the Richter scale, was one of the most destructive earthquakes in United States history. The earthquake killed approximately 3,000 people and destroyed more than 80% of the city of San Francisco, mainly from fires that spread after the earthquake. The earthquake was caused by movement of the San Andreas Fault, a transform fault 1,300 kilometers long that separates the Pacific Plate from the North American Plate.

The Tangshan earthquake of July 28, 1976, with a magnitude of 7.5 on the Richter scale, was one of the deadliest earthquakes of the twentieth century. The earthquake killed approximately 242,000 people (estimates range from 242,000 to 655,000), and caused devastating damage in the city of Tangshan, Hebei Province, China. The Tangshan earthquake was especially destructive because it occurred at 3:42 AM, when most of the population was sleeping, and because the city's dwellings were built with poor-quality materials that did not withstand the earthquake.

The Spitak earthquake (Armenia) of December 7, 1988, with a magnitude of 6.8 on the Richter scale, killed approximately 25,000 people and displaced 500,000. The earthquake destroyed the city of Spitak and caused severe damage in Gyumri (then Leninakan) and Vanadzor (then Kirovakan).

The Mexico earthquake of 1985, with a magnitude of 8.0 on the Richter scale, occurred on September 19, 1985, and killed between 10,000 and 40,000 people (estimates vary significantly), and caused devastating damage in Mexico City, mainly from the collapse of apartment and office buildings built on soft soils of the ancient Lake Texcoco.

### Twenty-First Century Earthquakes

The Indian Ocean earthquake of December 26, 2004, with a magnitude of 9.1 on the moment magnitude scale, was caused by the subduction of the Indian Plate beneath the Burma Plate in the northern Indian Ocean. The earthquake generated a tsunami that struck the coasts of 14 countries, killing approximately 230,000 people, making it one of the deadliest natural disasters in history. The tsunami reached heights of up to 30 meters in Sumatra and traveled across the Indian Ocean to the coasts of East Africa, more than 5,000 kilometers away.

The Haiti earthquake of January 12, 2010, with a magnitude of 7.0 on the moment magnitude scale, killed between 100,000 and 316,000 people (estimates vary significantly), and caused devastating damage in the capital Port-au-Prince, destroying the National Palace, the Parliament, the Port-au-Prince Cathedral, and thousands of buildings. The Haiti earthquake was especially destructive because the country had poor infrastructure and building codes that were not enforced.

The Tōhoku earthquake (Japan) of March 11, 2011, with a magnitude of 9.1 on the moment magnitude scale, was caused by the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North American Plate off the coast of Japan. The earthquake generated a tsunami up to 40 meters high that struck the coast of Japan, killing approximately 19,759 people, displacing more than 450,000 people, and causing the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster, the worst nuclear accident since Chernobyl in 1986.

## Chapter 26: Tsunamis

### What Are Tsunamis?

Tsunamis (from the Japanese 津波, "harbor wave") are long-wavelength ocean waves generated by vertical disturbance of the ocean floor, usually from submarine earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or submarine landslides. Tsunamis are different from wind waves, which are generated by wind action on the ocean surface. Tsunamis have wavelengths of up to 200 kilometers, wave heights of less than 1 meter in the deep ocean, and propagation speeds of up to 800 kilometers per hour in the deep ocean (equivalent to the speed of a passenger airplane).

As tsunamis approach the coast, the decreasing ocean depth causes the waves to slow down, increase in height, and decrease in wavelength, creating destructive waves that can reach heights of up to 30 meters or more. Tsunamis are especially destructive because the waves are not a single wave, but a series of waves (called a "wave train") that reach the coast at intervals of between 10 minutes and 2 hours, and each wave can be larger than the previous one.

### The Most Destructive Tsunamis in History

The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami was the most destructive tsunami in history, generated by the magnitude 9.1 earthquake that occurred off the coast of Sumatra on December 26, 2004. The tsunami struck the coasts of 14 countries, from Indonesia to Somalia, killing approximately 230,000 people. The tsunami waves reached heights of up to 30 meters in Sumatra, 10 meters in Thailand, 5 meters in Sri Lanka, 5 meters in India, and 9 meters in Somalia.

The 1946 Hawai'i tsunami was generated by a magnitude 8.6 earthquake that occurred in the Aleutian Islands on April 1, 1946. The tsunami struck the coasts of Hawai'i, killing 159 people, and traveled across the Pacific Ocean, causing damage in Chile, Peru, New Zealand, Japan, and other Pacific islands. The 1946 Hawai'i tsunami prompted the creation of the Pacific Tsunami Warning System (PTWS) in 1949, which is a tsunami monitoring and warning system that covers the entire Pacific Ocean.

The 1960 Chile tsunami was generated by the magnitude 9.5 Valdivia earthquake, the most powerful earthquake ever recorded, which occurred on May 22, 1960. The tsunami struck the coasts of Chile, killing between 1,000 and 6,000 people, and traveled across the Pacific Ocean, causing damage in Hawai'i (where 61 people died), Japan (where 142 people died), the Philippines (where 32 people died), and other Pacific islands.

The 1964 Alaska tsunami was generated by the magnitude 9.2 Good Friday earthquake, the second most powerful earthquake ever recorded, which occurred on March 27, 1964. The tsunami struck the coasts of Alaska, killing 128 people, and traveled across the Pacific Ocean, causing damage in Oregon, California, and Hawai'i.

### The Pacific Tsunami Warning System

The Pacific Tsunami Warning System (PTWS) is a tsunami monitoring and warning system that covers the entire Pacific Ocean. The PTWS was established in 1949 by the International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) in response to the 1946 Hawai'i tsunami. The PTWS is coordinated by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) of the United States, and consists of 32 tsunami warning centers in 28 countries.

The PTWS continuously monitors earthquakes that occur in the Pacific Ocean and issues tsunami warnings when an earthquake that could generate a tsunami is detected. The PTWS uses a network of seismographs, tide gauges, DART buoys (Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis), and satellites to monitor seismic and oceanic activity in real time.

## Chapter 27: Modern Volcanology and Monitoring

### Volcanic Monitoring Tools

Modern volcanology uses a variety of tools and techniques to monitor volcanic activity and predict volcanic eruptions. These tools include seismographs, GPS, thermal cameras, gas sampling, synthetic aperture radar (SAR), interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR), and lava and gas sampling.

Seismographs are the most important tool for volcanic monitoring, because earthquakes are the most common precursor of volcanic eruptions. Volcanic earthquakes occur when magma rises through the Earth's crust and creates stress in the surrounding rocks. Monitoring volcanic earthquakes can provide information about the location, depth, and size of the magma chamber, and about the ascent rate of magma.

GPS (Global Positioning System) is used to measure ground deformation caused by magma accumulation in the magma chamber. When magma accumulates in the magma chamber, the magma pressure causes the ground to uplift and deform. Monitoring ground deformation can provide information about the rate of magma accumulation in the magma chamber, and about the probability of an eruption.

Thermal cameras are used to measure the temperature of the volcano's surface and detect temperature changes that may indicate the presence of new magma beneath the surface. Thermal cameras can detect temperature changes of less than 0.1 degrees Celsius, and can be used to monitor the activity of craters, fumaroles, and lava flows.

Gas sampling is used to measure the composition and quantity of volcanic gases emitted from the volcano. The most important volcanic gases include sulfur dioxide (SO2), carbon dioxide (CO2), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and halogens (HF, HCl). Changes in the composition and quantity of volcanic gases can indicate changes in the volcano's activity, such as the ascent of new magma or the opening of new fissures.

Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) and interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) are used to measure ground deformation with millimeter precision. InSAR compares SAR images taken at different times to detect changes in the volcano's topography, which can be caused by magma accumulation in the magma chamber, lava eruption, or volcanic collapse.

### Prediction of Volcanic Eruptions

Prediction of volcanic eruptions is one of the main goals of modern volcanology. Although exact prediction of volcanic eruptions is not yet possible, volcanologists can determine the probability of an eruption based on continuous monitoring of volcanic activity.

Precursors of volcanic eruptions include increased seismic activity (volcanic earthquakes), ground deformation (volcanic uplift), changes in volcanic temperature (increased fumarole temperature), changes in the composition of volcanic gases (increased SO2 and CO2), and changes in hydrothermal activity (increased hot spring temperature).

Volcanic alert systems are used to communicate the level of volcanic danger to communities near the volcano. The most common volcanic alert levels include green level (normal activity, no danger), yellow level (elevated activity, potential danger), orange level (significant activity, probable danger), and red level (eruption in progress or imminent, immediate danger).

### Successes in Prediction

The prediction of the Mount St. Helens (United States) eruption in 1980 was one of the greatest successes of modern volcanology. Volcanologist David A. Johnston of the United States Geological Survey (USGS) monitored the volcano's activity for several months before the eruption, and detected precursors that included a magnitude 4.2 earthquake on March 18, 1980, uplift of the volcano's northern flank by 100 meters, and increased seismic activity. Johnston issued an orange-level alert on March 30, 1980, and a red-level alert on May 18, 1980, the day of the eruption. Johnston died during the eruption while monitoring the volcano's activity from an observation post 10 kilometers from the volcano.

The prediction of the Mount Pinatubo (Philippines) eruption in 1991 was another major success of modern volcanology. Volcanologists at the Pinatubo Volcano Observatory (PVO) detected eruption precursors several months before the eruption, including increased seismic activity, changes in the composition of volcanic gases, and ground deformation. The PVO issued a red-level alert on June 12, 1991, three days before the main eruption on June 15, which allowed the evacuation of more than 60,000 people and the closure of Clark Air Base, saving thousands of lives.

## Chapter 28: Volcanism in Spain

### Volcanoes of the Canary Islands

Spain is home to some of Europe's most active volcanoes, primarily in the Canary Islands archipelago, located in the western Atlantic Ocean, off the coast of Africa. The Canary Islands are a volcanic archipelago of seven main islands formed by the activity of a hotspot in the Earth's mantle, which has created a chain of volcanic islands extending for more than 500 kilometers.

Teide (3,718 meters above sea level) on the island of Tenerife is the tallest volcano in Spain and the third-tallest volcano in the world from its base (from the ocean floor, Teide has an altitude of approximately 7,500 meters). Teide is a composite volcano (stratovolcano) that formed approximately 150,000 years ago, and its last eruption was in 1909 (the Chinyero eruption). Teide was declared a National Park in 1954 and a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2007.

Timanfaya (525 meters above sea level) on the island of Lanzarote is a volcano that had a catastrophic eruption in 1730, one of the longest eruptions in Canary Islands history, lasting six years (1730-1736) and destroying approximately one-third of the surface of Lanzarote. The Timanfaya eruption created a spectacular volcanic landscape that is now the Timanfaya Natural Park, declared a National Park in 1974.

Cumbre Vieja (2,426 meters above sea level) on the island of La Palma is an active volcano that had a catastrophic eruption in 1949, and a recent eruption in 2021 (from September 19 to December 13, 2021). The 2021 Cumbre Vieja eruption lasted 85 days and produced approximately 200 million cubic meters of lava that destroyed more than 2,900 buildings, including homes, infrastructure, and banana plantations.

### Volcanoes of the Iberian Peninsula

The Iberian Peninsula also has volcanoes, although most are extinct or inactive. The most important volcanoes of the Iberian Peninsula include the volcanoes of the Santa Margarida caldera, a volcanic caldera in the Natural Park of the Volcanoes of the La Garrotxa region, in the province of Girona, Catalonia. The Santa Margarida caldera has a diameter of 400 meters and a depth of 100 meters, and was formed by a phreatic eruption (caused by the interaction of magma with groundwater) approximately 11,500 years ago.

The volcanoes of the La Garrotxa region are a group of more than 40 volcanic cones and lava fields that formed during the Quaternary (between 700,000 and 10,000 years ago). The most important volcanoes include Croscat (1,138 meters above sea level), the tallest volcano on the Iberian Peninsula, and Roca Negra (469 meters above sea level), the most visited volcano in the region.

### Recent Volcanism in Spain

The Cumbre Vieja eruption on La Palma, Canary Islands, from September 19 to December 13, 2021, was the most recent volcanic eruption in Spain and one of the most significant in Europe in recent decades. The eruption began in the southern compartment of the Cumbre Vieja volcano, at an altitude of approximately 1,000 meters above sea level, and lasted 85 days.

The eruption produced approximately 200 million cubic meters of lava that destroyed more than 2,900 buildings, including homes, infrastructure, churches, schools, and banana plantations. The lava covered an area of approximately 1,200 hectares and created new terrain that extended to the ocean, where the hot lava (at approximately 1,100 degrees Celsius) met the ocean water, generating steam clouds that contained hydrochloric acid and volcanic glass particles.

The Cumbre Vieja eruption also caused the evacuation of more than 7,000 people, who left their homes due to the proximity of lava flows and ashfall. The ashfall covered an area of approximately 1,000 square kilometers, and caused disruption of air traffic at La Palma airport for several days.

## Chapter 29: Seafloor Spreading in Depth

### The Process of Oceanic Crust Creation

Seafloor spreading is the process by which new oceanic crust is created at mid-ocean ridges, where tectonic plates separate and magma rises from the mantle and cools to form new oceanic crust. Seafloor spreading is one of the most important geological processes on the planet, because it creates most of the world's oceanic crust, which covers approximately 70% of the Earth's surface.

The process of oceanic crust creation begins when convection currents in the Earth's mantle rise toward the surface at mid-ocean ridges, creating tension that separates the tectonic plates. As the plates separate, basaltic magma rises from the mantle and cools on the seafloor surface, creating new oceanic crust. The new oceanic crust moves away from the ridge as the plates separate, and is replaced by new crust that forms at the ridge.

The rate of seafloor spreading varies depending on the mid-ocean ridge. Slow-spreading ridges (like the Mid-Atlantic Ridge) spread at rates of 1 to 5 centimeters per year, while fast-spreading ridges (like the East Pacific Rise) spread at rates of 5 to 15 centimeters per year. The rate of seafloor spreading determines the topography of the ridge: slow-spreading ridges have irregular topography with deep rifts and valleys, while fast-spreading ridges have smooth topography with shallow rifts.

### Oceanic Lithosphere

The oceanic lithosphere is the outer rigid layer of the Earth that forms the oceanic crust and the upper part of the mantle. The oceanic lithosphere has a thickness of approximately 7 to 10 kilometers (the oceanic crust) and 50 to 100 kilometers (the upper part of the mantle), for a total thickness of 57 to 110 kilometers. The oceanic lithosphere cools and contracts as it moves away from the mid-ocean ridge, and eventually becomes dense and cold enough to subduct beneath another tectonic plate at oceanic trenches.

The youngest oceanic lithosphere is found at mid-ocean ridges, where the oceanic crust has just been created, and the oldest oceanic lithosphere is found at oceanic trenches, where the oceanic crust is about to subduct. The age of the oceanic lithosphere ranges from 0 million years at mid-ocean ridges to approximately 200 million years at oceanic trenches, which is the maximum age of oceanic lithosphere before subduction.

The density of the oceanic lithosphere increases with age, because the oceanic lithosphere cools and contracts as it moves away from the mid-ocean ridge. The density of young oceanic lithosphere is approximately 2.9 g/cm3, while the density of old oceanic lithosphere is approximately 3.3 g/cm3. When the density of the oceanic lithosphere exceeds the density of the underlying asthenosphere, the oceanic lithosphere becomes unstable and begins to subduct, initiating a subduction zone.

### Magnetic Anomalies in Depth

The magnetic anomalies on the seafloor are parallel bands of oceanic rock with positive (normal) and negative (reversed) magnetization that are symmetric with respect to mid-ocean ridges. These magnetic anomalies form when magma rises from the mantle and cools at the mid-ocean ridge, recording the direction of the Earth's magnetic field at the time of its solidification.

The sequence of magnetic anomalies on the seafloor has been used to reconstruct the history of seafloor spreading and the velocity of tectonic plates. The magnetic anomalies on the seafloor are consistent with Hess's theory of seafloor spreading and the Vine-Matthews hypothesis, and have been used to create detailed maps of plate tectonics and the geological history of the oceans.

The magnetic anomalies on the seafloor have also been used to reconstruct the history of the fragmentation of Pangaea and the opening of the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific Oceans. For example, the magnetic anomalies on the Atlantic seafloor show that the opening of the Atlantic began approximately 200 million years ago, with the separation of the North and South American continents from the European and African continents.

## Chapter 30: Radiometric Dating Methods

### Radioactive Isotopes

Radiometric dating is a dating method that uses the radioactive decay of radioactive isotopes to determine the age of rocks and minerals. Radioactive isotopes (also called radionuclides) are unstable isotopes that spontaneously decay into stable isotopes at a known rate, called the half-life. The half-life is the time it takes for half of the atoms of a radioactive isotope to decay.

The most commonly used radioactive isotopes in rock dating include potassium-40 (40K), which decays into argon-40 (40Ar) with a half-life of 1.25 billion years; uranium-238 (238U), which decays into lead-206 (206Pb) with a half-life of 4.47 billion years; uranium-235 (235U), which decays into lead-207 (207Pb) with a half-life of 704 million years; rubidium-87 (87Rb), which decays into strontium-87 (87Sr) with a half-life of 48.8 billion years; and carbon-14 (14C), which decays into nitrogen-14 (14N) with a half-life of 5,730 years.

### Potassium-Argon Dating

Potassium-argon (K-Ar) dating is a dating method that uses the decay of potassium-40 (40K) into argon-40 (40Ar) to determine the age of igneous and metamorphic rocks. The K-Ar method is especially useful for dating volcanic rocks, because volcanic rocks contain minerals that incorporate potassium but not argon when they form, meaning that all the argon-40 present in the rock is produced by the decay of potassium-40 after the rock formed.

The K-Ar method has been used to date volcanic rocks around the world, including the volcanic rocks of the Pacific Ring of Fire volcanoes, the volcanic rocks of the Canary Islands, and the volcanic rocks of the Iberian Peninsula. The K-Ar method has been especially important for dating the volcanic eruptions of the Pacific Ring of Fire, which have been used to reconstruct the history of volcanic activity of the Ring of Fire.

### Uranium-Lead Dating

Uranium-lead (U-Pb) dating is a dating method that uses the decay of uranium-238 (238U) into lead-206 (206Pb) and uranium-235 (235U) into lead-207 (207Pb) to determine the age of igneous and metamorphic rocks. The U-Pb method is one of the most precise and reliable dating methods, and is used for dating rocks from millions to billions of years old.

The U-Pb method is commonly used for dating zircon minerals (ZrSiO4), which incorporate uranium but not lead when they form, meaning that all the lead present in the mineral is produced by the decay of uranium after the mineral formed. The U-Pb method has been used to date the oldest rocks on Earth, including the Jack Hills zircon in Western Australia, which has an age of approximately 4.4 billion years and is the oldest known mineral.

### Carbon-14 Dating

Carbon-14 (14C) dating is a dating method that uses the decay of carbon-14 (14C) into nitrogen-14 (14N) to determine the age of organic materials. Carbon-14 is produced in the upper atmosphere by the interaction of cosmic rays with nitrogen-14, and is incorporated into living organisms through photosynthesis and the food chain. When an organism dies, it stops incorporating carbon-14 and the existing carbon-14 decays at a known rate, allowing the age of the organism to be determined.

The carbon-14 dating method has a dating range of up to approximately 50,000 years, and is the most commonly used dating method for organic materials from the last 50,000 years. The carbon-14 dating method has been used to date fossils, archaeological remains, wood, bone, shell, and other organic materials.


## Chapter 31: Geothermal Resources and Tectonic Plates

### Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy is the thermal energy stored within the Earth, originating from the residual heat of the planet's formation, the decay of radioactive elements within the Earth, and heat transfer from the Earth's mantle to the surface. Geothermal energy can be used to generate electricity, heating, cooling, and industrial processes, and is a clean, renewable energy source that does not produce greenhouse gas emissions.

Geothermal energy is found everywhere in the world, but the most accessible geothermal sources are found in zones of tectonic activity, such as tectonic plate boundaries, hotspots in the Earth's mantle, and rift zones. In these zones, the heat from the Earth's mantle is closer to the surface, making geothermal energy extraction easier.

### Geothermal Power Plants

Geothermal power plants are facilities that generate electricity from geothermal energy. Geothermal power plants are classified into three main types based on the temperature of the geothermal resource: dry steam geothermal plants, flash steam geothermal plants, and binary cycle geothermal plants.

Dry steam geothermal plants are the simplest, and use geothermal steam directly to drive turbines that generate electricity. Dry steam plants are used in geothermal resources with temperatures above 180 degrees Celsius, such as The Geysers geothermal field in California, which is the largest geothermal field in the world with an installed capacity of 1,517 megawatts.

Flash steam geothermal plants use hot geothermal water (at temperatures above 180 degrees Celsius) extracted from deep wells, where the pressure is reduced, causing part of the water to flash into steam that is used to drive turbines that generate electricity. Flash steam plants are the most common, and are used in geothermal fields such as Hellisheiði in Iceland, which is one of the largest geothermal power plants in the world with an installed capacity of 303 megawatts.

Binary cycle geothermal plants use hot geothermal water (at temperatures of 100 to 180 degrees Celsius) to heat a secondary fluid with a low boiling point (such as isobutane or isopentane) that vaporizes and is used to drive turbines that generate electricity. Binary cycle plants are used in low-temperature geothermal resources, and are more efficient than dry steam and flash steam plants.

### Leading Countries in Geothermal Energy

The United States is the world's largest producer of geothermal energy, with an installed capacity of 3,714 megawatts in 2023, primarily in California (The Geysers) and Nevada. The Geysers geothermal field in California is the largest geothermal field in the world, with 15 geothermal plants generating electricity for more than one million homes.

Iceland is one of the world's most geothermally dependent countries, with approximately 30% of the country's electricity generated by geothermal energy and 90% of homes heated by geothermal energy. Iceland is located on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the North American and Eurasian plates are separating, creating a zone of extreme geothermal activity. Iceland's most important geothermal fields include Hellisheiði, Nesjavellir, and Krafla.

The Philippines is the world's second-largest producer of geothermal energy, with an installed capacity of 1,935 megawatts, primarily in the Tiwi, Makiling-Banahaw, and Tongonan geothermal fields. The Philippines is located on the Pacific Ring of Fire, where the Eurasian Plate subducts beneath the Philippine Plate, creating a zone of extreme volcanic and geothermal activity.

Indonesia is the world's third-largest producer of geothermal energy, with an installed capacity of 2,356 megawatts, primarily in the Java geothermal fields. Indonesia is located on the Pacific Ring of Fire, where several tectonic plates subduct beneath each other, creating a zone of extreme volcanic and geothermal activity.

Kenya is Africa's largest producer of geothermal energy, with an installed capacity of 861 megawatts, primarily in the Olkaria geothermal field. Kenya is located on the East African Rift, a continental rift zone where the African Plate is separating, creating a zone of extreme geothermal activity.

## Chapter 32: Plate Tectonics on Other Planets

### Plate Tectonics on Mars

Mars does not have active plate tectonics like Earth, but has evidence of past tectonic activity. The surface of Mars is divided into two distinct hemispheres: the northern hemisphere, which is low and flat, and the southern hemisphere, which is high and mountainous. This asymmetry is consistent with the hypothesis that Mars had a primitive form of plate tectonics in its early history, which was responsible for the formation of the Borealis basin (a huge impact crater in the northern hemisphere) and the volcanoes of the southern hemisphere.

Olympus Mons on Mars is the largest volcano in the solar system, with an altitude of 21.9 kilometers above the Mars reference level, a basal diameter of 600 kilometers, and a volume of approximately 1.2 million cubic kilometers. Olympus was formed by the activity of a hotspot in the mantle of Mars, which has created a chain of volcanoes extending across the Tharsis region. Olympus has been inactive for the last millions of years, but is not considered extinct.

### Plate Tectonics on Venus

Venus does not have active plate tectonics, but has evidence of significant tectonic activity. The surface of Venus is covered by tectonic structures, including folds, faults, volcanoes, and lava domes, which indicate that Venus has had an active tectonic history in the past. The absence of active plate tectonics on Venus is attributed to the high surface temperature of Venus (462 degrees Celsius), which prevents the subduction of the Venusian lithosphere.

Venus's volcanoes are numerous, with more than 1,600 volcanoes identified on the planet's surface. The largest volcanoes of Venus include Maat Mons (8 kilometers altitude), Maxwell Montes (11 kilometers altitude), and Sapas Mons (4 kilometers altitude). Venus's volcanoes were formed by hotspot activity in the mantle of Venus, and some may have been active until recently in geological time.

### Plate Tectonics on the Moons of Jupiter and Saturn

The moons of Jupiter and Saturn have significant geological activity that resembles Earth's plate tectonics. Io, one of Jupiter's moons, is the most volcanically active object in the solar system, with more than 400 active volcanoes producing eruptions of sulfur, silicates, and gases. Io's volcanic activity is caused by tidal heating, which is the heat generated by tidal forces exerted by Jupiter and Jupiter's other moons on Io.

Europa, another of Jupiter's moons, has an icy surface that shows evidence of tectonic activity, including faults, folds, and ice blocks that have been displaced. Europa's tectonic activity is caused by tidal heating and by the interaction of the icy surface with the ocean of liquid water believed to exist beneath Europa's surface.

Enceladus, one of Saturn's moons, has water geysers emanating from fractures in the surface, indicating geological and tectonic activity. Enceladus's geysers emanate from the south pole region, where tidal heating is most intense, and are believed to be connected to an ocean of liquid water beneath the moon's surface.

## Chapter 33: The Science of the Earth's Mantle

### Structure of the Mantle

The Earth's mantle is the layer of the Earth located between the Earth's crust and the outer core, with a thickness of approximately 2,900 kilometers. The Earth's mantle represents approximately 84% of the Earth's volume and 67% of the Earth's mass. The Earth's mantle is composed primarily of iron- and magnesium-rich silicate rocks, such as olivine, pyroxene, and garnet.

The Earth's mantle is subdivided into several layers based on its composition, temperature, and mechanical properties. The upper mantle layer extends from the base of the Earth's crust (at a depth of approximately 35 kilometers beneath continents and 7 kilometers beneath oceans) to a depth of approximately 410 kilometers. The upper mantle includes the lithosphere (the rigid layer that includes the crust and upper mantle) and the asthenosphere (the partially molten layer that allows the movement of tectonic plates).

The mantle transition zone extends from a depth of 410 kilometers to 660 kilometers, and is characterized by changes in the crystal structure of mantle minerals due to increasing pressure. Transition zone minerals include wadsleyite (at 410 kilometers) and ringwoodite (at 520 kilometers), which are forms of olivine under high pressure.

The lower mantle extends from a depth of 660 kilometers to the base of the mantle (at a depth of 2,900 kilometers), and is composed primarily of perovskite silicates (Mg,Fe)SiO3 and ferropericlase (Mg,Fe)O, which are minerals stable under high pressure and high temperature. The lower mantle is the most voluminous layer of the Earth, and is believed to contain most of the residual heat from the planet's formation.

### Mantle Convection

Mantle convection is the slow movement of mantle rocks due to differences in temperature and density. Mantle convection is the engine of plate tectonics, because convection currents in the mantle create forces that move tectonic plates over the asthenosphere. The velocity of mantle convection currents is approximately 1 to 10 centimeters per year, which is comparable to the velocity of tectonic plates.

Mantle convection occurs because the mantle is heated from below (by heat from the outer core) and cooled from above (by the Earth's surface). Heat from the outer core creates convection currents in the lower mantle, which rise toward the surface and create spreading zones at mid-ocean ridges. Cooling of the Earth's surface creates convection currents in the upper mantle, which descend toward the lower mantle at subduction zones.

Mantle convection currents are also responsible for hotspots in the Earth's mantle, which are regions where hot material from the deep mantle rises toward the surface and creates volcanoes and archipelagos. Hotspots in the Earth's mantle are believed to be associated with mantle plumes, which are columns of hot material that rise from the base of the mantle to the surface.

### Mantle Plumes

Mantle plumes are columns of hot material that rise from the base of the mantle to the Earth's surface, creating hotspots that produce volcanoes and archipelagos. Mantle plumes were proposed by Canadian geophysicist John Tuzo Wilson in 1963, and have been confirmed by seismic, geochemical, and geodetic evidence.

Mantle plumes have a diameter of 100 to 300 kilometers and a temperature 200 to 300 degrees Celsius higher than the surrounding mantle. Mantle plumes originate at the base of the mantle, near the boundary between the lower mantle and the outer core, and rise through the entire mantle to the surface. As a plume rises, the hot material of the plume expands and partially melts, creating magma that rises through the Earth's crust and erupts at the surface creating volcanoes.

The world's most important hotspots include the Hawaiian hotspot, which has created the Hawaiian archipelago; the Iceland hotspot, which has created the island of Iceland; the Canary Islands hotspot, which has created the Canary Islands archipelago; the Galápagos hotspot, which has created the Galápagos archipelago; and the Réunion hotspot, which has created the island of Réunion and the Réunion volcanic chain.

## Chapter 34: Supervolcanic Eruptions

### What Are Supervolcanic Eruptions?

Supervolcanic eruptions are volcanic eruptions of magnitude 8 on the Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI), which produce more than 1,000 cubic kilometers of volcanic material and cause global climate changes. Supervolcanic eruptions are the most explosive and destructive volcanic events on the planet, and can cause species extinction, ecosystem collapse, and global climate changes that last decades.

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) is a logarithmic scale that measures the explosivity of volcanic eruptions, from effusive eruptions (VEI 0) to supervolcanic eruptions (VEI 8). VEI 8 eruptions produce more than 1,000 cubic kilometers of volcanic material, generate eruptive columns more than 25 kilometers high, and cause global climate changes through the injection of enormous quantities of sulfur aerosols into the stratosphere.

### Supervolcanic Eruptions in History

The Yellowstone eruption (United States) 640,000 years ago was one of the largest supervolcanic eruptions in recent history, producing 1,000 cubic kilometers of volcanic material. The eruption created the Yellowstone caldera, a volcanic depression 72 by 55 kilometers in diameter that is now Yellowstone National Park. The Yellowstone eruption injected enormous quantities of ash and sulfur aerosols into the atmosphere, causing global climate changes that lasted decades.

The Toba eruption (Sumatra, Indonesia) 74,000 years ago was the largest supervolcanic eruption of the last 2 million years, producing 2,800 cubic kilometers of volcanic material. The Toba eruption created the Toba caldera, a volcanic depression 100 by 30 kilometers in diameter that now contains Lake Toba, the largest volcanic lake in the world. The Toba eruption injected enormous quantities of ash and sulfur aerosols into the atmosphere, causing a global volcanic winter that lasted between 6 and 10 years, and a decrease in global temperature of between 3 and 5 degrees Celsius for the following 1,000 years. Some scientists have proposed that the Toba eruption caused a bottleneck in the human population, reducing the world population to only a few thousand individuals, although this hypothesis is debated.

The La Garita Caldera eruption (United States) 27.8 million years ago was one of the largest supervolcanic eruptions in geological history, producing 5,000 cubic kilometers of volcanic material. The eruption created the Fish Canyon Formation, one of the largest volcanic formations in the world, extending across more than 300 square kilometers in the state of Colorado.

The Whakamaru Caldera eruption (New Zealand) 340,000 years ago was one of the largest supervolcanic eruptions in history, producing 20,000 cubic kilometers of volcanic material. The eruption created the Whakamaru caldera, which is now Lake Taupo, the largest lake in New Zealand. The Whakamaru eruption injected enormous quantities of ash into the atmosphere, causing global climate changes.

### The Danger of Future Supervolcanic Eruptions

Future supervolcanic eruptions are an improbable but potentially catastrophic event. The best-known active supervolcanoes include Yellowstone (United States), Campi Flegrei (Italy), Taupo (New Zealand), Toba (Indonesia), and the Canary Islands Caldera (Spain). These supervolcanoes show evidence of continuous activity, including earthquakes, ground deformation, gas emissions, and hydrothermal activity, which indicate that the magmatic systems are active.

The risk of a supervolcanic eruption at Yellowstone has been the subject of numerous scientific studies, and the results show that the probability of a supervolcanic eruption at Yellowstone in the next 10,000 years is approximately 1 in 730,000, which is extremely low. However, scientists warn that a supervolcanic eruption at Yellowstone would cause catastrophic damage in the United States and global climate changes, which is why it is important to continuously monitor the supervolcano's activity.

## Chapter 35: The Impact of Volcanoes on Climate

### Volcanic Aerosols and Climate

Volcanic eruptions can cause significant climate changes on a global scale, primarily through the injection of sulfur aerosols (SO2 and H2SO4) into the stratosphere. Sulfur aerosols reflect solar radiation back into space, causing cooling of the Earth's surface. The cooling caused by volcanic eruptions varies depending on the magnitude of the eruption, the amount of SO2 injected into the stratosphere, and the duration of the injection.

The largest volcanic eruptions can cause significant global cooling. The Mount Santa María eruption (Guatemala) in 1902 caused global cooling of approximately 0.3 to 0.5 degrees Celsius over the following two years. The Mount Pinatubo eruption (Philippines) in 1991 caused global cooling of approximately 0.5 degrees Celsius over the following two years, and a decrease in global precipitation of 1 to 3%. The Krakatoa eruption (Indonesia) in 1883 caused global cooling of approximately 1.2 degrees Celsius over the following five years, and a decrease in global temperature of up to 1.4 degrees Celsius during the summer of 1884.

### The Volcanic Sulfur Cycle

The volcanic sulfur cycle is the biogeochemical cycle of sulfur that involves the emission of SO2 by volcanoes, the formation of sulfur aerosols in the stratosphere, the deposition of sulfur aerosols on the Earth's surface, and the absorption of sulfur by living organisms. Volcanic SO2 reacts with water vapor in the stratosphere to form sulfuric acid (H2SO4), which condenses into aerosol particles that reflect solar radiation and cause global cooling.

Volcanic SO2 can also cause acid rain, which is precipitation with a pH below 5.6 due to the dissolution of sulfuric and nitric acid in rainwater. Acid rain can cause damage to terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems, corrosion of buildings and infrastructure, and degradation of air quality.

### Volcanic Eruptions and Climate Evolution

Volcanic eruptions have played an important role in the evolution of Earth's climate throughout geological history. Supervolcanic eruptions have caused global climate changes that have lasted decades or even centuries, and have been associated with mass extinction events, such as the Permian-Triassic extinction (252 million years ago), which was caused in part by the volcanic activity of the Siberian Traps, a huge igneous province that released enormous quantities of volcanic gases, including SO2, CO2, and methane.

Volcanic eruptions have also played an important role in the long-term regulation of Earth's climate through the volcanic carbon cycle. Volcanoes release CO2 from the degassing of the Earth's mantle, which is absorbed by silicate minerals on the Earth's surface through chemical weathering, creating a cycle that regulates the planet's global temperature. This volcanic carbon cycle has maintained Earth's climate within habitable ranges for life for billions of years.

## Chapter 36: Earthquakes Induced by Human Activity

### What Are Induced Earthquakes?

Induced earthquakes are earthquakes caused directly by human activity, such as underground fluid injection, mining, dam construction, oil and gas extraction, and underground nuclear tests. Induced earthquakes are different from natural earthquakes, which are caused by the movement of tectonic plates, but can have similar magnitudes and cause similar damage.

The most common induced earthquakes are caused by underground fluid injection, which increases the pore pressure in underground rocks and reduces friction on existing faults, causing the faults to activate and produce earthquakes. Underground fluid injection occurs in several human activities, including industrial wastewater disposal, oil and gas extraction (fracking), carbon capture and storage (CCS), and geothermal energy.

### Earthquakes Induced by Fracking

Fracking (hydraulic fracturing) is an oil and gas extraction technique that involves injecting fluids at high pressure into rock formations to create fractures that allow the extraction of oil and gas trapped in the rock. Fracking has been associated with induced earthquakes in several regions of the world, including the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and China.

Earthquakes induced by fracking are generally of low magnitude (less than 3.0 on the Richter scale), but earthquakes induced by the disposal of fracking wastewater can be of higher magnitude. In Oklahoma (United States), the injection of fracking wastewater into deep rock formations has caused a significant increase in seismic activity, with more than 900 earthquakes of magnitude 3 or greater between 2009 and 2016, compared to an average of 2 earthquakes per year before 2009.

The strongest earthquake induced by human activity in Oklahoma was the Prague earthquake of November 6, 2011, with a magnitude of 5.7 on the Richter scale, which caused damage to buildings and was felt in several states. The Prague earthquake was followed by more than 1,000 aftershocks, including 2 earthquakes of magnitude 5 or greater.

### Earthquakes Induced by Dams

Dams can induce earthquakes through the weight of water stored in the reservoir, which increases the pressure on the Earth's crust and can activate existing faults. Earthquakes induced by dams are generally of low magnitude (less than 5.0 on the Richter scale), but can cause damage to the dam's infrastructure and nearby communities.

The strongest dam-induced earthquake was the Koyna earthquake (India) of December 10, 1967, with a magnitude of 6.3 on the Richter scale, which was induced by the Koyna Dam, a 103-meter-high gravity dam in the state of Maharashtra, India. The earthquake killed 177 people and caused significant damage to the dam and nearby communities.

## Chapter 37: Plate Tectonics and Mineral Resources

### The Relationship between Tectonics and Mineralization

Tectonic processes play a fundamental role in the formation of mineral deposits, because the movements of tectonic plates create the conditions for the concentration of minerals in the Earth's crust. The world's most important mineral deposits are associated with specific tectonic processes, including subduction, seafloor spreading, continental collision, and hotspot activity.

Volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) deposits form at mid-ocean ridges, where ocean water circulates through hot oceanic crust and dissolves minerals that are deposited on the ocean floor when the cold ocean water meets the hot water from the ridge. VMS deposits contain metals such as copper, zinc, lead, gold, and silver, and are important sources of these metals.

Copper and gold porphyry deposits form in subduction zones, where subduction magma rises through the continental crust and deposits minerals in igneous rock formations. Porphyry deposits are the world's most important sources of copper and gold, and are found primarily on the Pacific Ring of Fire, in countries such as Chile, Peru, Mexico, Indonesia, and the Philippines.

Diamond deposits form in kimberlites, which are igneous rocks that rise from the Earth's mantle through diatremes (volcanic pipes). Kimberlites are associated with hotspot activity in the Earth's mantle, and the world's most important diamond deposits are found in hotspot activity regions, such as South Africa (Kalahari Hotspot), Russia (Siberian Hotspot), Canada (Slave Hotspot), and Australia (Kimberley Hotspot).

Lithium deposits are associated with volcanic activity in continental rift zones, where alkaline magma rises through the continental crust and deposits lithium in igneous rock formations and in geothermal brines. The world's most important lithium deposits are found in the South American Lithium Belt (Bolivia, Argentina, and Chile), which is a continental rift zone associated with the subduction of the Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate.

## Chapter 38: Glaciation, Climate, and Tectonics

### The Relationship between Glaciation and Tectonics

Tectonic processes significantly influence Earth's climate over the long term, including continental glaciation and glaciation-interglaciation cycles. Plate tectonics affects Earth's climate through several mechanisms, including the configuration of continents, the uplift of mountains, the opening and closing of ocean straits, and the emission of volcanic gases.

The configuration of continents affects Earth's climate because continents at high latitudes promote continental glaciation, while continents at low latitudes promote a warmer climate. For example, the fragmentation of Pangaea approximately 200 million years ago contributed to global cooling, because the fragmentation created new coastlines that increased evaporation and precipitation, which increased the weathering of silicate minerals and the absorption of atmospheric CO2.

The uplift of mountains affects Earth's climate because elevated mountains create barriers that block winds and create regional microclimates. For example, the uplift of the Andes Mountain Range approximately 10 million years ago contributed to global cooling, because the mountain range blocked atmospheric circulation and created conditions favorable for glaciation in the southern hemisphere.

### Milankovitch Cycles

Milankovitch cycles are periodic cycles in the Earth's orbit around the Sun that affect the distribution of solar radiation on the Earth's surface and cause glaciation-interglaciation cycles with periodicities of 100,000, 41,000, and 23,000 years. Milankovitch cycles were proposed by Serbian astronomer Milutin Milankovitch in the 1920s, and have been confirmed by geological, oceanographic, and glaciological evidence.

Milankovitch cycles include orbital eccentricity (the change in the shape of the Earth's orbit around the Sun with a periodicity of 100,000 years), obliquity (the change in the tilt of the Earth's axis with a periodicity of 41,000 years), and precession (the change in the direction of the Earth's axis with a periodicity of 23,000 years). These cycles affect the amount of solar radiation that the Earth's surface receives at different latitudes and seasons, which affects global temperature, ice distribution, and sea level.

Milankovitch cycles are not caused by plate tectonics, but interact with tectonic processes to produce the glaciation-interglaciation cycles that have occurred over the last 2.6 million years (the Quaternary period). Plate tectonics creates the conditions for glaciation (for example, the uplift of mountains at high latitudes, the opening of ocean straits that allow cold ocean circulation), while Milankovitch cycles modulate the intensity of glaciation through changes in solar radiation.

## Chapter 39: Plate Tectonics and Human Evolution

### The Influence of Tectonics on Evolution

Plate tectonics has significantly influenced human evolution through the creation of habitats, the formation of geographic barriers, and climate changes that affected the distribution and diversification of hominids. The fragmentation of Pangaea and the creation of the current continents created a variety of habitats that promoted the diversification of hominids, from African savannas to Asian tropical rainforests.

The formation of the East African Rift approximately 25 million years ago created a geographic barrier that separated hominid populations in East and West Africa, promoting allopatric speciation (speciation by geographic isolation). The East African Rift also created a variety of habitats, from deep valleys to elevated plateaus, that promoted the adaptation of hominids to different environmental conditions.

### Climatic Changes and Human Evolution

Climate changes caused by plate tectonics influenced human evolution through the creation of new habitats and the destruction of existing habitats. The formation of the Andes Mountain Range approximately 10 million years ago created a barrier that separated hominid populations in South and North America, promoting allopatric speciation. The formation of the Panama Canal approximately 3 million years ago connected North and South America, allowing the migration of species between the two continents.

Quaternary glaciation (between 2.6 million years ago and 11,700 years ago) created cooling and warming cycles that affected the distribution of hominids in Africa, Asia, and Europe. During glacial periods, hominids took refuge in warmer regions, such as East and Southern Africa, Southern Asia, and Southern Europe, while during interglacial periods, hominids expanded into colder regions, such as Northern Europe, Northern Asia, and the Americas.

### Human Migration and Tectonics

Plate tectonics has influenced human migration patterns through the creation and destruction of land bridges, the formation of geographic barriers, and sea level changes. The Bering Strait, which separates Asia from North America, was formed by the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North American Plate, and has been used as a migration route by hominids during glacial periods, when sea level dropped and exposed the continental shelf.

The Suez Canal, which connects the Mediterranean Sea with the Red Sea, was formed by plate tectonics and the opening of the East African Rift. The Suez Canal has been a geographic barrier to human migration between Africa and Asia, and has influenced hominid migration patterns for millions of years.

## Chapter 40: The Future of Tectonic Research

### New Technologies

Tectonic research is advancing rapidly thanks to the development of new technologies that allow a better understanding of tectonic processes. These technologies include Earth observation satellites, high-density seismograph networks, numerical models of mantle convection, and high-precision dating techniques.

Earth observation satellites, such as synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellites, allow ground deformation to be measured with millimeter precision, enabling the monitoring of tectonic plate movement, volcanic deformation, and stress accumulation on faults. GRACE satellites (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) measure changes in the Earth's gravitational field, which can be used to detect changes in mass distribution in the Earth's crust and mantle.

High-density seismograph networks, such as permanent and temporary seismograph networks, allow better earthquake location and better imaging of the Earth's internal structure. High-density seismograph networks are used to map the structure of the Earth's mantle, detect mantle plumes, and monitor volcanic activity.

Numerical models of mantle convection allow the simulation of Earth's mantle convection processes and the prediction of tectonic plate movement over the long term. Numerical models of mantle convection are used to reconstruct the history of plate tectonics, predict the formation of future supercontinents, and assess the risk of volcanic eruptions and earthquakes.

High-precision dating techniques, such as high-precision uranium-lead dating (U-Pb SHRIMP), allow rock dating with a precision of less than 0.1%, enabling a better understanding of the chronology of geological events and the velocity of tectonic processes.

### Major Challenges

The major challenges of tectonic research include earthquake prediction, understanding the mechanisms of volcanic eruptions, assessing the risk of supervolcanic eruptions, and understanding the relationship between plate tectonics and Earth's climate.

Earthquake prediction remains one of the great challenges of seismology, because earthquakes are chaotic events that are difficult to predict precisely. However, advances in seismic monitoring, numerical modeling, and understanding of earthquake precursors are improving the ability to predict earthquakes in the short term.

Understanding the mechanisms of volcanic eruptions is a major challenge because volcanic eruptions are complex events involving physical and chemical processes in the magma chamber, the volcanic conduit, and the volcano's surface. Advances in volcanic monitoring, numerical modeling, and geochemistry are improving the understanding of volcanic eruption mechanisms.

Assessing the risk of supervolcanic eruptions is a major challenge because supervolcanic eruptions are extremely rare but potentially catastrophic events. Advances in supervolcano monitoring, reconstruction of past eruptions, and numerical modeling are improving the assessment of supervolcanic eruption risk.

Understanding the relationship between plate tectonics and Earth's climate is a major challenge because plate tectonics affects Earth's climate through multiple mechanisms that interact with each other. Advances in the reconstruction of plate tectonics, reconstruction of Earth's climate, and numerical modeling are improving the understanding of the relationship between plate tectonics and Earth's climate.
