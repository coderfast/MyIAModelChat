The theory of plate tectonics is the geological theory that describes the movement of tectonic plates, large blocks of Earth's crust that move slowly over the asthenosphere, a partially molten layer of the upper mantle, and that interact with each other at their boundaries, creating geological phenomena such as earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges, oceanic trenches, rifts, and other forms of terrestrial relief. The theory of plate tectonics was developed in the 1960s by scientists such as Alfred Wegener, Harry Hess, Frederick Vine, Drummond Matthews, and John Tuzo Wilson, who proposed that the Earth's crust is fragmented into large tectonic plates that move over the asthenosphere, driven by convection currents in the Earth's mantle. The theory of plate tectonics is based on several lines of scientific evidence, including the fit of the continents, which fit together like pieces of a puzzle when joined, suggesting they were united in the past; the distribution of fossils, where identical fossils are found on continents separated by oceans, suggesting they were once connected; geological similarities, where rock formations, minerals, and geological structures are similar on continents separated by oceans, suggesting they were once connected; paleomagnetic evidence, where magnetic rocks record the direction of the Earth's magnetic field at the time of their formation and show distribution patterns consistent with the movement of tectonic plates; the distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes, which concentrate at the boundaries of tectonic plates, suggesting that tectonic activity is related to plate movement; and satellite measurements, which show that tectonic plates move at a speed of one to ten centimeters per year, comparable to the rate of human fingernail growth.
