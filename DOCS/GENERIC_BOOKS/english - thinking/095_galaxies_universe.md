The expansion of the universe is one of the most revolutionary discoveries in the history of science. In 1929, Edwin Hubble discovered that distant galaxies are moving away from us with velocities proportional to their distance, a phenomenon known as Hubble's Law or the Hubble-Lemaître Law. This relationship is expressed as v = H₀ × d, where v is the recession velocity, H₀ is the Hubble constant, and d is the distance to the galaxy. The Hubble constant is currently estimated at approximately 70 kilometers per second per megaparsec, meaning that a galaxy at a distance of 1 megaparsec (3.26 million light-years) is receding at 70 kilometers per second. The expansion of the universe does not mean that galaxies are moving away from us in particular, but rather that space-time itself is expanding, increasing the distances between all objects.

Dark matter is a form of matter that does not emit, absorb, or reflect light, making it invisible to conventional telescopes. Dark matter represents approximately 85% of the total matter in the universe and is fundamental to large-scale structure formation.
