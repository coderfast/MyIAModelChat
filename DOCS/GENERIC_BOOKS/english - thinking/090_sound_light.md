Sound is a mechanical vibration that propagates as a longitudinal wave through a material medium, such as air, water, or solids. Unlike light, sound cannot propagate in a vacuum, as it requires a material medium to transmit the vibration. When an object vibrates, it compresses and decompresses the molecules of the surrounding air, creating sound waves that propagate spherically from the source. Sound is characterized by its frequency, amplitude, wavelength, and timbre. Frequency determines the pitch of the sound, and is measured in hertz (Hz). The human ear can perceive sounds with frequencies between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz, although this ability decreases with age. Amplitude determines the intensity or volume of the sound, and is measured in decibels (dB). Timbre depends on the waveform of the sound, which in turn depends on the harmonics that accompany the fundamental note. Sound waves are longitudinal waves, meaning that the particles of the medium vibrate in the same direction as the wave's propagation. When a sound wave propagates in air, it creates zones of compression (where molecules are closer together) and zones of rarefaction (where molecules are farther apart). Interference of sound waves occurs when two or more waves overlap at the same point in space. If the waves are in phase (their crests coincide), constructive interference occurs, and the resulting amplitude is the sum of the individual amplitudes. If the waves are in opposite phase (a crest coincides with a trough), destructive interference occurs, and the resulting amplitude is the difference of the individual amplitudes. Destructive interference is used in active noise cancelers, which generate a wave with the same amplitude but in opposite phase to the noise, canceling it. This principle is applied in noise-canceling headphones, car sound systems, and hearing protection in noisy industries.

Resonance is a phenomenon that occurs when an object vibrates at its natural frequency of oscillation. Resonance can be useful, as in musical instruments, where the resonance of the body amplifies the sound. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 due to wind-induced resonant oscillations. The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons when light strikes a material. This phenomenon, discovered by Heinrich Hertz in 1887, could not be explained by the wave theory of light, since the energy of the emitted electrons depended on the frequency of the light, not its intensity. Albert Einstein explained the photoelectric effect in 1905 by introducing the concept of photons, discrete packets of energy with energy E = hf, where h is Planck's constant and f is the frequency. The maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons is Ec = hf - W, where W is the work function of the material (the minimum energy needed to remove an electron). This discovery was fundamental to the development of quantum mechanics and earned Einstein the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1921.

Sound and light are fundamental in numerous industrial applications. Ultrasound is used in ultrasonic welding (joining plastics without external heat), ultrasonic cleaning (removing dirt through cavitation), thickness measurement (time-of-flight technology), and non-destructive testing (detecting cracks and defects in materials). Laser light is used in metal cutting and welding (micrometer-level precision), engraving and marking (product identification), 3D printing (selective laser sintering), and interferometric measurement (nanometer precision). Industrial spectroscopy is used in quality control of chemical processes, detection of impurities in food and pharmaceuticals, and monitoring of polluting gas emissions. Optical and acoustic sensors are critical components in automation systems, robotics, and machine vision for Industry 4.0.
