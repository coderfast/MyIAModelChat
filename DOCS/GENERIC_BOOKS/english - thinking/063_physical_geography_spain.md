# Physical Geography of Spain

## Chapter 1: The Pyrenees

### The Pyrenees: The Natural Barrier between Spain and France

The Pyrenees are a mountain range that extends from the Atlantic to the Mediterranean, forming a natural barrier of more than 400 kilometers in length that separates Spain from France and Andorra. This mountain range, which reaches its highest point at Aneto (3,404 meters), is one of the most imposing and spectacular geographical features of the Iberian Peninsula, and its influence on the climate, fauna, flora, and culture of the regions it traverses is profound and lasting. The Pyrenees were formed during the Tertiary era, approximately 30 million years ago, as a result of the collision between the European and Iberian tectonic plates, a geological process that raised the marine sediments and created the peaks, valleys, glaciers, and lakes that characterize this mountain range. The Pyrenees are divided into three sectors: the Western Pyrenees (from the Atlantic to Sobrarbe), the Central Pyrenees (from Sobrarbe to Montseny), and the Eastern Pyrenees (from Montseny to the Mediterranean), each with its own geological, climatic, and biogeographical characteristics that make them unique and special. The Western Pyrenees, with gentler peaks and wider valleys, are home to beech, oak, and pine forests, as well as alpine meadows where flocks of sheep and cattle graze. The Central Pyrenees, with higher and more rugged peaks, are home to glaciers, mountain lakes, and rocky summits that challenge mountaineers and hikers. The Eastern Pyrenees, with a drier and milder climate, are home to holm oak, cork oak, and Scots pine forests, as well as Mediterranean beaches and coves.

### The Pyrenean Valleys: Refuges of Life and Culture

The Pyrenean valleys are privileged spaces where nature, history, and culture intertwine to create landscapes of extraordinary beauty and richness. These valleys, formed by the erosion of rivers and glaciers over millions of years, are home to towns, villages, and hamlets that have survived for centuries by adapting to the climatic and geographical conditions of the mountains. The most important Pyrenean valleys include the Aran Valley (on the northern slope, in the province of Lleida), the Hecho Valley (on the southern slope, in the province of Huesca), the Bujaruelo Valley (on the southern slope, in the province of Huesca), and the Nuria Valley (on the southern slope, in the province of Girona). These valleys are home to extraordinary biodiversity, with animal species such as the brown bear, the Iberian wolf, the golden eagle, the bearded vulture, the chamois, the ibex, and the marmot, as well as a variety of alpine plants such as the edelweiss, the snow flower, the rhododendron, and the gentian. The Pyrenean valleys are also a refuge for cultural traditions that have remained alive for centuries: transhumant livestock farming, mountain agriculture, textile and wood craftsmanship, traditional music and dances, and the popular stone architecture with slate roofs that characterizes the Pyrenean villages. The Pyrenean valleys are not just landscapes; they are living ecosystems where nature and culture coexist in harmony, creating a natural and cultural heritage of incalculable value that deserves to be protected and preserved for future generations.

### The Pyrenean Glaciers: Witnesses of the Past

The Pyrenean glaciers are one of the most impressive testimonies of the geological history of the Pyrenees, and their study has allowed scientists to reconstruct the climate and landscape of this mountain range over the past millions of years. The Pyrenean glaciers were formed during the Quaternary glaciations, when global temperatures dropped several degrees and snow accumulated on the peaks and valleys of the Pyrenees, forming masses of ice that slowly descended toward the valleys, eroding the terrain and creating landscapes of spectacular beauty. Currently, the Pyrenean glaciers are scarce and retreating due to global warming, and only a few remnants of ice remain on the highest peaks (such as Aneto, Posets, and Monte Perdido), which constitute the last vestiges of an era when the Pyrenees were covered in ice. The study of the Pyrenean glaciers has allowed glaciologists to reconstruct the paleoclimate of the region, determine sea level fluctuations during glaciations, and understand the glacial erosion processes that have shaped the Pyrenean landscape. The Pyrenean glaciers are also important as a tourist and sports resource, as they attract skiers, mountaineers, and hikers who seek to enjoy the glacial landscapes and the winter activities offered by the ski resorts of the Pyrenees. However, the retreat of glaciers due to climate change poses significant threats to tourism, freshwater supply, and alpine ecosystem biodiversity, and requires urgent mitigation and adaptation measures to preserve this natural heritage for future generations.

## Chapter 2: The Central System

### The Central System: The Backbone of the Meseta

The Central System is a mountain range that extends from east to west across the central Spanish meseta, forming a natural barrier that separates the northern sub-meseta from the southern sub-meseta and that has profoundly influenced the climate, hydrography, flora, fauna, and culture of the regions it traverses. This mountain range, which extends through the autonomous communities of Madrid, Castile and Leon, Castilla-La Mancha, Extremadura, and Portugal, reaches its highest point at Almanzor (2,592 meters), in the Sierra de Gredos, and is composed of granitic and gneiss rocks dating from the Precambrian and Paleozoic eras, indicating that the Central System is one of the oldest mountain formations in the Iberian Peninsula. The Central System is divided into several mountain ranges: the Sierra de Guadarrama (in the central area), the Sierra de Gredos (in the western area), the Sierra de Bejar (in the southwestern area), the Sierra de la Serrezuela (in the northwestern area), and the Sierra de San Pedro (in the southwestern area), each with its own geological, climatic, and biogeographical characteristics that make them unique and special. The Central System has profoundly influenced the climate of the central Spanish meseta, as it acts as an orographic barrier that retains Atlantic humidity on the northern slope (creating a more humid and temperate climate) and blocks the entry of moist air on the southern slope (creating a drier and more continental climate). This climatic influence has determined the distribution of vegetation, agricultural and livestock activity, and human settlement patterns in the regions traversed by the Central System.

### The Peaks of the Central System: Summits and Landscapes

The peaks of the Central System are summits that rise above 2,000 meters and constitute the highest points of this mountain range, offering landscapes of spectacular beauty and extraordinary biodiversity. Almanzor (2,592 meters), in the Sierra de Gredos, is the highest peak of the Central System and one of the highest in the Iberian Peninsula, and its ascent is a challenge for mountaineers and hikers seeking to conquer this imposing mountain. Peak Almanzor is composed of granite and gneiss, and its conical and rugged shape makes it a geographical landmark visible from great distances. Siete Picos (2,430 meters), in the Sierra de Guadarrama, is another emblematic peak of the Central System, and its ascent, which requires mountaineering experience and skill, offers extraordinary panoramic views of the central meseta, the Guadarrama valleys, and the lands of Madrid. Peak Penalara (2,428 meters), in the Sierra de Guadarrama, is the highest point of the Community of Madrid and a protected natural space that is home to glacial lakes, alpine meadows, and a variety of mountain fauna and flora. The peaks of the Central System are not just geographical features; they are also spaces for recreation, sports, and leisure that attract thousands of visitors each year, who seek to enjoy nature, the outdoors, and the mountain landscapes offered by these imposing summits.

## Chapter 3: The Picos de Europa

### The Picos de Europa: The Most Impressive Limestone Massif in Spain

The Picos de Europa are a mountain massif that extends through the autonomous communities of Asturias, Cantabria, and Castile and Leon, and constitute one of the most spectacular and impressive mountain landscapes in all of Europe. This massif, which reaches its highest point at Naranjo de Bulnes (2,648 meters), is composed of limestone rocks dating from the Mesozoic era, and its landscape is dominated by rugged peaks, vertical rock walls, glacial valleys, mountain lakes, and beech, fir, and pine forests that cover the slopes and valley floors. The Picos de Europa were formed during the Alpine orogeny, approximately 65 million years ago, when the collision between the European and Iberian tectonic plates raised the marine sediments and created the peaks and valleys that characterize this massif. The Picos de Europa are divided into three main massifs: the Urriellu Massif (or Picos de Europa proper), the Cornion Massif (or Western Massif), and the Andara Massif (or Eastern Massif), each with its own geological, topographical, and landscape characteristics that make them unique and special. The Picos de Europa are home to extraordinary biodiversity, with animal species such as the Cantabrian brown bear (one of the most important populations in Europe), the Cantabrian capercaillie, the chamois, the ibex, the Iberian lynx, and the golden eagle, as well as a variety of alpine and forest plants that cover the slopes and valley floors.

### The Glacial Lakes of the Picos de Europa

The glacial lakes of the Picos de Europa are freshwater bodies formed by the erosion of glaciers during the Quaternary glaciations, and they constitute one of the most beautiful and tranquil landscapes of this mountain massif. These lakes, located at altitudes above 1,500 meters, are surrounded by rugged peaks, rock walls, and alpine meadows that create an environment of dazzling beauty. The most important glacial lakes of the Picos de Europa include Lake Enol, Lake Ercina, Lake Covadonga, Lake de la Ercina, and Lake del Balsexon, each with its own geographical, biological, and landscape characteristics that make them unique and special. Lake Enol, located at an altitude of 1,070 meters in the municipality of Onis (Asturias), is one of the most visited lakes of the Picos de Europa, and its crystal-clear waters reflect the surrounding peaks, creating a postcard-like landscape that attracts thousands of photographers and hikers each year. Lake Ercina, located at an altitude of 1,100 meters next to Lake Enol, is a smaller but equally beautiful lake, and its waters are home to a variety of fish, amphibians, and invertebrates that contribute to the biodiversity of the massif. The glacial lakes of the Picos de Europa are not just landscapes; they are also fragile ecosystems that require protection and conservation, as they are threatened by climate change, pollution, and tourist pressure affecting the alpine ecosystems of all of Europe.

## Chapter 4: The Basque Mountains

### The Basque Mountains: Landscape and Tradition

The Basque Mountains are a mountain chain that extends through the Basque Country, Navarre, and the north of Burgos, and constitute one of the most characteristic and important natural landscapes of northern Spain. This chain, which reaches its highest point at Gorbea (1,482 meters), is composed of sedimentary and volcanic rocks dating from the Mesozoic and Cenozoic eras, and its landscape is dominated by rounded peaks, green valleys, beech, oak, and holm oak forests, and alpine meadows where flocks of sheep and cattle graze. The Basque Mountains were formed during the Alpine orogeny, approximately 65 million years ago, when the collision between the European and Iberian tectonic plates raised the marine sediments and created the peaks and valleys that characterize this chain. The Basque Mountains are divided into several mountain ranges: the Sierra de Gorbea (between Alava and Vizcaya), the Sierra de Urkiola (in Vizcaya), the Sierra de Aralar (between Guipuzcoa and Navarre), the Sierra de Aizkorri (in Guipuzcoa), and the Sierra de Salbada (in Alava), each with its own geological, climatic, and biogeographical characteristics that make them unique and special. The Basque Mountains are home to extraordinary biodiversity, with animal species such as the brown bear, the wolf, the fox, the wild boar, the roe deer, the chamois, and the golden eagle, as well as a variety of alpine and forest plants that cover the slopes and valley floors.

### The Forests of the Basque Mountains

The forests of the Basque Mountains are one of the most important and fragile ecosystems of northern Spain's biodiversity, and their conservation is fundamental to maintaining the ecological health of the region. These forests, which cover the slopes and valley floors of the Basque Mountains, are composed primarily of beech (Fagus sylvatica), oaks (Quercus robur and Quercus pyrenaica), holm oaks (Quercus ilex), and Scots pines (Pinus sylvestris), trees that form dense and lush forests that are home to a variety of fauna and flora of extraordinary richness and diversity. Beech forests are the most widespread in the Basque Mountains, and their beech groves, some of which are centuries old, are spaces of spectacular beauty, especially in autumn, when the beech leaves take on golden, orange, and reddish hues that create a postcard-like landscape. Holm oak and cork oak forests are more typical of the lower and middle zones of the Basque Mountains, and their forests, which often mix with pine forests and other species, are home to a variety of fauna such as the wild boar, the roe deer, the fox, and a variety of forest birds. The forests of the Basque Mountains are also important as an economic resource, as they provide wood, resin, nuts, and other forest products that sustain the rural communities of the region. However, the forests of the Basque Mountains are threatened by deforestation, pollution, forest fires, and climate change, which threaten to alter ecological balances and reduce the biodiversity of these ecosystems fundamental to the health of the planet.

## Chapter 5: The Ebro

### The Ebro: The Most Water-Abundant River in Spain

The Ebro is the most water-abundant and longest river in Spain, and its hydrographic basin is one of the most important in the Iberian Peninsula, as it covers an area of more than 85,000 square kilometers that includes the autonomous communities of Aragon, Catalonia, Navarre, La Rioja, Castile and Leon, the Valencian Community, and the Basque Country. The Ebro originates at the Fontibre spring (Cantabria), at an altitude of 1,858 meters, and flows into the Mediterranean Sea, forming a delta of more than 300 square kilometers that is one of the most important wetlands in Europe. The Ebro flows 930 kilometers from its source to its mouth, traversing a variety of landscapes that include mountains, valleys, plains, canyons, and wetlands that reflect the geographical and climatic diversity of Spain. The average flow of the Ebro is approximately 300 cubic meters per second, a figure that makes it the most water-abundant river in Spain and one of the most important in the Iberian Peninsula. The Ebro has been fundamental to the human development of the regions it traverses, as it has provided water for irrigation, food, industry, and transportation, and has created fertile lands where agricultural products of extraordinary quality are cultivated, such as rice, cotton, nuts, olives, and wines. The Ebro has also profoundly influenced the culture, history, and traditions of the regions it traverses, as it has been a source of inspiration for artists, writers, musicians, and poets who have celebrated its beauty, strength, and generosity throughout the centuries.

### The Ebro Delta: A Wetland of International Importance

The Ebro Delta is one of the most important wetlands in Europe and the Mediterranean, and its ecological, environmental, and landscape value is of the highest order. This delta, which covers an area of more than 300 square kilometers in the province of Tarragona (Catalonia), is formed by the sediments that the Ebro has deposited over millions of years at its mouth, creating a landscape of alluvial plains, lagoons, marshes, channels, and rice paddies that are home to extraordinary biodiversity. The Ebro Delta is a breeding, wintering, and migratory stopover site for more than 300 bird species, among which are the greater flamingo, the little egret, the Eurasian bittern, the common coot, the northern shoveler, and a variety of aquatic and coastal birds that use the wetlands of the delta as refuge and food. The Ebro Delta is also home to a variety of fish, amphibian, reptile, mammal, and invertebrate species that contribute to the biological richness of the wetland, and that depend on water quality, food availability, and habitat integrity for their survival. The rice paddies of the Ebro Delta are one of the most characteristic and emblematic landscapes of the delta, and their cultivation, which dates back to the 15th century, has created a landscape of flooded fields that change color with the seasons, going from the intense green of spring to the golden hue of autumn. The Ebro Delta is threatened by climate change, sea level rise, coastal erosion, water pollution, and reduced river flow due to irrigation withdrawals, factors that threaten to alter ecological balances and reduce the biodiversity of this wetland fundamental to the health of the Mediterranean.

## Chapter 6: The Duero

### The Duero: The River of Kings

The Duero is the second longest and most water-abundant river in Spain, and its hydrographic basin is one of the most important in the Iberian Peninsula, as it covers an area of more than 97,000 square kilometers that includes the autonomous communities of Castile and Leon, Aragon, Extremadura, and Portugal. The Duero originates in the Picos de Urbion (Soria), at an altitude of 2,160 meters, and flows into the Atlantic in Oporto (Portugal), forming an estuary of more than 80 kilometers in length that is one of the most spectacular river landscapes in Europe. The Duero flows 897 kilometers from its source to its mouth, traversing a variety of landscapes that include mountains, valleys, plains, canyons, and reservoirs that reflect the geographical and climatic diversity of the Iberian Peninsula. The average flow of the Duero is approximately 220 cubic meters per second, a figure that makes it one of the most water-abundant rivers in Spain and a fundamental resource for the regions it traverses. The Duero has been fundamental to the human development of the regions it traverses, as it has provided water for irrigation, food, industry, and transportation, and has created fertile lands where agricultural products of extraordinary quality are cultivated, such as grapes (the Ribera del Duero Designation of Origin is one of the most prestigious in Spain), wheat, barley, and nuts. The Duero has also profoundly influenced the culture, history, and traditions of the regions it traverses, as it has been a source of inspiration for artists, writers, musicians, and poets who have celebrated its beauty, strength, and generosity throughout the centuries.

### The Arribes del Duero: The Canyon of Silence

The Arribes del Duero are one of the most spectacular and pristine river landscapes in Spain, and their designation as a Natural Park in 2008 has made it possible to preserve an ecosystem of incalculable value that combines landscape beauty with biological richness. These arribes, which extend along the border between Spain and Portugal for more than 100 kilometers along the course of the Duero, are composed of deep canyons, vertical rock walls, crystal-clear waters, and Mediterranean forests that are home to extraordinary biodiversity. The Arribes del Duero are home to a variety of animal species such as the Iberian lynx, the otter, the dipper, the osprey, the griffon vulture, and a variety of freshwater fish such as the calandino, the common trout, and the barbel, as well as a variety of Mediterranean plants such as the holm oak, the cork oak, the Aleppo pine, the rock rose, and the rosemary. The Arribes del Duero are also important as a tourist and sports resource, as they attract hikers, canoeists, birdwatchers, and nature lovers who seek to enjoy the landscapes, tranquility, and biodiversity of this protected natural space. The Arribes del Duero are an example of how nature and human activity can coexist in harmony, and their conservation is fundamental to maintaining the ecological health of the Duero basin and the Iberian Peninsula as a whole.

## Chapter 7: The Tagus

### The Tagus: The Longest River in the Iberian Peninsula

The Tagus is the longest river in the Iberian Peninsula, with a length of 1,007 kilometers from its source in the mountains of Albarracin (Teruel) to its mouth in the Atlantic in Lisbon (Portugal). The Tagus originates at an altitude of 1,580 meters in the mountains of the Iberian System, and its hydrographic basin covers an area of more than 80,000 square kilometers that includes the autonomous communities of Aragon, Castilla-La Mancha, Madrid, Extremadura, and Portugal. The Tagus traverses a variety of landscapes that include mountains, valleys, plains, canyons, and important cities such as Toledo, Aranjuez, Talavera de la Reina, and Lisbon, becoming a structural axis of the geography, economy, and culture of the regions it traverses. The average flow of the Tagus is approximately 160 cubic meters per second, a figure that makes it one of the most important rivers in the Iberian Peninsula, although its flow varies significantly depending on the season and rainfall in its basin. The Tagus has been fundamental to the human development of the regions it traverses, as it has provided water for irrigation, food, industry, and transportation, and has created fertile lands where agricultural products of extraordinary quality are cultivated, such as olives, grapes, wheat, and nuts. The Tagus has also profoundly influenced the culture, history, and traditions of the regions it traverses, as it has been a source of inspiration for artists, writers, musicians, and poets who have celebrated its beauty, strength, and generosity throughout the centuries.

### The Tagus at Toledo: Landscape and Legend

The Tagus at Toledo is one of the most emblematic and picturesque river landscapes in Spain, and its contemplation from the viewpoints of the city of Toledo has inspired artists, writers, and poets for centuries. The Tagus surrounds the city of Toledo on three sides, forming a deep meander that creates a landscape of rock walls, crystal-clear waters, and Mediterranean vegetation that contrasts with the monumental architecture of the city, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986. The Tagus at Toledo is not just a landscape; it is also a symbol of Toledo's identity, as the river has been a witness to the city's history for more than two thousand years, from the Roman era to the present day, through Visigothic, Muslim, Christian, and contemporary domination. The Tagus at Toledo is home to a variety of fauna and flora that includes fish such as the barbel, the bream, and the chub, birds such as the dipper, the kingfisher, and the grey heron, and plants such as the willow, the poplar, the elm, and the bramble, which cover the banks of the river creating a green corridor in the heart of the city. The Tagus at Toledo is also a space for recreation and leisure for the people of Toledo and visitors, as its banks are home to parks, gardens, promenades, and picnic areas that allow people to enjoy nature in the heart of the city. The Tagus at Toledo, therefore, is not just a river; it is a natural and cultural heritage that connects the past with the present and that continues to be the foundation of Toledo's identity and landscape.

## Chapter 8: The Guadalquivir

### The Guadalquivir: The River of Cities

The Guadalquivir is the second longest and most water-abundant river in Spain, and its hydrographic basin is one of the most important in the Iberian Peninsula, as it covers an area of more than 57,000 square kilometers that includes the autonomous communities of Andalusia, Extremadura, and Castilla-La Mancha. The Guadalquivir originates in the Sierra de Cazorla (Jaen), at an altitude of 1,410 meters, and flows into the Atlantic in Sanlucar de Barrameda (Cadiz), forming a marshland of more than 1,800 square kilometers that is one of the most important wetlands in Europe. The Guadalquivir flows 657 kilometers from its source to its mouth, traversing a variety of landscapes that include mountains, valleys, plains, marshlands, and important cities such as Cordoba, Seville, and Cadiz, becoming a structural axis of the geography, economy, and culture of Andalusia. The average flow of the Guadalquivir is approximately 170 cubic meters per second, a figure that makes it one of the most important rivers in Spain, although its flow varies significantly depending on the season and rainfall in its basin. The Guadalquivir has been fundamental to the human development of the regions it traverses, as it has provided water for irrigation, food, industry, and transportation, and has created fertile lands where agricultural products of extraordinary quality are cultivated, such as olives (Jaen is the world's largest producer of olive oil), grapes (the Jerez and Montilla-Moriles Designations of Origin are among the most prestigious in Spain), cotton, rice, and citrus fruits. The Guadalquivir has also profoundly influenced the culture, history, and traditions of Andalusia, as it has been a source of inspiration for artists, writers, musicians, and poets who have celebrated its beauty, strength, and generosity throughout the centuries.

### The Marshlands of the Guadalquivir: The Largest Wetland in Spain

The Marshlands of the Guadalquivir are the largest wetland in Spain and one of the most important in Europe, and their ecological, environmental, and landscape value is of the highest order. These marshlands, which cover an area of more than 1,800 square kilometers at the mouth of the Guadalquivir, between the provinces of Cadiz, Huelva, and Seville, are composed of lowlands, lagoons, channels, rice paddies, and flooded pastures that are home to extraordinary biodiversity. The Marshlands of the Guadalquivir are a breeding, wintering, and migratory stopover site for more than 300 bird species, among which are the greater flamingo, the black-winged stilt, the avocet, the marbled teal, the common coot, the Eurasian curlew, and a variety of aquatic and coastal birds that use the wetlands of the marshlands as refuge and food. The Marshlands of the Guadalquivir are also home to a variety of fish, amphibian, reptile, mammal, and invertebrate species that contribute to the biological richness of the wetland, and that depend on water quality, food availability, and habitat integrity for their survival. The marshlands of the Guadalquivir are threatened by climate change, sea level rise, coastal erosion, water pollution, and water withdrawals for irrigation, factors that threaten to alter ecological balances and reduce the biodiversity of this wetland fundamental to the health of the Atlantic and the Mediterranean.

## Chapter 9: The Atlantic Coasts

### The Atlantic Coasts: Landscapes of Sea and Land

The Atlantic coasts of Spain extend from the border with France to the border with Portugal, and constitute one of the most varied, beautiful, and productive coastlines in Europe. This coast, which covers the autonomous communities of Galicia, Asturias, Cantabria, the Basque Country, and part of Castile and Leon, is characterized by its rocky cliffs, golden sandy beaches, deep estuaries, rich fisheries, and landscapes of spectacular beauty that have inspired artists, writers, and poets for centuries. The Atlantic coasts of Spain were formed over millions of years of marine, tectonic, and glacial erosion, which shaped the coastline creating a variety of landscapes that include vertical cliffs, protected coves, estuaries, rias, bays, and beaches that reflect the geological and climatic diversity of the region. The Atlantic coasts of Spain are home to extraordinary biodiversity, with marine species such as cod, sea bream, hake, tuna, sardine, octopus, oysters, mussels, barnacles, langoustines, and lobsters, as well as a variety of seabirds such as the gannet, the cormorant, the seagull, the puffin, and the shearwater that nest on the cliffs and coastal islands. The Atlantic coasts of Spain are also important as a tourist and sports resource, as they attract millions of visitors each year who seek to enjoy the beaches, surfing, fishing, birdwatching, and coastal landscapes offered by these privileged regions of northern Spain.

### The Galician Rias: Landscape and Tradition

The Galician rias are one of the most emblematic and productive coastal landscapes in Spain, and their ecological, economic, and cultural importance is of the highest order. These rias, which extend along the coast of Galicia for more than 1,500 kilometers, are formed by sea inlets that invade river valleys creating deep and narrow fjords that are home to a variety of marine and coastal habitats of extraordinary richness and diversity. The most important Galician rias include the Ria de Vigo, the Ria de Arousa, the Ria de Muros and Noia, the Ria of Pontevedra, the Ria of Ares and Betanzos, and the Ria of Ferrol, each with its own geographical, biological, and economic characteristics that make them unique and special. The Galician rias are home to extraordinary biodiversity, with marine species such as the cockle, the clam, the mussel, the scallop, the barnacle, the langoustine, the octopus, the lobster, and a variety of fish that use the rias as breeding, feeding, and refuge zones. The Galician rias are also important as an aquaculture resource, as they are home to mussel, oyster, scallop, and cockle farms that produce shellfish of extraordinary quality that is exported worldwide. The Galician rias are also important as a tourist and sports resource, as they attract millions of visitors each year who seek to enjoy the beaches, fishing, sailing, birdwatching, and coastal landscapes offered by these privileged regions of northwestern Spain.

## Chapter 10: The Mediterranean Coasts

### The Spanish Mediterranean: Sun, Sea, and Culture

The Mediterranean coasts of Spain extend from the border with France to the border with Gibraltar, and constitute one of the most visited, developed, and touristy coastlines in the world. This coast, which covers the autonomous communities of Catalonia, the Valencian Community, the Region of Murcia, Andalusia (Mediterranean coast), and the Balearic Islands, is characterized by its golden and white sandy beaches, rocky cliffs, protected coves, marinas, coastal cities, and landscapes of beauty and diversity that attract millions of visitors each year. The Mediterranean coasts of Spain were formed over millions of years of marine, tectonic, and climatic erosion, which shaped the coastline creating a variety of landscapes that include extensive beaches, rugged coves, vertical cliffs, coastal islands, and protected bays that reflect the geological and climatic diversity of the region. The Mediterranean coasts of Spain are home to extraordinary biodiversity, with marine species such as the sea bream, the bass, the dentex, the bluefin tuna, the squid, the octopus, the cuttlefish, the prawns, the langoustines, the mussels, the oysters, and a variety of fish, crustaceans, and mollusks that use the Mediterranean coasts as breeding, feeding, and refuge zones. The Mediterranean coasts of Spain are also important as a tourist and sports resource, as they attract more than 40 million foreign visitors each year who seek to enjoy the sun, the beach, gastronomy, culture, and leisure offered by these privileged regions of eastern Spain.

### The Costa Brava: Landscape and Art

The Costa Brava is one of the most beautiful, picturesque, and artistic coasts in Spain, and its landscape of rocky cliffs, protected coves, sandy beaches, and fishing villages has inspired artists, writers, and poets for centuries. This coast, which extends through the province of Girona (Catalonia) for more than 200 kilometers from the French border to the city of Blanes, is characterized by its landscapes of spectacular beauty that combine the force of the Mediterranean with the softness of the light and colors that have fascinated artists such as Salvador Dali, Pablo Picasso, Joan Miro, and other masters of contemporary painting. The Costa Brava is home to a variety of landscapes that include rocky cliffs (such as those of Cap de Creus, declared a Natural Park), protected coves (such as those of Aiguablava, Sa Tuna, and Tamariu), extensive beaches (such as those of Lloret de Mar, Blanes, and Tossa de Mar) and fishing villages (such as Cadaques, Roses, and Palamos) that reflect the history, culture, and maritime traditions of the region. The Costa Brava is also important as a cultural and tourist destination, as it is home to museums, art galleries, theaters, and cultural festivals that attract visitors from around the world who seek to enjoy the natural beauty, gastronomy, wine (the Emporda and DO Alella Designations of Origin are among the most prestigious in Catalonia), and art that characterize this privileged Mediterranean coast.

## Chapter 11: The Canary Islands

### The Canary Islands: The Archipelago of Eternity

The Canary Islands are a volcanic archipelago located in the Atlantic Ocean, approximately 100 kilometers from the coast of Africa, and constitute one of the most special, diverse, and fascinating autonomous communities of Spain. This archipelago, formed by seven main islands (Tenerife, Gran Canaria, Lanzarote, Fuerteventura, La Palma, La Gomera, and El Hierro) and several minor islands, was formed over millions of years of volcanic activity that created islands with a variety of landscapes, climates, and ecosystems that make them a natural laboratory of extraordinary scientific importance. The Canary Islands are characterized by their spectacular volcanic landscapes (such as Teide on Tenerife, the highest volcano in Spain at 3,718 meters), their black and golden sandy beaches, their laurisilva forests (a type of subtropical forest found only in the Canaries and a few other regions in the world), their deep ravines, their rocky cliffs, and their arid landscapes that reflect the climatic and geological diversity of the archipelago. The Canary Islands are home to extraordinary biodiversity, with endemic animal and plant species found only in the Canaries, such as the dragon tree, the Canary Island palm, the Canary Island pine, the Canary Island lily, the tagasaste, the Canarian lizard, the Egyptian vulture, the Houbara bustard, and a variety of invertebrate species that contribute to the biological richness of the archipelago. The Canary Islands are also important as a tourist and economic resource, as they attract more than 12 million visitors each year who seek to enjoy the sun, the beach, gastronomy, culture, and the spectacular landscapes offered by these privileged islands of the Atlantic.

## Chapter 14: The DANA

### The DANA: The Most Destructive Meteorological Phenomenon

The DANA (Depresion Aislada en Niveles Altos / Isolated Depression at High Altitudes) is the most destructive and dangerous meteorological phenomenon that affects the Iberian Peninsula, and its occurrence, increasingly frequent due to climate change, poses significant threats to the population, infrastructure, and environment of the regions it affects. The DANA occurs when a mass of cold air breaks away from the main jet stream and moves southward, creating an atmospheric depression that stagnates over a region and causes torrential rains, severe storms, hail, and hurricane-force winds that can cause floods, landslides, material damage, and loss of life. The DANA is more frequent in the western Mediterranean, where the combination of cold air aloft and warm sea water creates the ideal conditions for the formation of vertically developed clouds that produce intense and prolonged rainfall. The most destructive DANAs in recent history include the DANA of 1997, which caused floods in Alicante and Murcia with 87 deaths; the DANA of 2019, which caused floods in the Valencian Community with more than 70 deaths; and the DANA of 2024, which caused catastrophic floods in Valencia, Murcia, and Andalusia with more than 200 deaths, becoming the worst natural disaster in Spain's recent history. The DANA is a phenomenon that highlights the vulnerability of coastal and urban areas to extreme weather events, and underscores the need to improve early warning systems, urban planning, risk management, and climate change adaptation to minimize the impact of these phenomena in the future.
