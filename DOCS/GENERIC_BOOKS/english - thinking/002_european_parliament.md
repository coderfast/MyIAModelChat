The European Parliament is the parliamentary assembly of the European Union and one of the most important institutions of the integration project. It is currently composed of 720 MEPs elected by universal direct suffrage by the citizens of the 27 Member States. The distribution of seats follows the principle of proportional representation with descent, but ensures that all Member States have a minimum number of representatives. The most populous countries, such as Germany, have the largest number of MEPs (96), while the smallest countries, such as Malta, Luxembourg and Cyprus, have 6 seats each. This distribution seeks to balance democratic representation with the principle of sovereign equality of the Member States.

MEPs are elected for a five-year term, and European elections are held every five years in all Member States simultaneously, generally in the month of June. European citizens can stand as candidates in any Member State, although in practice most candidates run in their country of origin. MEPs do not represent the interests of their countries, but the interests of European citizens as a whole, which distinguishes them from representatives in national parliaments. This immunity may be lifted by Parliament itself at the request of the national authorities.

The elections to the European Parliament are the largest transnational elections in the world, in which more than 400 million eligible citizens from the 27 Member States participate. Since 1979, when the first direct elections were held, European citizens have elected their representatives in Parliament by universal, direct and secret suffrage. Universal direct suffrage is a fundamental principle of European democracy, as it gives the European Parliament a direct democratic legitimacy, unlike the other European institutions, which are appointed by national governments. European elections are an important moment in the democratic life of the EU, as they allow citizens to express their political preferences and influence the orientation of European policy.

The ordinary legislative procedure is the most widely used procedure in the European Union, and establishes that legislative proposals must be jointly approved by the European Parliament and the Council of the EU. This procedure, introduced by the Treaty of Amsterdam in 1997 and reinforced by the Treaty of Lisbon in 2009, gives Parliament an equal role with the Council in the decision-making process. The European Commission presents a legislative proposal, which is examined by Parliament and the Council, which may propose amendments and negotiate a common text. If Parliament and the Council fail to agree on a common text, the proposal may be referred to a conciliation procedure, which seeks to reach a text acceptable to both institutions.

The ordinary legislative procedure reflects the principle of codecision, which is a central element of the EU's institutional system. Codecision means that Parliament and the Council have equal powers in the legislative process, which gives Parliament a democratic legitimacy it did not have in the early years of integration. The Treaty of Lisbon broadened the scope of the ordinary legislative procedure, extending it to new policy areas, such as the common agricultural policy, transport policy and part of justice and home affairs. This extension reflects the recognition that Parliament, as the body directly elected by citizens, must play an important role in all policy areas that affect European citizens.

The ordinary legislative procedure begins with the submission of a proposal by the European Commission. The proposal is first examined by the competent parliamentary committee, which prepares a report with amendments and a recommendation for the Parliament plenary. The plenary debates and votes on the committee report, and if it is approved, Parliament establishes its negotiating position. This position is communicated to the Council of the EU, which examines the proposal and, if necessary, proposes amendments. If the Council and Parliament do not agree, a conciliation procedure is initiated, in which a conciliation committee composed of representatives of both institutions seeks a common text. If an agreement is reached, the text is returned to Parliament and the Council for final approval.
