# Climate and Weather

## Chapter 1: Describing the Weather

### Basic concepts of atmospheric weather

Atmospheric weather refers to the state of the Earth's atmosphere at a given time and place, describing the conditions we experience in our daily environment. To describe the weather, it is necessary to consider multiple atmospheric variables that interact with each other and determine the conditions that prevail at any given moment. Temperature is one of the most important variables, as it indicates the degree of heating or cooling of the air and is measured in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit. Atmospheric pressure, which is the weight of the air column over a given point, directly influences cloud formation, precipitation, and weather changes. Relative humidity represents the amount of water vapor present in the air relative to the maximum capacity it can hold at that temperature, and is expressed as a percentage. Wind speed and direction determine the movement of air in the atmosphere, transporting air masses with different characteristics and contributing to the formation of meteorological phenomena. Cloudiness describes the quantity and type of clouds present in the sky, and precipitation indicates the fall of water in the form of rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Visibility determines the distance at which objects can be seen clearly, and is influenced by the presence of fog, rain, dust, or pollution. All these elements combine to create the complete picture of atmospheric weather at a given time and place.

### The language for describing the weather

Meteorological language has a rich and precise vocabulary for describing different atmospheric weather conditions. To describe temperature, terms such as frosty, cool, mild, warm, hot, or sweltering are used, indicating temperature ranges perceived by humans. Atmospheric pressure is described with terms such as high pressure, low pressure, anticyclone, or storm, which indicate the predominant meteorological systems. Wind is characterized by its speed (calm, breeze, strong wind, gale) and its direction (north wind, west wind, mistral, sirocco). Clouds are classified into different types according to their shape, altitude, and composition: cirrus, stratus, cumulus, nimbostratus, cumulonimbus, among others. Precipitation is described with terms such as rain, downpour, storm, drizzle, snowfall, hailstorm, or fog. To describe the general state of the sky, expressions such as clear sky, partly cloudy, cloudy, overcast, or stormy are used. Meteorological language also includes terms for special phenomena such as rainbows, dense fog, frost, fog banks, or thermal inversion. The precise use of this vocabulary allows for clear and detailed communication of weather conditions, facilitating decision-making in activities that depend on meteorology, such as agriculture, transportation, construction, or outdoor recreation.

### Human perception of weather

Human perception of atmospheric weather varies significantly depending on personal, cultural, and environmental factors. Each person perceives the climate differently, as the sensation of heat or cold depends not only on the actual temperature, but also on humidity, wind, solar radiation, and one's own physical constitution. For example, the same temperature of twenty degrees can feel pleasant in spring but cool in autumn, due to the body's acclimatization to previous conditions. Relative humidity significantly influences the perception of heat: with high humidity, the body has difficulty regulating its temperature through perspiration, which makes the heat feel more intense. Wind, in turn, intensifies the sensation of cold in winter and can be refreshing in summer. Cultural differences also affect weather perception: what is considered a cool day for a person from a tropical country may seem very cold to someone accustomed to warm climates. Weather perception is also influenced by psychological factors such as mood, expectations, and previous experiences. People who enjoy the outdoors may perceive a rainy day as unpleasant, while others may find it comforting. Modern meteorology seeks not only to provide objective data about atmospheric conditions, but also to provide relevant information for citizens' daily lives, interpreting data in understandable and useful terms.

### Tools for observing the weather

The observation of atmospheric weather is carried out through a combination of meteorological instruments, observation networks, and remote sensing technologies. At weather stations, thermometers are used to measure air temperature, barometers to record atmospheric pressure, hygrometers to determine relative humidity, anemometers to measure wind speed, and wind vanes to indicate wind direction. Pluviometers measure the amount of precipitation that has fallen, and sunshine recorders log the hours of sunshine received. Automatic weather stations, distributed throughout the territory, collect data continuously and transmit it to processing centers in real time. Meteorological satellites orbit the Earth and capture images of the atmosphere, clouds, and water bodies, providing a global view of the weather that allows tracking of moving meteorological systems. Meteorological radars detect the presence and movement of precipitation, providing real-time information about the location, intensity, and trajectory of rainfall. Radiosondes, launched with weather balloons, measure atmospheric conditions at different altitudes, providing vertical profiles of temperature, humidity, and wind. Numerical weather prediction models, based on physical equations and computational algorithms, process observation data to generate weather forecasts at different temporal and spatial scales. The combination of all these tools allows meteorologists to obtain a complete and detailed view of the state of the atmosphere, improving the accuracy of forecasts and the ability to alert against adverse meteorological phenomena.

## Chapter 2: Weather Forecasts

### Principles of weather forecasting

Weather forecasting is the science and technique that allows anticipating the future state of the Earth's atmosphere, providing information about expected weather conditions in a given location and time period. The principles of weather forecasting are based on the study of the physical processes that govern the atmosphere, including heat transfer, fluid dynamics, thermodynamics, and cloud physics. Modern meteorology relies on numerical weather prediction models, which are computer programs that solve mathematical equations describing the evolution of the atmosphere based on initial and boundary conditions. These models process enormous amounts of data from weather stations, satellites, radars, aircraft, ships, and other observation devices, and generate weather predictions at different scales: from hourly forecasts to seasonal predictions. The accuracy of weather forecasts has improved dramatically in recent decades thanks to advances in computational capacity, improved numerical models, expanded observation networks, and the incorporation of new data sources. However, the atmosphere is a chaotic system, which imposes fundamental limits on weather predictability, especially at medium and long range. The study of atmospheric chaos, initially developed by Edward Lorenz in the 1960s, demonstrates that small uncertainties in initial conditions can grow exponentially, limiting forecast accuracy beyond ten or fifteen days.

### Types of weather forecasts

Weather forecasts are classified according to their temporal scale, level of detail, and intended use. Short-range forecasting, also known as nowcasting, covers the period of the next six to twelve hours and is based mainly on direct observation and weather radars, being especially useful for alerting against severe phenomena such as storms, hailstorms, or wind gusts. Synoptic-scale forecasting covers a period of one to three days and is based on numerical weather prediction models, providing information about expected temperature, precipitation, wind, and cloudiness. Medium-range forecasting covers three to ten days and offers a general view of weather evolution, with less precision but valuable information for planning activities. Long-range forecasting, or seasonal prediction, attempts to anticipate climatic trends over periods of weeks to months, being useful for sectors such as agriculture, energy, and water management. In addition to these general categories, there are specialized forecasts for specific sectors: agricultural forecasts, air quality forecasts, wave forecasts, wind forecasts for wind energy, wildfire risk forecasts, and pollination forecasts. Forecasts are communicated through multiple channels: television, radio, internet, mobile applications, SMS alert services, and information panels in public spaces. The quality and usefulness of a weather forecast depend not only on its accuracy, but also on its clarity, specificity, and relevance to the user.

### The accuracy of weather forecasts

The accuracy of weather forecasts has experienced notable improvement in recent decades, but remains a complex challenge due to the chaotic nature of the atmosphere. Studies carried out by international meteorological agencies show that three-day forecast accuracy exceeds eighty percent in terms of temperature and precipitation, representing a significant advance compared to twenty years ago. At five days, accuracy is around seventy percent, and at ten days it drops to approximately fifty percent. These figures may vary depending on the geographical region, the time of year, and the type of meteorological phenomenon. The main factors affecting forecast accuracy include the quality and quantity of observation data, the resolution of numerical models, the representation of small-scale physical processes, and terrain complexity. Areas with dense observation networks, such as Europe and North America, tend to have more accurate forecasts than regions with less data coverage. The prediction of local phenomena, such as convective storms or fog, is especially difficult due to their reduced scale and high variability. To improve forecast accuracy, meteorological agencies invest in improving observation networks, developing more detailed models, incorporating data from unconventional sources, and applying data analysis techniques based on artificial intelligence. The effective communication of uncertainty inherent in forecasts is also an important aspect, as users must understand the limits of weather prediction to make informed decisions.

### Weather services in Spain

In Spain, the national weather service is managed by the State Meteorological Agency (AEMET), a public body attached to the Ministry for the Ecological Transition and the Demographic Challenge. AEMET is responsible for meteorological observation, weather prediction, weather monitoring, climatology, and meteorological research throughout the Spanish territory. The agency has an extensive network of automatic weather stations, precipitation radars, satellites, and observation systems that cover the entire country, including the island territories and the autonomous cities of Ceuta and Melilla. AEMET's weather forecasting services include weather warnings for adverse phenomena, detailed regional and local forecasts, and specialized forecasts for sectors such as aviation, agriculture, energy, and tourism. Weather warnings are classified by severity levels: green (no risk), yellow (local risk), orange (significant risk), and red (extreme risk), and are disseminated through multiple channels to ensure they reach the affected population. In addition to AEMET, there are other organizations that contribute to weather services in Spain, such as the meteorological agencies of the autonomous communities, universities, and research centers. International cooperation is a fundamental aspect of Spanish meteorology, as weather does not respect borders and forecasting requires a global view of the atmosphere. Spain actively participates in international meteorological organizations such as the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF).

## Chapter 3: The Seasons of the Year

### Spring: rebirth and hope

Spring is one of the four seasons of the year, situated between winter and summer, and is characterized by the return of warmth, the blooming of vegetation, and the increase in daylight hours. In the Northern Hemisphere, spring officially begins on March 21 with the spring equinox, when the duration of day and night are equal, and extends until June 21, the date of the summer solstice. During spring, the average temperature experiences a progressive increase, precipitation is frequent but generally moderate, and days become increasingly longer. Vegetation experiences a period of intensive growth, with the flowering of trees, plants, and wildflowers that transform landscapes with vibrant colors. Migratory birds return to their breeding areas, animals emerge from their winter lethargy, and the natural world comes alive with special activity. In Spain, spring is a particularly pleasant season, with mild temperatures across most of the territory, although rainfall can be frequent in the north and late frosts affect some mountainous regions. Spring is culturally associated with hope, rebirth, and renewal, and in many cultures festivities and traditions linked to this season are celebrated, such as Holy Week, the San Isidro festivals in Madrid, or the April fairs in Seville.

### Summer: heat and activity

Summer is the warmest season of the year, situated between spring and autumn, and is characterized by high temperatures, long days, and short nights. In the Northern Hemisphere, summer officially begins on June 21 with the summer solstice, the longest day of the year, and extends until September 21, the date of the autumn equinox. During summer, the average temperature reaches its annual maximum values, precipitation is scarce in most regions, and solar radiation is intense and prolonged. In Spain, summer is characterized by very high temperatures in the interior and the Mediterranean basin, with values that can exceed forty degrees in the Guadalquivir valley, La Mancha, and the Ebro valley. The Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts offer more moderate temperatures thanks to the influence of the sea, becoming popular tourist destinations during this season. Summer is the vacation season for many citizens, who take advantage of the good weather to travel, enjoy the beach, practice outdoor sports, and engage in recreational activities. However, summer also presents meteorological risks such as convective storms, wildfires, heat waves, and air pollution. Agriculture faces the challenge of drought and the need for intensive irrigation to maintain crops. Summer is a season of intense activity both in human and natural terms, with long daylight hours that allow enjoyment of the outdoors and the many activities that this season offers.

### Autumn: changes and transition

Autumn is the transitional season between summer and winter, and is characterized by the progressive reduction of daylight hours, the decrease in temperatures, and the transformation of vegetation color. In the Northern Hemisphere, autumn officially begins on September 21 with the autumn equinox and extends until December 21, the date of the winter solstice. During autumn, temperatures gradually decline, precipitation becomes more frequent and intense, especially in the north and west of the Peninsula, and days progressively shorten while nights lengthen. The color of deciduous tree leaves progressively changes from green to yellowish, orange, and reddish tones, creating landscapes of great beauty that attract tourists and photographers. Migratory birds begin their journey south to escape the winter cold, and animals prepare their food reserves for the period of scarcity. In Spain, autumn is a season of great climatic diversity, with mild temperatures in the south and east, frequent rainfall in the north and west, and the first frosts in mountainous areas. Autumn is culturally associated with the harvest season, festivities such as All Saints' Day and Halloween, and the start of the school year and work activity after the summer holidays. Autumn landscapes offer an ideal opportunity for hiking, enjoying seasonal gastronomy, and admiring the transformation of nature.

### Winter: cold and rest

Winter is the coldest season of the year, situated between autumn and spring, and is characterized by low temperatures, short days, and long nights. In the Northern Hemisphere, winter officially begins on December 21 with the winter solstice, the shortest day of the year, and extends until March 21, the date of the spring equinox. During winter, average temperatures are the lowest of the year, precipitation can occur as snow in mountainous areas, and solar radiation is weak and of short duration. In Spain, winter presents a wide range of climatic conditions depending on the region: the Mediterranean coasts enjoy mild winters, with temperatures above ten degrees, while interior and mountainous areas experience frequent frosts and abundant snowfall. The Pyrenees, the Cantabrian Mountains, the Basque Mountains, the Central System, and Sierra Nevada receive significant snow accumulation during winter, becoming ski resorts and snow tourism destinations. Winter precipitation is especially abundant on the Cantabrian coast, which receives the most rainfall of the entire Peninsula during this season. Winter is culturally associated with the celebration of festivities such as Christmas and New Year, which bring families and communities together around traditions, meals, and celebrations. Despite the cold, winter offers activities such as skiing, winter hiking, bird watching, and enjoyment of snow-covered landscapes of great beauty. Agriculture enters a period of rest, fields remain fallow, and fruit trees lose their leaves, preparing for the new growth cycle that will begin with spring.

## Chapter 4: Meteorological Phenomena

### Storms and severe phenomena

Storms are meteorological phenomena characterized by the presence of cumulonimbus clouds, rain, lightning, thunder, and sometimes hail or wind gusts. Storms form when warm, moist air rises rapidly in the atmosphere, creating columns of clouds that can reach heights of several kilometers. The energy released during the formation of a storm is enormous, equivalent to that of multiple atomic bombs, and manifests in the form of electrical discharges (lightning), intense sounds (thunder), and abundant precipitation. Storms can be local or affect wide areas, and their duration varies from a few minutes to several hours. Severe phenomena associated with storms include wind gusts, which can exceed one hundred kilometers per hour and cause damage to trees, buildings, and infrastructure; hailstorms, with ice stones that can reach the size of a golf ball and cause significant damage to crops and vehicles; tornadoes, which are rotating columns of air that form within storms and can cause devastating destruction; and flash floods, caused by intense rainfall over short periods of time. Storms are especially frequent in the interior regions of the Peninsula during summer, when ground heating favors convection, and on the Mediterranean coast during autumn, when the interaction between cold air aloft and sea water creates favorable conditions for the formation of intense storms.

### Mist and fog

Fog is a meteorological phenomenon that occurs when the humidity in the air condenses, forming small water droplets suspended near the Earth's surface, reducing visibility to less than one kilometer. Mist is similar to fog but with visibility greater than one kilometer, although the boundary between both concepts is flexible and varies according to the criteria of meteorological agencies. Fog formation occurs when air cools to reach the dew point, which can happen through several mechanisms: nocturnal cooling of the ground (radiation fog), the passage of moist air over cold surfaces (advection fog), the evaporation of water in cold air (evaporation fog), or the lifting of moist air in mountainous areas (orographic fog). In Spain, fog is especially frequent in interior valleys, river basins, and coastal areas during autumn and winter, when calm conditions and clear skies favor nocturnal ground cooling. Dense fog can cause road safety problems, delays in air and ground transportation, and difficulties in agricultural activities. Fog also has positive effects, such as providing moisture to the soil, which benefits crops, and creating landscapes of great artistic beauty. Fog prediction is especially difficult due to its reduced scale and high spatial variability, which requires detailed local observations and high-resolution models.

### Droughts and floods

Droughts and floods are two opposing meteorological phenomena that can cause significant damage to both the environment and human populations. Drought is defined as a prolonged period of below-normal precipitation, which causes a reduction in water availability in soil, aquifers, reservoirs, and rivers. Droughts can be classified as meteorological (lack of rain), hydrological (reduction of river flow and reservoir levels), agricultural (soil moisture deficit for crops), and socioeconomic (impact on human activity). In Spain, droughts are a recurring phenomenon that has historically caused water supply problems, agricultural losses, and conflicts over water resource use. Floods, on the other hand, occur when water invades normally dry land, whether from intense rainfall, river overflow, dam failure, or rising sea levels. Floods are the meteorological phenomenon that causes the most deaths and material damage worldwide, and in Spain they especially affect Mediterranean river basins, where torrential rains can cause sudden and devastating flash floods. The management of drought and flood risk requires comprehensive hydrological planning, the construction of storage and defense infrastructure, continuous meteorological monitoring, and the implementation of early warning systems that allow populations to prepare for these phenomena.

### Extreme phenomena: heat waves and cold spells

Heat waves and episodes of intense cold are extreme meteorological phenomena that represent a significant risk to public health, agriculture, and infrastructure. A heat wave is defined as a prolonged period of abnormally high temperatures for the time of year and the affected geographical region. In Spain, heat waves are becoming more frequent and intense due to climate change, with temperatures that can exceed forty-five degrees in the Guadalquivir valley and La Mancha. These episodes cause an increase in mortality, especially among the elderly, the chronically ill, and children, as extreme heat can cause heat stroke, dehydration, and worsening of cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. Heat waves also affect agriculture, causing crop losses due to water and thermal stress, and infrastructure, causing deformation of roads and railway lines. Episodes of intense cold, on the other hand, are defined as periods with abnormally low temperatures, persistent frosts, and abundant snowfall. In Spain, cold waves are less frequent than heat waves, but can cause significant problems, such as transportation disruption due to snow and ice, damage to frost-sensitive crops, and increased energy demand for heating. Protection against these extreme phenomena requires early warning systems, emergency plans, social protection measures for vulnerable populations, and adaptation of infrastructure and crops to extreme climatic conditions.

## Chapter 5: The Climate in Spain

### Spanish climatic diversity

Spain is one of the most climatically diverse countries in Europe, thanks to its geographical location, varied relief, and the influence of multiple factors that shape its climate. The Spanish territory extends from the Atlantic coasts of the north to the Mediterranean coasts of the east and south, including the Balearic and Canary Islands, and encompasses a variety of landscapes ranging from the highest mountains of the Peninsula to the lowest coasts. This geographical diversity translates into a wide range of climates, from the oceanic climate of the north, with abundant precipitation and moderate temperatures year-round, to the semi-arid climate of the southeast, with scarce precipitation and high temperatures for most of the year. The Mediterranean climate, which affects most of the Peninsula and the Balearic Islands, is characterized by hot summers and mild winters, with precipitation concentrated in autumn and spring. The continental climate of the interior of the Peninsula presents marked thermal differences between summer and winter, with very high temperatures in July and August and frequent frosts in January and February. The Atlantic climate of the north and northwest is characterized by abundant precipitation distributed uniformly throughout the year, moderate temperatures, and high ambient humidity. The Canary Islands, located off the coast of Africa, enjoy a subtropical climate with stable temperatures year-round and scarce precipitation. This climatic diversity has important implications for agriculture, tourism, energy, and the quality of life of Spanish citizens.

### The Mediterranean climate

The Mediterranean climate is the one that affects most of the Spanish territory, including the Mediterranean coasts, the Ebro valley, the Guadalquivir basin, and the Balearic Islands. This type of climate is characterized by hot, dry summers, with temperatures that can exceed thirty-five degrees in interior areas, and mild, humid winters, with average temperatures above ten degrees on the coasts. Precipitation in the Mediterranean climate is concentrated mainly in autumn and spring, with prolonged drought periods during summer. The irregular distribution of precipitation throughout the year, together with high evapotranspiration during summer, creates a recurring water deficit that affects agriculture and water supply. The Mediterranean climate also presents great interannual variability, with very rainy years alternating with periods of severe drought. Characteristic meteorological phenomena of the Mediterranean climate include cold drops, which cause torrential rains and flash floods in autumn; summer storms, which mainly affect the interior of the Peninsula; and snowfall in mountainous areas during winter. The influence of the Mediterranean Sea moderates coastal temperatures, creating a thermal regulation effect that softens differences between summer and winter. The Mediterranean climate is ideal for growing olives, grapevines, citrus fruits, and vegetables, and supports one of the world's healthiest and most balanced diets, the Mediterranean diet, recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

### The oceanic climate of the north

The oceanic climate, also known as oceanic or Atlantic, affects the northern strip of the Peninsula, including Galicia, Asturias, Cantabria, the Basque Country, and parts of Aragon and Catalonia. This climate is characterized by abundant precipitation distributed uniformly throughout the year, with values that can exceed two thousand millimeters annually in mountainous areas near the coast. Temperatures are moderate year-round, with mild winters (average temperatures above eight degrees) and cool summers (average temperatures below twenty degrees). Ambient humidity is high and cloudiness frequent, which reduces sunshine hours compared to other Spanish regions. The oceanic landscape is characterized by green meadows, deciduous forests, misty mountains, and lush vegetation maintained by constant rainfall. Precipitation in the oceanic climate can occur as rain, snow (in mountainous areas), or sleet, and drought periods are scarce and short-lived. West and northwest winds predominate in this region, transporting humidity from the Atlantic and favoring cloud and precipitation formation. The oceanic climate significantly influences the lifestyle of the inhabitants of northern Spain, with architecture adapted to rainfall (sloped roofs, enclosed balconies), cuisine based on sea and garden products, and a culture linked to the natural and rainy environment. The climatic diversity of northern Spain, with milder coastal areas and more continental interior areas, creates a wide variety of landscapes and ecosystems that harbor rich biodiversity.

### The continental interior climate

The continental climate of the interior of the Spanish Peninsula is characterized by marked thermal differences between summer and winter, with hot summers and cold winters, and moderate precipitation distributed throughout the year. This climate mainly affects the regions of Castile and Leon, Castilla-La Mancha, Extremadura, Madrid, and parts of Aragon and Andalusia, where altitude and distance from the sea create more extreme conditions than in coastal areas. During summer, temperatures in the interior can exceed forty degrees for prolonged periods, with nights that do not drop below twenty degrees in urban areas. In winter, temperatures can drop below zero degrees for several consecutive days, with frequent nighttime frosts and occasional snowfall in higher altitude areas. Precipitation in the continental climate is moderate, generally between four hundred and six hundred millimeters annually, and is distributed more evenly than in the Mediterranean climate, although with a maximum in spring and autumn. Characteristic meteorological phenomena of the continental climate include dense fog in valleys during autumn and winter, late spring frosts that affect crops, and summer storms with localized and intense precipitation. The diurnal temperature range is significant, with differences of more than fifteen degrees between the maximum and minimum temperature on the same day, especially during spring and autumn. The continental landscape is characterized by extensive cultivated plains, holm cork oak and cork oak dehesas, limestone moors, and river valleys that support rain-fed agriculture based on cereals, olives, and grapevines.

## Chapter 6: Climate Change

### Climate change science

Climate change refers to the global variation in Earth's climate observed over a prolonged period of time, and is primarily attributed to human activity, especially the emission of greenhouse gases. Climate change science studies the physical, chemical, and biological processes that regulate the Earth's climate system, as well as the causes, consequences, and possible solutions to this phenomenon. The main human-origin greenhouse gas is carbon dioxide (CO2), which is emitted during the combustion of fossil fuels (oil, natural gas, coal) for energy production, transportation, and industry. Other important greenhouse gases include methane (CH4), emitted by livestock, rice paddies, and landfills; nitrous oxide (N2O), from agriculture and industrial processes; and fluorinated gases, used in industry and refrigeration. The greenhouse effect is a natural phenomenon that maintains the Earth's temperature at a level suitable for life, but the increased concentration of greenhouse gases due to human activity is intensifying this effect, causing global warming that is transforming climate patterns around the planet. Scientific data shows that the global average temperature has increased approximately one degree Celsius since the pre-industrial era, and projections indicate an additional increase of between one and four degrees by the end of the 21st century, depending on future emissions.

### Effects of climate change in Spain

Spain is one of the European countries most vulnerable to climate change due to its geographical location, Mediterranean climate, and exposure to multiple impacts. The effects of climate change in Spain include the increase in average temperature, which has already experienced an increase of approximately one and a half degrees since the 1960s, at a rate of warming higher than the global average. Heat waves are becoming more frequent and intense, with episodes affecting wide areas of the country over prolonged periods. Precipitation shows an overall downward trend, especially in the south and east of the Peninsula, with increased interannual irregularity and drought frequency. The sea level around Spanish coasts has experienced an increase of approximately three millimeters annually in recent decades, with implications for low-lying coastal areas and coastal ecosystems. The cryosphere, formed by glaciers and permanent snow in Spanish mountains, has experienced a significant reduction, with the disappearance of more than eighty percent of Pyrenean glaciers since the beginning of the 20th century. Natural ecosystems are experiencing changes in species distribution, flowering and fruiting periods, and species migration toward higher altitude or latitude areas. Agriculture faces challenges such as increased water stress, the arrival of new pests and diseases, and the need to adapt crops to new climatic conditions. Extreme meteorological risks, such as torrential rains, severe storms, and strong winds, are expected to increase in frequency and intensity, with implications for the safety of people and infrastructure.

### Mitigation and adaptation to climate change

The response to climate change is articulated in two complementary strategies: mitigation and adaptation. Mitigation seeks to reduce greenhouse gas emissions to limit global temperature increase and avoid the worst impacts of climate change. The main mitigation strategies include the energy transition to renewable sources (solar, wind, hydroelectric), improving energy efficiency in buildings, industry, and transportation, electrification of transportation, reducing meat consumption, and protecting and restoring forests and wetlands that act as carbon sinks. Spain has assumed ambitious emission reduction commitments within the framework of the Paris Agreement, with the objective of reducing emissions by twenty-three percent in 2030 compared to 1990 and achieving carbon neutrality by 2050. Adaptation, for its part, seeks to prepare society and ecosystems for the inevitable impacts of climate change that are already underway. Adaptation strategies in Spain include the creation of early warning systems for extreme meteorological phenomena, the implementation of water efficiency measures and wastewater reuse, the adaptation of crops to new climatic conditions, the protection of coastal areas against sea level rise, and urban planning to reduce the urban heat island effect. Education and citizen awareness are essential components of the fight against climate change, as changes in individual and collective habits are fundamental to reducing emissions and adapting to a changing climate.

## Chapter 7: Meteorological Risks

### Types of meteorological risks

Meteorological risks are those atmospheric phenomena that can cause significant damage to people, property, agriculture, and the environment. These risks are classified according to their nature, intensity, and potential impact, ranging from the most common phenomena to the rarest but most devastating extreme events. Among the most frequent meteorological risks are intense rainfall, which can cause flash floods and river floods; storms with hail, which cause damage to crops, vehicles, and buildings; wind gusts, which can topple trees, power lines, and structures; and abundant snowfall, which disrupts transportation and causes isolation of populations. Extreme meteorological phenomena include tornadoes, which can cause devastating destruction in a small area; waterspouts, which are rotating columns of air that form over the sea; heat waves, which cause increased mortality, especially among vulnerable populations; and intense frosts, which cause significant agricultural losses. In Spain, the most important meteorological risks include torrential rains and flash floods in the Mediterranean, snowfall in mountainous areas, hailstorms in the interior, heat waves in the Guadalquivir valley and La Mancha, and strong winds in coastal and mountainous areas. The management of meteorological risks requires monitoring, prediction, and warning systems that allow authorities and the population to prepare for and respond to these phenomena.

### Alert and prevention systems

Meteorological alert systems are fundamental tools for the prevention and management of risks associated with adverse atmospheric phenomena. These systems are based on the combination of meteorological observation, numerical prediction, and the experience of meteorologists to identify, locate, and evaluate phenomena that may pose a risk to the population. In Spain, the State Meteorological Agency (AEMET) manages the National Meteorological Alert System, which issues warnings classified by severity levels: green (no risk), yellow (local risk), orange (significant risk), and red (extreme risk). Weather warnings are disseminated through multiple channels, including the AEMET website, mobile applications, media, and SMS alert systems and phone calls to local authorities. The prevention of meteorological risks goes beyond the issuance of warnings and includes territorial planning, the construction of defense infrastructure (reservoirs, dikes, channeling), the creation of safe flood zones, citizen education on safe behavior during adverse phenomena, and the preparation of emergency plans at the municipal, regional, and national levels. International cooperation is also an important aspect of meteorological risk management, as atmospheric phenomena do not respect borders and effective response requires coordination between affected countries. Organizations such as the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) play a fundamental role in monitoring and predicting meteorological phenomena that affect multiple regions.

### Impact of meteorological phenomena on society

Adverse meteorological phenomena have a significant impact on multiple dimensions of society, from people's safety to the economy, public health, and the environment. In terms of safety, extreme meteorological phenomena are responsible for a significant number of deaths and injuries worldwide, especially in developing countries where prevention and response infrastructure is less developed. Floods, landslides caused by intense rainfall, lightning, and wind gusts are the main causes of death from meteorological phenomena. The economic impact of adverse meteorological phenomena includes direct damage to infrastructure, housing, crops, and vehicles; disruption of economic activity during and after the phenomenon; and the costs of response, recovery, and reconstruction. In Spain, annual economic losses from adverse meteorological phenomena can reach billions of euros, especially in years with torrential rains, exceptional snowfall, or intense heat waves. The impact on public health includes not only direct victims, but also indirect effects such as increased respiratory and cardiovascular diseases during heat waves, mental health problems associated with natural disasters, and the impact on healthcare services during emergencies. Adverse meteorological phenomena also affect the environment, such as soil erosion, water pollution, loss of biodiversity, and ecosystem degradation. The comprehensive management of meteorological risks requires a multidisciplinary approach that combines meteorological science, engineering, territorial planning, public health, and citizen education.

## Chapter 8: Meteorology and Agriculture

### Influence of climate on agriculture

Climate is one of the determining factors of agricultural activity, as it directly influences crop selection, planting and harvest schedules, farming techniques, and the productivity of agricultural holdings. Temperature determines the range of crops that can be grown in an area, as each species has specific thermal requirements to germinate, grow, and fruit. Precipitation is essential for the development of rain-fed crops, while irrigated crops can compensate for water deficit through artificial irrigation. Solar radiation is fundamental for photosynthesis, the process by which plants convert light energy into chemical energy, and its duration and intensity influence the quantity and quality of production. Wind can be beneficial by facilitating pollination and ventilation of crops, but can also be harmful when excessive, as it can cause mechanical damage, increase soil evaporation, and favor the spread of pests and diseases. The relative humidity of the air influences plant transpiration, the spread of fungal diseases, and the conservation of agricultural products. Climate also affects crop phenology, that is, the timing of biological processes such as budding, flowering, and ripening, which must coincide with adequate environmental conditions to ensure a good harvest. Interannual climatic variability is an important challenge for agriculture, as differences in temperature, precipitation, and extreme phenomena from one year to another can cause significant fluctuations in production and farmer income.

### Agricultural meteorology and prediction techniques

Agricultural meteorology is a specialized discipline that applies meteorological principles to the specific needs of the agricultural sector, providing relevant climatic information for decision-making on agricultural holdings. Agricultural meteorology services provide information on temperature, precipitation, soil moisture, solar radiation, wind, and other climatic parameters that directly affect crops. This information is used to plan agricultural tasks (planting, pruning, harvesting), manage irrigation, apply phytosanitary treatments, prevent damage from frosts or droughts, and optimize resource use. Agricultural prediction models integrate meteorological data with information on soil characteristics, crop phenological status, and water needs to generate customized forecasts for each holding. In Spain, agricultural meteorology services are provided by AEMET, the autonomous communities, and specialized private companies, which offer information through digital platforms, mobile applications, and personalized advice. Agricultural prediction techniques include monitoring soil moisture through remote sensing, estimating crop evapotranspiration, predicting frosts and heat waves, and evaluating the risk of pests and diseases based on climatic conditions. Precision agriculture, which uses technologies such as GPS, remote sensing, and geographic information systems, allows optimizing resource use based on spatial variability within the same plot, improving productive efficiency and reducing environmental impact.

### Impact of climate change on Spanish agriculture

Climate change is having a significant impact on Spanish agriculture, with consequences that affect productivity, crop quality, and sector sustainability. The increase in temperature is causing changes in crop phenology, with advances in flowering and ripening dates, which can affect production quality and synchronization with pollination periods. More frequent and intense heat waves cause thermal stress in crops, reducing the yield and quality of fruits, vegetables, and cereals. Recurring water deficit, aggravated by decreased precipitation and increased evaporation, is causing a reduction in rain-fed cultivation areas and an increase in demand for irrigation water. Crops typical of the Mediterranean climate, such as olives, grapevines, and citrus fruits, are experiencing changes in their geographical distribution, shifting toward higher altitude or latitude areas in search of more favorable conditions. The arrival of new pests and diseases, favored by global warming, poses threats to traditional crops that are not adapted to these biological pressures. Sea level rise and saltwater intrusion into coastal aquifers affect crops in coastal areas, especially in deltas and coastal plains. To adapt to climate change, Spanish agriculture needs to incorporate crop varieties more resistant to heat and drought, optimize irrigation and water management techniques, diversify crops, improve soil health, and adopt more sustainable farming practices. Agricultural research and knowledge transfer are essential components of this adaptation, as farmers need information, tools, and advice to make correct decisions in the context of climate change.

## Chapter 9: Weather and Health

### Effects of climate on human health

Climate and meteorological conditions have a direct and indirect influence on human health, affecting multiple organs and systems of the body. Ambient temperature is one of the most important factors, as the human body has an optimal body temperature range (approximately thirty-seven degrees Celsius) that must be maintained to ensure proper functioning of physiological processes. Extreme heat can cause heat stroke, dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and multiorgan failure, especially in the elderly, children, athletes, and people with chronic diseases. Intense cold can cause hypothermia, frostbite, worsening of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, and increased mortality, especially among homeless people and in areas with poorly insulated homes. Precipitation, wind, humidity, and solar radiation also affect health through indirect mechanisms, such as the spread of infectious diseases, contamination of water and food, and exposure to atmospheric agents. Air quality, which is influenced by meteorological conditions, is a key factor in respiratory health, as the presence of pollutants such as tropospheric ozone, suspended particles, and nitrogen oxides can aggravate asthma, bronchitis, and other lung diseases. Climate also affects mental health, as lack of sunlight during winter can cause seasonal affective disorder (winter depression), while meteorological disasters can cause post-traumatic stress and other psychological health problems.

### Weather-sensitive diseases

Weather-sensitive diseases are those whose incidence or severity is influenced by meteorological conditions, varying according to temperature, atmospheric pressure, humidity, wind, and other climatic factors. Among the most common weather-sensitive diseases are cardiovascular diseases, whose risk increases during both heat waves and episodes of intense cold, as thermal variations cause changes in blood pressure, blood viscosity, and cardiac effort. Respiratory diseases such as asthma, chronic bronchitis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) worsen during episodes of cold, dry air and when air quality is poor due to high pollutant concentrations. Rheumatic and joint diseases may worsen with changes in atmospheric pressure and high humidity, as these factors influence joint inflammation. Headaches and migraines are frequent during storms and sudden changes in atmospheric pressure. Skin diseases such as eczema and psoriasis may be affected by humidity, solar radiation, and temperature. In Spain, weather-sensitive diseases represent a significant public health problem, especially during summer heat waves and winter cold waves, which cause increased hospitalizations and mortality. The protection of vulnerable populations against the health effects of climate requires the implementation of prevention measures, improvement of meteorological and health information, and adaptation of public health policies to climatic conditions.

### Protection and prevention strategies

Protecting health against the effects of climate requires a comprehensive approach that combines prevention, healthcare, and environmental adaptation. For heat waves, protection strategies include adequate hydration, controlled sun exposure, wearing light and light-colored clothing, staying in air-conditioned spaces, reviewing medication for chronic patients, and active monitoring of vulnerable people such as the elderly, children, and the sick. Heat wave action plans, implemented by health authorities, coordinate the response of health services, care for vulnerable people, and citizen information during these episodes. For intense cold, protection measures include wearing warm clothing, adequate heating of homes, proper hydration and nutrition, and review of medication for patients with cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. Air pollution, which can worsen during adverse meteorological conditions, requires measures such as traffic restrictions, limiting industrial activities, and recommending limiting outdoor exercise. The prevention of vector-borne diseases such as dengue or chikungunya, which can be affected by climatic conditions, requires entomological surveillance and the implementation of mosquito control measures. Health education about the effects of climate on health is an essential component of protection, as citizens need to know the risks and prevention measures to protect themselves effectively. The integration of meteorological information into public health systems allows anticipating periods of higher risk and activating protection protocols in a timely manner.

## Chapter 10: Meteorological Instruments

### Basic measurement instruments

Meteorological instruments are devices designed to measure the different atmospheric variables that characterize weather and climate. These instruments have evolved significantly throughout history, from the first mechanical devices to the high-precision electronic sensors used today. The thermometer is one of the most basic and essential instruments, used to measure air temperature. Traditional thermometers are based on the thermal expansion of a liquid (mercury or alcohol) in a calibrated glass tube, while electronic thermometers use electrical resistances or thermocouples to measure temperature with greater precision and speed. The barometer measures atmospheric pressure, and can be mercury (a mercury column balances the weight of the atmosphere) or aneroid (a metal spring deforms with pressure changes). The hygrometer determines the relative humidity of the air, and can be based on the deformation of a hygroscopic material (human hair, synthetic fibers) or on the measurement of the dew point temperature. The anemometer measures wind speed, and the most common is the cup anemometer, which rotates at a speed proportional to wind speed. The wind vane indicates wind direction, and usually has the shape of an arrow or cone pointing in the direction from which the wind comes. The pluviometer collects and measures the amount of precipitation that has fallen over a given period of time, and can be simple (a container that collects water) or automatic (a device that records precipitation in real time). The sunshine recorder logs the hours of sunshine received during the day, using a magnifying glass that concentrates solar rays onto a heat-sensitive paper strip.

### Automatic weather stations

Automatic weather stations (AWS) are devices that collect meteorological data continuously and automatically, without the need for permanent technical staff presence. These stations are composed of a set of sensors that measure the main atmospheric variables: temperature, relative humidity, atmospheric pressure, wind speed and direction, precipitation, solar radiation, soil temperature, among others. The data collected by the sensors is processed by a data logger, an electronic device that stores and transmits information through communication networks (GPRS, internet, satellite) to processing centers. AWS are usually powered by solar panels, which allows them to operate autonomously in remote locations without access to the electrical grid. In Spain, the AEMET automatic weather station network is composed of more than eight hundred stations distributed throughout the territory, including mountainous areas, coasts, valleys, and cities. These stations provide real-time data that is fundamental for weather prediction, climate monitoring, and the issuance of warnings for adverse phenomena. Modern AWS are equipped with high-precision and reliable sensors, and their maintenance includes periodic inspections, calibrations, and replacement of deteriorated components. The density of the weather station network is a key factor for the quality of weather prediction, as a higher density of observations allows a better representation of the spatial variability of weather.

### Weather radars and satellites

Weather radars and meteorological satellites are two of the most powerful tools for large-scale weather observation and prediction. The weather radar works by emitting microwave pulses that bounce off water or ice particles present in clouds, allowing detection of the presence, location, intensity, and movement of precipitation. Precipitation radars are especially useful for real-time monitoring of storms, torrential rains, and snowfall, providing information that allows issuing weather warnings with sufficient advance notice for the population to take protective measures. In Spain, the AEMET radar network is composed of thirteen radars that cover practically the entire territory, providing a complete view of precipitation affecting the country. Meteorological satellites orbit the Earth at altitudes between thirty and thirty-six thousand kilometers (geostationary satellites) or between seven hundred and nine hundred kilometers (polar orbit satellites), and capture images of the atmosphere, clouds, water bodies, and the Earth's surface. Geostationary satellites provide continuous images of the same region, allowing tracking of the evolution of meteorological systems in real time, while polar orbit satellites cover the entire planet on each orbit, providing a global view of the state of the atmosphere. Satellite data is used for weather prediction, climate monitoring, severe weather monitoring, precipitation estimation, and air quality assessment. The combination of radar and satellite data with surface observations and radiosonde measurements provides a complete and detailed view of the state of the atmosphere, significantly improving the accuracy of weather forecasts.

## Chapter 11: Types of Climate in the World

### Koppen climate classification

The Koppen-Geiger climate classification is the most widely used system worldwide for describing and classifying the different types of climate on the planet. Originally developed by the German meteorologist Wladimir Koppen in 1884 and subsequently modified by Rudolf Geiger, this classification is based on threshold values of temperature and precipitation that determine the distribution of natural vegetation. The system uses a letter code that combines five major climatic groups: A (tropical), B (arid), C (temperate), D (continental), and E (polar). Each group is subdivided into subtypes according to temperature and precipitation, creating a detailed system that allows comparison of climates in different regions of the world. The Koppen classification has become a fundamental tool for climatology, geography, agriculture, and territorial planning, as it provides a common framework for describing the planet's climatic diversity. Modern variants of the classification incorporate temperature, precipitation, and evapotranspiration data to more precisely define the boundaries between different climate types. The Koppen-Geiger climate classification maps allow visualizing the global distribution of climates and observing how they change as a function of latitude, altitude, continentality, and other geographical factors. The most recent update of the Koppen-Geiger map, published in 2017, incorporates observation data from more than ten thousand weather stations and shows the distribution of climates across the entire Earth's surface at a resolution of one square kilometer.

### Humid tropical climate (Af)

The humid tropical climate, classified as Af in the Koppen system, is characterized by high temperatures throughout the year and abundant precipitation that is distributed relatively uniformly throughout the year. The monthly average temperature never drops below eighteen degrees Celsius, and the driest month receives at least sixty millimeters of precipitation. This type of climate is found in the equatorial zones of the planet, including the Amazon basin in South America, the Congo basin in Central Africa, Southeast Asia, and the islands of the western Pacific. In these regions, solar radiation is intense throughout the year, causing continuous evaporation of water from oceans, rivers, and vegetation, creating an atmosphere laden with moisture that generates nearly daily convective storms. The annual average temperature ranges between twenty-five and twenty-eight degrees Celsius, with a very small annual temperature range, generally less than three degrees. Annual precipitation can exceed two thousand millimeters, and in some areas such as the Pacific coast of Colombia or northeastern India it exceeds ten thousand millimeters annually. The natural vegetation of the humid tropical climate is tropical rainforest, an ecosystem of extraordinary biodiversity that harbors more than half of the world's plant and animal species. Humid tropical forests play a fundamental role in the global carbon cycle, absorbing large amounts of CO2 from the atmosphere and contributing to global climate regulation. The hydrological cycle in the humid tropical climate is very active, with constant evaporation, transpiration, and precipitation maintaining a continuous flow of water between land, atmosphere, and oceans.

### Tropical savanna climate (Aw)

The tropical savanna climate, classified as Aw in the Koppen system, is characterized by high temperatures throughout the year and a well-defined dry season during the period of lowest sunshine. The monthly average temperature never drops below eighteen degrees, but unlike the humid tropical climate, there is a dry period during which precipitation is scarce or absent. This type of climate is found in tropical regions with marked seasonality, such as the African savanna, the Venezuelan llanos, the Brazilian cerrado, the plains of northern Australia, and parts of Southeast Asia. The dry season generally occurs during the boreal winter (from June to September in the Northern Hemisphere) or during the austral winter (from December to March in the Southern Hemisphere), when the tropical precipitation belts shift toward the equator. During the rainy season, precipitation is intense and frequent, caused by thermal convection and interaction between tropical air masses. The annual average temperature ranges between twenty-four and twenty-eight degrees Celsius, with a slightly larger temperature range than in the humid tropical climate. Annual precipitation ranges between seven hundred and one thousand five hundred millimeters, with concentration in the rainy season that can represent more than eighty percent of the annual total. The natural vegetation of the tropical savanna climate is a mixture of tall grasses and scattered trees, creating an open landscape that supports large herds of herbivores and a rich community of predators. The savanna is a fire-dominated ecosystem, which plays a fundamental role in vegetation dynamics, favoring grass growth and limiting forest expansion. ### Tropical monsoon climate (Am)

The tropical monsoon climate, classified as Am in the Koppen system, is characterized by high temperatures throughout the year and a very intense rainy season caused by the monsoon. Unlike the tropical savanna climate, the dry period in the monsoon climate is short and total annual precipitation is abundant, generally exceeding one thousand five hundred millimeters. This type of climate is found in regions affected by monsoon systems, such as India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, and parts of Southeast Asia. The monsoon is a seasonal wind system that reverses its direction twice a year, causing an intense rainy season during the boreal summer (from June to September) and a dry season during the boreal winter. During the rainy season, the southwesterly monsoon winds transport moisture from the Indian Ocean toward the Indian subcontinent, causing extremely intense precipitation that can exceed three thousand millimeters in four months in some regions. The city of Cherrapunji, in northeastern India, is one of the wettest places in the world, with an average annual precipitation of more than eleven thousand seven hundred millimeters. The annual average temperature in the monsoon climate ranges between twenty-five and twenty-eight degrees Celsius, with a pre-monsoon maximum in April and May that can exceed forty degrees in the plains of northern India. Floods are a recurring and devastating phenomenon during the monsoon season, affecting millions of people and causing significant economic losses. Agriculture in the monsoon climate depends fundamentally on the monsoon, with rice as the main crop during the rainy season and rain-fed crops such as wheat and barley during the dry season.

### Desert climate (BWh and BWk)

The desert climate, classified as BW in the Koppen system, is characterized by extremely scarce precipitation, generally below two hundred fifty millimeters annually, and a potential evapotranspiration that far exceeds the precipitation received. The BWh subtype corresponds to hot desert, with annual average temperatures above eighteen degrees, while BWk corresponds to cold desert, with annual average temperatures below eighteen degrees. Hot deserts are found in subtropical zones, where subtropical anticyclones create conditions of dry, stable air that prevent cloud and precipitation formation. The world's most extensive deserts include the Sahara in North Africa (the largest desert in the world, with more than nine million square kilometers), the Arabian Desert on the Arabian Peninsula, the Kalahari Desert in southern Africa, the Sonoran Desert in North America, and the Gobi Desert in Central Asia. Temperatures in hot deserts can exceed fifty degrees Celsius during the day and drop below zero at night, creating an exceptionally high diurnal temperature range that can exceed forty degrees. Precipitation in deserts is irregular and unpredictable, and may not record rain for consecutive years, followed by episodes of torrential rain that cause flash floods. Cold deserts are found in continental areas of high latitude or high altitude, such as the Gobi Desert, the Patagonian Desert, and the Antarctic polar zones. Vegetation in deserts is extremely sparse and is adapted to the lack of water, with xerophytic plants that can store water in their tissues, reduce their leaf surface, and enter periods of dormancy during the driest seasons. ### Semi-arid climate (BSh and BSk)

The semi-arid climate, classified as BS in the Koppen system, is characterized by scarce precipitation but higher than that of desert climates, generally between two hundred fifty and five hundred millimeters annually, and a potential evapotranspiration that exceeds precipitation, although to a lesser extent than in deserts. The BSh subtype corresponds to hot semi-arid, with annual average temperatures above eighteen degrees, while BSk corresponds to cold semi-arid, with annual average temperatures below eighteen degrees. Semi-arid climates are found in transitions between deserts and more humid areas, forming strips of steppe or dry savanna vegetation. In North America, the Great Plains region experiences a semi-arid climate that extends from Texas to Alberta. In South America, Patagonia and northwestern Argentina present cold semi-arid climates. In Europe, the steppes of Ukraine and southern Russia experience a continental semi-arid climate. In Asia, the steppes of Mongolia and Kazakhstan present cold semi-arid climates. The natural vegetation of the semi-arid climate consists of grasses, low shrubs, and xerophytic plants adapted to prolonged periods of drought. Agriculture in semi-arid zones is mainly extensive livestock farming and rain-fed crops such as wheat, barley, and sunflower, although irrigation allows the development of more intensive crops. Soil degradation from overexploitation, overgrazing, and inadequate farming practices is a significant problem in semi-arid zones, which can lead to desertification, an irreversible process of loss of soil productivity. Climate change is expected to expand the arid and semi-arid zones of the world, increasing pressure on water resources and agriculture in these regions.

### Temperate climate (C)

The temperate climate, classified as C in the Koppen system, is characterized by hot or cool summers and mild winters, with the temperature of the coldest month above three degrees Celsius but below eighteen degrees, and the temperature of the warmest month above ten degrees. This type of climate is found in the middle latitudes of the planet, between tropical climates and continental or polar climates, and encompasses a wide range of subtypes ranging from humid temperate climates to Mediterranean climates. The Cfa subtype (humid temperate, no dry season, hot summer) is found in regions such as the southeastern United States, southeastern China, southeastern Brazil, northeastern Argentina, and southeastern Australia. The Cfb subtype (oceanic temperate, cool summer) affects Western Europe, including the United Kingdom, France, the Netherlands, western Germany, and the coast of Norway. The Csa subtype (hot Mediterranean, dry summer) is found in the Mediterranean basin, California, central Chile, southwestern Australia, and southwestern Africa. The Csb subtype (cool Mediterranean, dry summer) affects regions such as the coast of Oregon and Washington, the coast of central Chile, and parts of New Zealand. The vegetation of the temperate climate includes deciduous forests in areas with uniform precipitation, mixed forests in areas with moderate seasonality, and xerophytic scrubland in Mediterranean areas. Agriculture in the temperate climate is very diverse, with crops such as wheat, barley, grapevines, citrus fruits, olives, and a wide variety of vegetables and fruits. The temperate climate is favorable for human activity due to its moderate temperatures, adequate precipitation, and well-defined seasons, which has contributed to the development of the most densely populated civilizations on the planet.

### Continental climate (D)

The continental climate, classified as D in the Koppen system, is characterized by hot or cool summers and cold winters, with the temperature of the coldest month below three degrees Celsius or below zero degrees depending on the criteria used. This type of climate is found in the high and middle latitudes of both hemispheres, where ocean influence is moderate and continentality creates marked thermal differences between summer and winter. The Dfa subtype (humid continental, hot summer) is found in regions such as central United States, central Eastern Europe, and northeastern China. The Dfb subtype (humid continental, cool summer) affects southern Scandinavia, central Europe, and northeastern Asia. The Dfc subtype (subarctic, short, cool summer) extends across Canada, Alaska, northern Scandinavia, and Siberia. The annual average temperature in the continental climate can range between zero and ten degrees Celsius, with annual temperature ranges that can exceed forty degrees in the most interior areas. Precipitation in the continental climate is generally moderate, between four hundred and eight hundred millimeters annually, with a summer maximum caused by thermal convection. Winters are harsh, with persistent frosts, abundant snowfall, and periods of extremely low temperatures that can drop below minus forty degrees in Siberia. The natural vegetation of the continental climate includes coniferous forests (taiga) at high latitudes, deciduous forests at middle latitudes, and steppes in the driest areas. Continental agriculture is limited by the short growing season and the risk of late frosts, with crops such as winter wheat, barley, oats, and potatoes. ### Polar climate (E)

The polar climate, classified as E in the Koppen system, is characterized by extremely low temperatures throughout the year, with the temperature of the warmest month below ten degrees Celsius. The ET subtype (tundra polar) corresponds to areas where the temperature of the warmest month ranges between zero and ten degrees, while the EF subtype (ice cap polar) corresponds to areas where the temperature of the warmest month is below zero degrees. Polar climates are found in high-latitude regions, including the Arctic and Antarctica, and in high mountain zones above the permanent snow line. The annual average temperature in polar regions can drop below minus twenty degrees Celsius, with absolute minimums in Antarctica that have recorded values below minus eighty-nine degrees Celsius at Vostok station. Precipitation in polar climates is generally scarce, between one hundred and two hundred millimeters annually in water equivalent, but low evaporation allows the accumulation of snow and ice that form the polar ice caps. The Antarctic ice sheet contains approximately seventy percent of the planet's fresh water, while the Greenland ice sheet contains approximately ten percent. Vegetation in the polar tundra climate is sparse, composed of mosses, lichens, grasses, and low shrubs adapted to extreme cold and periods of continuous light during summer. Polar fauna includes species adapted to cold such as polar bears, white bears, penguins, seals, walruses, and caribou. Climate change is dramatically affecting polar regions, with warming that can be two or three times higher than the global average, causing the reduction of Arctic sea ice, the destabilization of Antarctic glaciers, and global sea level rise.

### High mountain climates

High mountain climates constitute a special type of climate characterized by abrupt changes in temperature, pressure, and meteorological conditions as a function of altitude. Unlike climates determined primarily by latitude, mountain climates are influenced by altitude, slope orientation, gradient, and exposure to wind and sun. In mountains, temperature decreases with altitude at an average rate of six degrees Celsius per thousand meters of ascent, which allows finding climates ranging from tropical at the base of mountains to polar at their summits. This altitudinal zonation creates a succession of bioclimatic levels with vegetation and fauna adapted to each altitude range. In the Andes, for example, one can find tropical rainforests in the Amazon to glaciers on summits exceeding six thousand meters. Meteorological phenomena in mountains are especially variable and can change rapidly over short periods of time. Convective storms are frequent during the afternoon in mountains, when ground heating causes the ascent of moist air. Orographic precipitation, caused by the lifting of air when encountering a mountain barrier, can be extremely intense on windward slopes, while leeward slopes experience a foehn effect with warm, dry air. Snowfall in mountains can be abundant, especially in areas where air humidity is high, and mountain glaciers are sensitive indicators of climate change, as their retreat reflects global temperature rise. Mountain agriculture adapts to the climatic conditions of each altitudinal level, with crops such as corn in low-lying areas, potatoes and quinoa in intermediate areas, and livestock in higher areas. ### Influence of ocean currents on climate

Ocean currents play a fundamental role in global climate regulation, transporting heat from tropical zones to high latitudes and modulating the climatic conditions of coastal regions. The thermohaline circulation, also known as the global conveyor belt, is a system of deep ocean currents that transports warm water from the tropics to the North Atlantic, where it cools, becomes denser, and sinks to the depths, initiating a circuit that completes its journey in approximately one thousand years. This circulation is responsible for the relatively mild climate of Western Europe, which experiences temperatures much higher than other regions at the same latitude. The Gulf Stream, a warm ocean current flowing from the Gulf of Mexico toward the North Atlantic, transports an amount of energy equivalent to one hundred thousand gigawatt power plants, warming the western coasts of Europe and contributing to the oceanic climate of the United Kingdom, Ireland, Norway, and Iceland. Without the Gulf Stream, winter temperatures in Western Europe would be approximately five degrees lower than they currently are. ### Microclimates and their importance

Microclimates are the specific climatic conditions that occur in small areas, where local factors significantly modify the general climatic conditions of the region. While general climates are classified at regional or global scales, microclimates can vary significantly over distances of just a few meters, creating favorable or unfavorable conditions for vegetation, agriculture, and human life. The factors that determine microclimates include topography (slope, orientation, altitude), land cover (vegetation, water, asphalt), the presence of buildings and artificial structures, proximity to water bodies, and exposure to wind and shade. In the same city, microclimates can vary notably between areas with parks and groves, which are cooler and more humid, and areas with buildings and asphalt, which accumulate heat and create urban heat islands. Valleys experience special microclimates, with cold air that descends slopes at night (katabatic drainage) and accumulates at the valley bottom, creating thermal inversions that favor fog and frost formation. South-facing slopes in the Northern Hemisphere receive more solar radiation than north-facing slopes, creating significant thermal differences that influence vegetation and crops. Coastal microclimates are characterized by the thermal moderation of the sea, which reduces diurnal and annual temperature ranges, and by the presence of sea breezes that cool coastal areas during the day. ## Chapter 12: Precipitation Phenomena

### Rain: types and formation

Rain is the most common form of precipitation and consists of water droplets that fall from clouds toward the Earth's surface when they reach a size sufficient to overcome air resistance. Rain formation requires three simultaneous conditions: sufficient humidity in the atmosphere for cloud formation, condensation nuclei on which water vapor condenses into droplets, and a growth mechanism for the droplets that allows them to reach the necessary size to precipitate. Condensation nuclei are tiny particles present in the atmosphere, such as sea salt crystals, dust particles, volcanic ash, or pollutants, that facilitate the condensation of water vapor. Rain is classified according to its intensity: light rain (less than two millimeters per hour), moderate rain (between two and eight millimeters per hour), heavy rain (between eight and fifty millimeters per hour), and torrential rain (more than fifty millimeters per hour). Torrential rain is especially dangerous because it can cause flash floods, landslides, and damage to infrastructure. In Spain, torrential rains are especially frequent on the Mediterranean coast during autumn, when the interaction between cold air aloft and sea water creates favorable conditions for the formation of intense storms. The main mechanism for raindrop growth in temperate climates is the Bergeron process, which occurs when supercooled water droplets (below zero degrees) coexist with ice crystals in clouds. The ice crystals grow at the expense of supercooled droplets, as the saturation vapor pressure of ice is lower than that of water, and when they reach sufficient size they break free and melt as they descend through warmer air, reaching the surface as rain. ### Snow: crystals and accumulation

Snow is a form of solid precipitation that consists of ice crystals that form in clouds when the temperature is low enough for water vapor to sublimate directly into ice crystals without passing through the liquid state. Snow crystals have a hexagonal structure based on the molecular structure of water, and their shape depends on the temperature and humidity of the atmosphere in which they form. At temperatures near zero degrees, crystals take the form of plates or needles; between minus five and minus ten degrees, six-branched stars with elaborate patterns form; at temperatures below minus twenty degrees, crystals are simple hexagonal prisms. Each snow crystal is unique, with a different thermal and historical heritage that determines its final form. Snow is classified by its fall as: light snowfall (less than one centimeter per hour), moderate snowfall (between one and five centimeters per hour), heavy snowfall (between five and ten centimeters per hour), and very heavy snowfall (more than ten centimeters per hour). Snow accumulation depends on the relationship between precipitation and temperature: when the temperature is near zero degrees, the snow is wet and heavy, and its ratio to liquid precipitation is approximately ten to one (ten centimeters of snow equals one millimeter of water); when the temperature is very low, the snow is dry and light, with a ratio that can reach thirty to one. In Spain, the most significant snowfalls occur in mountainous areas (Pyrenees, Cantabrian Mountains, Central System, Sierra Nevada) and in interior regions during cold waves, when polar or Siberian air masses reach the Peninsula. ### Hail: formation and damage

Hail is a form of solid precipitation that consists of balls or irregular pieces of ice that fall from cumulonimbus clouds when the storm's updraft is not strong enough to keep them suspended. Hail formation requires storms with strong updrafts (exceeding thirty kilometers per hour) that can transport supercooled water droplets toward the upper areas of the cloud, where they freeze and grow through successive layers of ice. Hail growth occurs when ice grains pass through areas of the cloud with different concentrations of supercooled droplets, accumulating layers of transparent ice (when the droplet freezes rapidly) and layers of opaque ice (when the droplet freezes slowly and traps air bubbles). Hail grains can reach sizes from small grains less than one centimeter to giant stones more than fifteen centimeters in diameter, which can weigh more than one kilogram and cause devastating damage. Hail is classified by its size: small hail (less than one centimeter), medium hail (between one and two centimeters), heavy hail (between two and five centimeters), and giant hail (more than five centimeters). In Spain, the most frequent hailstorms occur in the interior of the Peninsula during summer, when ground heating causes the formation of intense convective storms. Damage caused by hail includes destruction of crops (especially fruit trees, vineyards, and vegetables), damage to vehicles (dents in bodywork and broken glass), damage to buildings (broken roofs and windows), and damage to infrastructure (solar panels, antennas, signage). Hail prediction is especially difficult due to its reduced scale and high spatial variability, which requires high-resolution radars and detailed numerical models. ### Sleet and drizzle

Sleet is a form of mixed precipitation that consists of a mixture of raindrops and snowflakes that fall simultaneously from clouds. It occurs when the air temperature in the middle zone of the atmosphere is high enough for ice crystals to partially melt, but the surface temperature is not high enough for them to arrive completely melted. Sleet typically occurs when the surface air temperature is between zero and two degrees Celsius, in a very narrow thermal band that determines whether precipitation falls as rain, sleet, or snow. Drizzle is precipitation of very small water droplets, generally less than half a millimeter in diameter, that fall from low stratiform clouds such as stratus and nimbostratus. Drizzle differs from rain in droplet size and precipitation intensity, which is generally less than one millimeter per hour. ## Chapter 15: Astronomical Basis of the Seasons

### The tilt of the Earth's axis

The fundamental cause of the seasons of the year is the tilt of the Earth's axis relative to the plane of its orbit around the Sun. The Earth's axis of rotation is inclined approximately twenty-three and a half degrees relative to the perpendicular to the ecliptic plane, the plane containing the Earth's orbit around the Sun. This tilt remains relatively constant during the precession cycle, which lasts approximately twenty-six thousand years, meaning that over a period of one year, the orientation of the Earth's axis relative to the stars remains virtually invariant. Consequently, as the Earth travels in its orbit around the Sun, the tilt of the axis causes different latitudes to receive different amounts of solar radiation throughout the year. When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, it receives solar radiation more directly and for more hours per day, causing summer in that hemisphere and winter in the Southern Hemisphere. Six months later, when the Earth has traveled halfway around its orbit, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun, receiving solar radiation more obliquely and for fewer hours per day, causing winter in that hemisphere and summer in the Southern Hemisphere. The equinoxes, which occur approximately on March 21 and September 21, are the moments when the Earth's axis is not tilted toward or away from the Sun, and both hemispheres receive equal amounts of solar radiation, with days and nights of approximately equal duration across the entire planet. The solstices, which occur approximately on June 21 and December 21, are the moments when the tilt of the Earth's axis is maximum toward or away from the Sun, and one of the hemispheres receives the most direct solar radiation for the most hours per day, while the other receives the most oblique radiation for the fewest hours.

### The Earth's orbit and eccentricity

The Earth's orbit around the Sun is not perfectly circular, but has an elliptical shape with an eccentricity of approximately zero point sixteen. This means that the distance between the Earth and Sun varies throughout the year, being smallest at perihelion (the point closest to the Sun), which occurs around January 3, and largest at aphelion (the point farthest from the Sun), which occurs around July 4. The difference in Earth-Sun distance between perihelion and aphelion is approximately five million kilometers, representing a variation of three point three percent in distance. This variation in distance causes a difference of approximately seven percent in the amount of solar radiation received by the Earth between perihelion and aphelion. However, this effect is secondary compared to the effect of the axis tilt, as the distribution of solar radiation on the Earth's surface depends more on the angle of solar rays than on distance from the Sun. In fact, the Northern Hemisphere receives more solar radiation during the Southern Hemisphere summer, when the Earth is at aphelion and farthest from the Sun, demonstrating that the effect of the axis tilt is predominant over the effect of orbital eccentricity. The eccentricity of the Earth's orbit is not constant, but fluctuates over cycles of approximately one hundred thousand years due to gravitational perturbations from other planets, especially Jupiter and Saturn. These fluctuations in eccentricity, together with variations in the axis tilt (forty-one thousand year cycle) and the precession of the equinoxes (twenty-six thousand year cycle), are the three orbital factors that determine long-term climatic variations known as the Milankovitch cycles. ## Chapter 18: Extreme Weather Events

### Definition and classification of extreme events

An extreme weather event is defined as an atmospheric phenomenon that deviates significantly from normal conditions for a given region and time of year, and can cause significant damage to people, property, agriculture, and the environment. Extreme weather events are classified into different categories according to their nature, intensity, and potential impact. The main categories include extreme temperatures (heat waves and cold waves), extreme precipitation (torrential rains, exceptional snowfall), extreme winds (storms, hurricanes, tornadoes), severe atmospheric phenomena (hail, lightning, waterspouts), and compound events that combine multiple extreme factors. The classification of a phenomenon as extreme is based on its statistical frequency: an event is considered extreme when its frequency of occurrence is below ten percent, that is, when it is expected to occur less than once every ten years in a given region. The rarest extreme events, with frequencies below one percent (once every hundred years or more), are called exceptional extreme events and can cause catastrophic damage. The intensity of an extreme event is measured using different indices depending on the type of phenomenon: the Saffir-Simpson scale for hurricanes, the Enhanced Fujita scale for tornadoes, the Beaufort scale for wind, and statistical percentiles for temperatures and precipitation. Climate change is altering the frequency, intensity, and distribution of extreme weather events, with evidence of an increase in the frequency and intensity of heat waves, intense rainfall, and severe meteorological phenomena in many regions of the world.

## Chapter 19: Climate and the Oceans

### Global thermohaline circulation

Global thermohaline circulation, also known as the ocean conveyor belt, is a system of deep ocean currents that transports water through all of the planet's oceans, distributing heat, nutrients, dissolved gases, and marine organisms on a global scale. This circulation system is driven by density differences in ocean water, which depend on temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline). Cold, salty water is denser than warm, fresh water, and tends to sink to the depths, creating a continuous flow of water from the surface to the ocean floor and back. The main driver of thermohaline circulation is the formation of deep water in the North Atlantic, where surface water flowing northward with the Gulf Stream cools, becomes denser, and sinks to the depths. This North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) spreads southward along the ocean floor, mixing with deep water formed in Antarctica (AABW), and eventually rises to the surface in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, completing a circuit that takes approximately one thousand years. Thermohaline circulation transports an enormous amount of energy, equivalent to one hundred thousand gigawatt power plants, and is responsible for the relatively mild climate of Western Europe, which experiences temperatures much higher than other regions at the same latitude. Climate change is potentially affecting thermohaline circulation, as ocean warming and freshwater input from melting Greenland glaciers are reducing water density in the North Atlantic, which could weaken deep water formation and slow circulation.
