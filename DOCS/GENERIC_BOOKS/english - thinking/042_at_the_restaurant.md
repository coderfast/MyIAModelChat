## Chapter 1: Arriving at the Restaurant

### 1.1 Choosing the Restaurant

Choosing the right restaurant is the first fundamental step to enjoying a satisfying and pleasant dining experience. In today's world, there are multiple factors we should consider before deciding on an establishment, including the type of cuisine, the available budget, the location, reviews from other diners, and our own food preferences. Technology has revolutionized the way we choose restaurants, with apps like TripAdvisor, Google Maps, and Yelp providing reviews, photos, and ratings that facilitate informed decision-making. However, it is important to remember that online reviews do not always reflect the complete experience, since factors like service, atmosphere, and dish presentation can vary significantly depending on the day and time. Recommendations from friends and family remain one of the most reliable sources of information, since they know our tastes and can provide detailed personal experiences. When choosing a restaurant, it is helpful to consider the occasion: a business dinner may require a more formal and elegant establishment, while a casual meal with friends may be better suited to a relaxed and cozy place. The location of the restaurant is also important, both in terms of accessibility and setting: a restaurant with sea views, in a historic neighborhood, or near a tourist attraction can add an experiential dimension to the meal. The restaurant menu, when available online, allows you to anticipate the gastronomic offerings and check whether there are suitable options for dietary restrictions or preferences. ### 1.2 Arrival and Entry Protocol

Arrival at the restaurant marks the formal beginning of the dining experience and establishes the first impressions that will influence the entire meal. Entry protocol varies depending on the type of establishment: in a casual restaurant, we can walk in directly and look for a free table or wait to be seated, while in a high-end restaurant, we will generally be greeted at the door by a maître or head waiter who will escort us to our table. Punctuality is especially important for reservations, as arriving late not only affects our own experience but can also disrupt the restaurant's planning and the experience of other diners. If we arrive before the reserved time, it is courteous to wait in the reception area or bar until our table is ready, avoiding occupying tables that may be designated for other guests. When being greeted, it is customary to provide the name under which the reservation was made, along with the number of people in the party. If we do not have a reservation, it is common to inquire about availability: "Good afternoon, do you have a table for two tonight?" is an appropriate and respectful way to initiate the interaction. In restaurants with a terrace or different areas, we can indicate our preference if possible: "We would like to sit on the terrace, if possible." Restaurant staff are usually friendly and helpful, and their role is to facilitate our experience, so we should not hesitate to ask questions about the menu, dish availability, or any other queries that arise. The appropriate attitude during arrival is one of patience and flexibility: there may sometimes be a brief wait, especially during peak hours, and this wait is part of the dining experience that we should accept with composure and good humor.

### 1.3 Table Assignment

Table assignment is an important moment within the dining experience that can significantly influence our comfort and enjoyment during the meal. In restaurants with a maître service, this professional will offer us a table based on availability and our preferences, considering factors such as the number of diners, the occasion, and the physical conditions of the establishment. If we have a preference for a specific location, it is appropriate to request it politely: "Would it be possible to sit by the window?" or "Do you have a table in the quieter corner?" However, it is important to understand that the restaurant manages its tables strategically to optimize service, so it will not always be possible to fulfill all requests. In establishments without maître service, table assignment is usually more flexible: we can choose a free table that appeals to us, but we should avoid sitting at reserved tables or in areas designated for a specific service. The comfort of the table is essential: we should verify that the chairs are comfortable, that there is enough space for the diners and the tableware, and that the lighting is adequate for reading the menu without difficulty. If the assigned table is not satisfactory for any reason (too close to the kitchen, too cold, too noisy), it is acceptable to politely ask if we can move to another one: "Excuse me, would it be possible to move us to a slightly quieter table?" The location of the table can also influence the experience: tables near the kitchen may have stronger odors, tables by the windows may be cooler, and central tables may be livelier. In large restaurants, it is sometimes helpful to indicate if we prefer a table in a specific area of the establishment, such as near the restroom (for convenience) or far from it (for hygiene). ### 1.4 Seating Protocol

Once the table is assigned, seating protocol includes a series of gestures and behaviors that set the tone for the dining experience and show respect for the restaurant and the other diners. The first thing we should do when sitting down is place the napkin on our thighs, making sure it does not hang too low or move during the meal. If there are cloth napkins, we should unfold them and place them on our laps, not on our chest or head. In formal restaurants, staff may help us with the chair, a gesture of courtesy that we should acknowledge with a simple "thank you." The arrangement of cutlery may seem intimidating at first, but it follows a simple logic: the utensils that will be used first are on the outer edges, and they are used from the outside in as the meal progresses. If we are not sure which utensil to use for a particular dish, it is best to observe what the other diners do or wait for the server to indicate the next course. When seated, it is important to maintain an upright but relaxed posture, avoiding leaning back excessively in the chair or resting elbows on the table. Hands should rest on the thighs or lap when not in use, and feet should be kept on the floor, avoiding swinging the legs or crossing them in a way that bothers others. If it is necessary to use a mobile phone before the meal begins, it is courteous to set it to silent and only check it briefly if strictly necessary, avoiding phone conversations or prolonged use that may be bothersome to other diners. Seating protocol establishes a framework of behavior that facilitates a pleasant dining experience for everyone present, creating an atmosphere of respect and comfort that allows full enjoyment of the meal and company.

### 1.5 The Initial Wait

The initial wait after sitting down is a natural moment within the dining experience that can vary in duration depending on the restaurant, the time of day, and the workload of the kitchen and service. During this wait, it is customary to first receive water (which is generally tap water and free of charge, although we can also request still or sparkling mineral water), followed by bread and butter or olive oil to accompany the meal. Water is usually served in a large glass, and it is appropriate to take small sips while waiting, without draining the glass quickly. The bread is presented in various ways depending on the type of restaurant: it may be a shared basket, individual rolls, or a slice of rustic bread, and it is consumed during the wait or before the arrival of the first course. It is common to spread butter on the bread or dip it in olive oil, but it is important not to start eating the bread before all diners have received theirs, as a gesture of consideration and patience. The wait is also a time for conversation, examining the menu at leisure, and discussing with fellow diners what dishes they might order and whether they wish to share some options. If the wait exceeds expectations, it is acceptable to ask the server about the status of the order politely: "Excuse me, approximately how much longer until the first course arrives?" The attitude during the wait should be patient and understanding: the kitchen may be working at full capacity, and a carefully prepared dish requires time that should not be rushed. Using this time to enjoy the restaurant's atmosphere, observe the decor, and socialize with table companions can turn the wait into a pleasant part of the dining experience, rather than a nuisance to be endured.

The daily menu or executive menu is a popular option especially in Spain and Latin America, offering a limited selection of dishes at a fixed price, generally more affordable than ordering individual dishes. This type of menu usually includes a first course, a second course, a beverage, bread, and dessert or coffee, representing excellent value for money. The tasting menu is a more elaborate dining experience that offers a succession of small dishes designed by the chef to showcase the variety and creativity of the kitchen. Tasting menus may include between five and fifteen dishes, and are often accompanied by wine pairings selected to complement each dish. The fixed menu or prix fixe offers a limited number of options for each course of the meal (usually two or three options per category) at a predetermined price. ### 2.3 Understanding Ingredients

Understanding the ingredients listed on the menu is fundamental to making informed decisions about what to order, especially for people with dietary restrictions, allergies, or specific dietary preferences. Modern menus usually include information about commonly recognized allergens, such as nuts, lactose, gluten, shellfish, eggs, and fish, which may be indicated with codes, icons, or explanatory text. For people with severe food allergies, it is crucial to communicate with the restaurant before ordering to ensure that dishes can be prepared safely. Culinary terms on the menu may be confusing for the non-expert diner: "rare" refers to lightly cooked meat, "medium" indicates a medium doneness, and "well done" means fully cooked. In fish dishes, "grilled" indicates cooking on a hot surface without oil, "baked" means cooked in the oven, and "fried" implies cooking in hot oil. Terms for sauces may also require explanation: "béchamel" is a white sauce made from butter, flour, and milk; "hollandaise" is an emulsified sauce made from egg yolk and butter; and "pesto" is a sauce made from basil, garlic, pine nuts, and Parmesan cheese. Dishes described as "seasonal" use fresh ingredients available at that time of year, which generally ensures better quality and flavor. Geographic references in dish names, such as "Milanese" or "Provençal," indicate preparation styles typical of those regions. If an ingredient is unfamiliar to us, it is entirely appropriate to ask the server to explain: "What exactly does dish X contain?" or "Could you describe how this dish is prepared?" Understanding ingredients not only improves our dining experience but also allows us to better appreciate the culinary art and creative decisions of the chef.

### 2.4 The Role of the Server

The server plays a crucial role in the dining experience, functioning as a guide, advisor, and facilitator who connects the diner with the kitchen and the restaurant's service. A good server should have exhaustive knowledge of the menu, including the ingredients of each dish, preparation techniques, possible allergens, and beverage pairing suggestions. Their primary role is to assist the diner in choosing dishes that suit their tastes, budget, and dietary needs, offering personalized recommendations based on their knowledge of the menu and the availability of fresh ingredients that day. The server is also responsible for managing the pace of the meal, ensuring that dishes are served in a logical order and at the appropriate temperature, and attending to special requests from diners with kindness and efficiency. In high-end restaurants, the server may offer a wine list and advise on optimal pairings for the chosen dishes, a service that adds an additional dimension to the dining experience. Communication with the server should be clear and respectful: when ordering, we should specify any special requests (no onions, cooked rare, gluten-free) explicitly to avoid misunderstandings. If there is a problem with a dish (too salty, cold, poorly cooked), it is important to communicate this to the server in a calm and constructive manner, allowing the restaurant the opportunity to correct it. The server can also provide information about the restaurant, such as its history, the chef, the gastronomic philosophy, or the house specialties, enriching the experience with cultural and historical context. Appropriate recognition of the server's service through tipping is a way to show appreciation for their dedication and professionalism, completing the dining experience with a gesture of generosity and appreciation.

## Chapter 3: Placing an Order

### 3.1 The Moment to Order

The moment of placing an order is one of the critical points of the dining experience where we must make decisions that will influence our satisfaction throughout the meal. This moment generally arrives after having examined the menu at leisure, consulted with fellow diners, and received the server's recommendations if we requested them. The most common signal to place an order is when the server approaches our table asking "Have you decided?" or "Would you like me to take your order?", indicating that they are ready to receive our orders. If we are not yet ready, it is appropriate to respond honestly: "A few more minutes, please, we are still deciding." We should not feel pressured to order quickly, since a restaurant that values service quality will allow its diners the time necessary to make an informed decision. Before ordering, it is helpful to verify with fellow diners whether they wish to share some dishes (such as tapas, starters, or desserts), as this collective decision should be made before each person orders individually. The order in which the order is placed may vary depending on the restaurant's custom: in some places it is customary to order drinks first and dishes later, while in others everything is noted simultaneously. If any diner has specific dietary needs, it is important to mention them at this moment so that the restaurant can adapt the dishes accordingly. The appropriate way to order varies depending on the formality level of the restaurant: in a casual establishment we can say "I'll have a hamburger with french fries," while in a more formal one it would be more appropriate to say "I would like to order the grilled salmon, please." Regardless of the style, the order should be clear, specific, and include any special requests we wish to make.

Once the shared dishes are decided, each person can place their individual order for the main course and dessert, although some groups prefer to continue sharing these items as well. Coordination is especially important when there are dietary restrictions in the group: if someone is vegetarian, allergic, or has religious preferences, it is essential to ensure that there are suitable options for all people before finalizing the order. In large groups, it may be useful to designate one person to communicate the complete order to the server, avoiding the confusion that can arise when multiple people speak simultaneously. If the group is very large (more than eight people), some restaurants offer special group menus that simplify the process and ensure a balanced experience for everyone. Communication during the group order should include confirmations that all special requests have been recorded correctly, since with multiple dishes the risk of errors increases. If someone in the group changes their mind after the order has been placed, it is important to communicate the change to the server as soon as possible, before the kitchen begins preparing the dishes. Group orders should also consider the pace of the meal: if some diners eat faster than others, it may be necessary to synchronize order timing so that everyone can enjoy the meal simultaneously. ## Chapter 4: Allergens and Restrictions

### 4.1 Types of Food Allergens

Food allergens are substances that can cause adverse reactions in sensitive people, and their identification and management is a fundamental responsibility for both restaurants and the diners themselves. The European Union recognizes fourteen major allergens that must be indicated on menus in food service establishments: gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soy, milk, nuts, celery, mustard, sesame seeds, sulfites, mollusks, and lupins. Gluten, present in wheat, barley, rye, and their derivatives, is one of the most common allergens and can cause anything from mild digestive discomfort to severe reactions in people with celiac disease. Nuts, including walnuts, almonds, hazelnuts, and cashews, are another common allergen that can potentially cause life-threatening reactions in highly sensitive people. Milk and dairy products contain proteins that can cause allergy or intolerance, manifesting in symptoms ranging from bloating and diarrhea to respiratory difficulty in severe cases. Eggs are another common allergen, especially in children, and can be found not only in obvious dishes but also in sauces, pastries, and processed products where their presence is not evident. Shellfish (shrimp, prawns, langoustines) and fish are allergens that can cause severe reactions, and their presence in dishes must be clearly indicated to avoid confusion. Sulfites, used as preservatives in wines, dried fruits, and some processed foods, can trigger asthma attacks in sensitive people. For people with food allergies, clear communication with the restaurant is absolutely critical: informing the server of our allergy before ordering, asking about the ingredients of each dish, and verifying that precautions are taken against cross-contamination are steps that can save lives.

### 4.2 Voluntary Dietary Restrictions

Voluntary dietary restrictions, unlike allergies, are personal choices based on ethical, religious, health, or personal preference reasons that equally require consideration and respect from restaurants. Vegetarianism, which excludes meat but allows other animal products such as eggs and dairy, is one of the most widespread restrictions, driven by ethical concerns about animal welfare, health reasons, or personal preferences. Veganism goes further, excluding all animal-derived products, including meat, fish, eggs, dairy, and honey, which requires careful planning to ensure adequate nutrition. Religion-based diets include halal in Islam, which prohibits pork and alcohol, and kosher in Judaism, which establishes strict rules about which animals may be consumed and how they must be slaughtered and prepared. Health-based diets, such as the ketogenic diet (low in carbohydrates), gluten-free diet for non-celiac sensitivity, or diabetic diets, require adapted menus that consider specific nutritional needs. Paleo and grain-free diets eliminate cereals, legumes, and processed products, focusing on natural foods like meat, fish, vegetables, fruits, and nuts. Low-FODMAP diets, recommended for people with irritable bowel syndrome, restrict certain fermentable carbohydrates that can cause digestive discomfort. Restaurants that seek to serve this growing diversity of voluntary dietary restrictions must invest in staff training, menu updates, and transparent communication about the ingredients and preparation methods of their dishes. ### 4.4 Food Safety Protocols

Food safety protocols are systems of procedures and practices designed to prevent food contamination and protect the health of diners, and are especially important regarding allergen management. Restaurants must comply with current regulations on allergen information, which requires the availability of clear and accessible information about the presence of the fourteen recognized allergens in all dishes offered. This information can be provided in various ways: on the menu itself, on a separate allergen-specific menu, through QR codes linking to detailed information, or by consulting restaurant staff. Staff training is a fundamental pillar of safety protocols: all employees, from the server to the chef, should know the allergens present in each dish and the procedures to follow when a diner reports an allergy. Cross-contamination prevention requires specific practices in the kitchen: separate utensils for preparing allergen-free foods, designated preparation areas, and rigorous cleaning procedures that prevent the transfer of allergenic residues between dishes. Proper labeling of ingredients in storage and supplier traceability are fundamental aspects of the food safety system that allow quick identification of the source of any problem. ## Chapter 5: The Bill and the Tip

### 5.1 Requesting the Bill

Requesting the bill is the final step of the dining experience that formally closes the meal and allows proceeding with payment for the services received. The appropriate moment to request the bill generally arrives when all diners have finished eating and drinking, and the conversation has reached a natural point of conclusion. The most common signal is making eye contact with the server and giving a discreet hand gesture or saying "The bill, please" or "Could you bring us the bill?" In some restaurants, especially more technologically advanced ones, the bill may be requested through a button at the table or a mobile app, although human contact remains the most common and universal form. It is important not to request the bill too soon, before all diners have finished, as it may be perceived as unnecessary rushing that negatively affects the experience. It is also not appropriate to wait an excessively long time after finishing the meal, as it may unnecessarily occupy the table and affect the restaurant's ability to serve other customers. The bill, when it arrives, is generally presented in a folder or small tray containing the breakdown of dishes and beverages consumed, plus taxes and, where applicable, service. It is good practice to review the bill carefully to verify that the dishes and prices match what was ordered, and that there are no billing errors. If we detect any error, it is appropriate to point it out to the server politely so that it can be corrected. The process of requesting the bill may be more or less formal depending on the type of restaurant: in a casual establishment it may suffice to raise a hand or say "The bill" to a passing server, while in a more formal one it may be more appropriate to wait for the server to approach and request it verbally.

Contactless card payments have become increasingly popular, enabling fast and secure transactions that reduce wait times at the end of the meal. Mobile payment apps like Apple Pay, Google Pay, or Bizum allow making payments from a smartphone, an option especially convenient for regular users of digital technology. Some restaurants accept restaurant checks or meal vouchers, payment instruments frequently used by companies as part of their employees' benefits. In international tourism contexts, it is important to verify whether the restaurant accepts international cards and in what currency the conversion will be made, to avoid surprises on the bank statement. Restaurants that offer online reservation services sometimes allow advance payment or storing card details as a guarantee, a practice that has become more common since the pandemic. ### 5.3 The Tip: Customs and Protocol

Tipping is a practice of gratuity for restaurant staff for their service, and its protocol varies significantly depending on the culture, the country, and the type of establishment. ## Chapter 7: Table Etiquette

### 7.1 Cutlery Protocol

Cutlery protocol is one of the most formal and potentially intimidating aspects of table etiquette, but understanding its simple logic eliminates confusion and allows us to navigate with confidence in any restaurant. The fundamental rule is that cutlery is used from the outside in as the meal progresses, starting with those furthest from the central plate. For a basic setting, forks go on the left of the plate, knives on the right (with the blade facing the plate), and spoons above the plate or to the right of the knives. The fork furthest from the plate is generally for the starter, the next for the fish if there is one, and the closest for the main course. Knives follow the same order: the furthest for the starter, and the closest for the main course. If there are additional spoons, they may be for dessert, soup, or coffee. Dessert cutlery, when present, is usually placed above the plate, with the fork pointing right and the spoon pointing left. When we have finished eating a dish, we place the cutlery parallel on the plate in the 4:20 position (fork on the left, knife on the right, both angled toward the center), indicating to the server that we are ready for the next course. If we need to take a break during the meal, we place the cutlery in the 10:10 position (fork and knife forming an inverted V), indicating that we have not yet finished. Special cutlery such as seafood forks, soup spoons, or spreading knives have specific uses that depend on the type of restaurant and the dishes served. Regular practice with different cutlery arrangements develops confidence and naturalness in their use, turning it into an automatic gesture that allows focus on the food and conversation.

### 7.2 Tableware Protocol

Tableware protocol complements cutlery protocol and establishes the rules for using plates, glasses, and other service elements that compose the complete table setting. The main plate, generally the largest, is placed in the center of the diner and serves as the reference point for all other elements. Smaller plates around the main one may be for bread (usually to the left of the diner), for butter, or for specific appetizers. Glasses are generally placed to the right of the diner, in order of use: the water glass is the largest and is placed furthest to the left of the glass group, followed by red wine glasses (larger), white wine glasses (medium), and dessert wine or champagne glasses (smaller). The arrangement of glasses follows the "finger and thumb" rule: the water glass is closer to the diner, and the wine glasses recede progressively. Cloth napkins are unfolded and placed on the lap when seated, and removed from the table when all diners have finished eating (although in some formal restaurants, the napkin is placed on the chair if one temporarily leaves). Serving dishes, such as cheese plates, sauce boats, or shared salad bowls, are placed in the center of the table so that each diner can serve themselves. Bread rolls are served individually or in a shared basket, and are consumed by tearing off small pieces rather than biting into the whole roll. Tableware protocol also includes the management of waste: fish bones, shells, meat bones, and other inedible waste are placed on the edge of the plate, never on the table. Used cutlery that falls on the floor should be reported to the server so that a new one can be provided, rather than picking it up directly from the floor. Contemporary chefs are recognized as artists who combine technical knowledge, creativity, and personal vision to create dishes that not only taste good but also tell stories, evoke emotions, and provoke wonder. Haute gastronomy, in its highest expression, is a temporal form of art that exists only during the moments when it is consumed, creating ephemeral but memorable experiences that leave a lasting impression on memory and the senses. The presentation of dishes has evolved to become an artistic aspect as important as flavor, with chefs studying composition, color, and texture to create visually striking presentations. Molecular gastronomy has demonstrated that the science and art of cooking can be combined to create experiences that challenge our expectations and expand our understanding of what food can be. Chef's gastronomy, where the chef expresses their personal vision through unique dishes, is comparable to painting or music in its individual and creative expression. Michelin-starred or internationally awarded restaurants are recognized not only for the quality of their food but also for their contribution to advancing gastronomy as an artistic discipline. Food photography has emerged as an artistic discipline in its own right, capturing the beauty of dishes and turning them into images that transcend the ephemeral nature of the eating experience. ### 14.2 Basic Culinary Techniques

Mastering basic culinary techniques is the foundation upon which all gastronomic skill is built, for both the home cook and the professional chef. Vegetable cutting is a fundamental skill that includes brunoise (small 3mm cubes), julienne (thin strips), chiffonade (herb ribbons), mirepoix (irregular 2cm pieces), and macedonia cut, each with specific uses depending on the dish. Browning or Maillard reaction, the chemical reaction that occurs when the proteins and sugars in food are heated, is the basis of the toasted and caramelized flavor of meats, breads, and many other foods, and requires adequate temperature and clean surfaces. Slow cooking or braising combines prior browning of the meat with prolonged cooking in liquid at low temperature, producing tender and juicy meats with a rich and concentrated sauce. Sautéing or stir-frying, a technique originating in Asia, uses very high heat and constant movement to cook food quickly while keeping it crispy and fresh. Steaming preserves the nutrients and natural flavors of food, being especially suitable for fish, vegetables, and dumplings. Convection ovens allow uniform and efficient cooking that is ideal for roasting meats, baking breads, and gratinating dishes. Emulsion, the combination of two normally immiscible liquids like oil and water, is the basis of sauces like mayonnaise, vinaigrette, and béchamel, and requires specific technique to prevent it from breaking. Confit, slow cooking in fat at low temperature, produces extremely tender meats like the famous French duck confit. Fermentation, although it is a biological process rather than a direct culinary technique, produces foods like bread, cheese, kimchi, and yogurt that are pillars of world gastronomy. ### 14.3 The Art of Pastry

Pastry is a culinary discipline that combines scientific precision with artistic creativity to create sweets, cakes, breads, and other baked goods that delight the palate and beautify the table. Unlike general cooking, where improvisation and adjustment are common, pastry requires exact measurement of ingredients and precise control of temperatures and times, since small variations can significantly alter the final result. The fundamentals of pastry include handling dough, understanding leavening agents (yeast, baking powder, whipped egg whites), and knowing how different ingredients (flour, sugar, butter, eggs) interact with each other. Sponge cakes, the base of many desserts, require specific techniques like whipping eggs and sugar to achieve a spongy texture, or creaming butter and sugar for a more compact crumb. Shortcrust pastries, like those for tarts, use specific proportions of flour, butter, and water that produce a crumbly and flavorful texture. Pastry creams, like crème pâtissière, buttercream, and ganache, are the base of many fillings and toppings that enrich cakes and tarts. Chocolate, in its different forms (dark, milk, white), requires specific tempering to achieve the correct shine and texture, a technique that once learned is essential for professional pastry. Breads, although often considered a separate discipline, share many principles with pastry: dough handling, fermentation, and oven baking are common aspects that require practice and patience. Cake decoration has emerged as an art in its own right, with techniques like piping, fondant, sugarcraft, and edible painting that allow creating visual masterpieces. ### 14.5 Gastronomic Glossary

The gastronomic vocabulary is extensive and rich, reflecting the complexity and diversity of world culinary culture, and mastering its essential terms allows precise communication in the kitchen and at the restaurant. Cutting terms include brunoise (3mm cubes), julienne (2x2x6cm strips), chiffonade (fine herb ribbons), mirepoix (irregular 2cm pieces), batonnet (6mm sticks), and concassé (peeled, seeded, and diced tomato). Cooking terms range from sauter (sauté over high heat), braiser (slow cooking in liquid), pocher (gentle cooking in liquid), gratiner (browning the surface with direct heat), to confiter (slow cooking in fat). Sauce terms include the mother sauces like béchamel, velouté, espagnole, hollandaise, and tomato, from which numerous secondary sauces derive. Pastry terms include pâte sucrée (sweet dough), pâte brisée (shortcrust dough), pâte feuilletée (puff pastry), choux (dough for profiteroles), and crème pâtissière (pastry cream). Wine terms include bouquet (aroma of aged wine), tannins (substances that give dryness), body (weight of wine in the mouth), acidity (freshness), and aftertaste (persistent flavor after swallowing). Shellfish terms include crustaceans (prawns, langoustines), bivalve mollusks (mussels, clams), gastropod mollusks (snails), and cephalopods (octopus, squid). Meat terms include grade one cut (loin), grade two (chuck), grade three (flank), and cooking specifications like azul (rare), saignant (medium-rare), à point (medium), and bien cuit (well done). Cheese terms vary by region: fromage frais (fresh cheese), camembert (soft cheese), comté (aged cheese), roquefort (blue cheese).
