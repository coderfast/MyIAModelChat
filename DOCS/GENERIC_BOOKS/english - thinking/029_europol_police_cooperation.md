# Europol and Police Cooperation in the EU

## Chapter 1: Foundations of Europol

### The origins and creation of Europol

Europol, the European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation (by its English acronym), is the European agency responsible for facilitating police cooperation between the Member States of the European Union in the fight against international organized crime and terrorism. The origins of Europol date back to the signing of the European Union Convention on Police Cooperation in 1991, which established the European Police Office (Europol) as a police cooperation mechanism between the Member States. The Europol Convention was replaced by the Convention establishing Europol in 1996, which expanded Europol's competencies and strengthened its institutional structure. Europol began its limited operations in 1994 and became a fully operational agency in 1999, with the entry into force of the Europol Convention. Since then, Europol has become a fundamental agency for police cooperation in the European Union, providing investigative support, intelligence analysis, and information exchange facilities to the police authorities of the Member States.

The creation of Europol was motivated by the need to strengthen police cooperation between the Member States to address growing international organized crime, which had benefited from the elimination of internal borders in the Schengen area and the free movement of persons, goods, and services in the internal market. The Member States recognized that fighting international crime required closer and more effective police cooperation, and that Europol could provide the tools and mechanisms necessary to facilitate this cooperation. Europol's mission is to support the Member States in preventing and combating terrorism, illicit drug trafficking, human trafficking, cybercrime, fraud, money laundering, and other forms of international organized crime that affect two or more Member States.

### Europol's competencies

Europol has competencies in a wide range of areas of international organized crime, including terrorism, illicit drug trafficking, human trafficking, cybercrime, fraud, money laundering, document forgery, weapons trafficking, economic and financial crime, and corruption. Europol does not have the authority to investigate crimes directly, but its role is to support the police authorities of the Member States by providing intelligence analysis, information exchange facilities, investigative support, and technical assistance in investigations of international organized crime. Europol also coordinates joint police operations between the Member States, facilitates the exchange of information between the police authorities of the Member States, and provides logistical and technical support for international crime investigations.

Europol's competencies extend to police cooperation with third countries and international organizations. Europol maintains cooperative relationships with the police authorities of numerous non-EU countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Norway, Switzerland, Canada, Australia, and other countries, through cooperation agreements that facilitate the exchange of information and cooperation in investigations of international organized crime. Europol also cooperates with international organizations such as Interpol, the United Nations, and the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE), to promote international police cooperation and to coordinate actions against international organized crime. Europol is a member of the European Judicial Network (Eurojust), which facilitates judicial cooperation between the Member States, and of the European Cybercrime Centre (EC3), which promotes cooperation in the fight against cybercrime.

## Chapter 2: Europol's Institutional Structure

### The Europol Director and executive management

The Europol Director is the agency's highest authority and is responsible for directing and managing Europol's activities, representing Europol before the authorities of the Member States, EU institutions, and international organizations, and implementing the decisions of the Management Board. The Director is appointed by the Europol Management Board, acting by a two-thirds majority of votes, after consulting the European Parliament and the European Commission, for a four-year term renewable once. The Director is responsible for ensuring compliance with Europol's mission and competencies, managing the agency's human and financial resources, maintaining relations with the police authorities of the Member States, EU institutions, and international organizations, and ensuring the quality and relevance of the services provided by Europol to the police authorities of the Member States.

Europol's executive management consists of the Director and several Deputy Directors, who are appointed by the Management Board on the proposal of the Director. The Deputy Directors are responsible for leading Europol's units and departments, coordinating the agency's operational and analytical activities, and supporting the Director in the management and direction of Europol. Europol's organizational structure includes the Directorate for Criminal Investigation, which coordinates Europol's operational activities in the fight against international organized crime; the Directorate for Criminal Analysis, which provides intelligence analysis and analytical support to the police authorities of the Member States; and the Directorate for Operations, which manages Europol's information systems and information exchange facilities. Europol currently has more than 1,000 employees, including analysts, investigators, and administrative staff, and its annual budget exceeds 200 million euros.

### The Management Board and the European Parliament

The Europol Management Board is the agency's decision-making body and is composed of one representative from each Member State, typically the director of the national police or their equivalent, and one representative from the European Commission. The Management Board is responsible for adopting decisions on Europol's strategic and operational priorities, approving the agency's annual budget and work plan, appointing and dismissing the Director and Deputy Directors, and overseeing Europol's activities. The Management Board meets at least twice a year to discuss Europol's priorities and activities, and may hold extraordinary meetings when circumstances require. The Management Board also establishes working groups and committees that advise Europol's executive management on strategic priorities, international cooperation, and budget matters.

The European Parliament plays an important role in overseeing Europol's activities, in accordance with the provisions of the Treaty of Lisbon that establish the European Parliament's accountability towards EU agencies. The European Parliament receives regular information about Europol's activities through the European Parliament's Committee on Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs, which is the committee responsible for overseeing Europol's activities. The European Parliament also approves Europol's annual budget and supervises the agency's financial management. The Europol Director appears before the European Parliament's Committee on Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs to answer MEPs' questions about Europol's activities, and the European Parliament may request additional information about Europol's activities at any time. The European Parliament's oversight ensures Europol's accountability and the transparency of its activities.

## Chapter 3: Europol's Information Systems

### The Europol Information System (EIS)

The Europol Information System (EIS, by its English acronym) is Europol's central database that contains information on criminal organizations, suspects, and crimes of international organized crime. The EIS was established in 1998 and has been improved and expanded several times since then, and is used by the police authorities of the Member States to search for and share information on international organized crime. The EIS contains information on criminal organizations, including their structure, members, activities, and contact networks; on suspects, including their identity, criminal history, links to criminal organizations, and movements; and on crimes, including their nature, location, victims, and known perpetrators. The EIS is accessible to the police authorities of the Member States through the Europol National Units, which are the national contact points that facilitate access to the EIS and the collection of information.

The EIS is subject to strict personal data protection rules, which ensure that the information contained in the system is used in an appropriate and secure manner. The Europol Regulation establishes the personal data protection rules applicable to the processing of personal data by Europol, including the principles of purpose, data minimization, storage limitation, and security. Europol has established a Data Protection Officer, who is responsible for supervising Europol's compliance with personal data protection rules, and a Data Protection Advisory Board, which advises Europol on personal data protection matters. The EIS is also subject to regular audits by the European Data Protection Authority (EDPA), which supervises EU institutions' compliance with personal data protection rules. The strict personal data protection rules ensure that the information contained in the EIS is used in an appropriate and secure manner, and that citizens' fundamental rights are respected.

The SIS was created in 1995 as part of the Schengen Agreement, and has been reformed several times to improve its capabilities and scope. The SIS contains millions of entries, including European arrest warrants, requests for searching missing persons, stolen or lost objects, stolen vehicles, forged identity documents, and persons who pose a threat to public safety. The SIS is accessible to the police, customs, judicial, and migration authorities of all Member States, and provides real-time information that facilitates the identification and location of wanted persons and objects.

The SIS has been reformed to create SIS II, which represents a significant improvement in the system's capabilities, including the incorporation of new types of alerts, improved information access speed, the incorporation of biometric data (fingerprints and photographs), and interoperability with other EU information systems. SIS II has also expanded system access to new categories of users, including migration authorities, asylum authorities, and judicial authorities. SIS II is a fundamental instrument for police cooperation in the European Union, as it enables the authorities of the Member States to share information quickly and efficiently, which facilitates the identification and location of wanted persons and objects and contributes to the security of European citizens.

The SIS has also been the subject of debates on personal data protection, as the system contains sensitive information about persons, including biometric data, health data, and criminal records data. The EU has adopted strict rules for personal data protection in the SIS, including the Directive on the Protection of Personal Data in the SIS, which establishes rules for the processing of personal data in the system, and the General Data Protection Regulation, which establishes general principles of personal data protection in the EU.
