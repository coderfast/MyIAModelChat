Basque pintxos represent one of the most refined and democratized culinary expressions of Spanish cuisine. This tradition, born in the bars of the Basque Country, has transcended borders to become a cultural phenomenon of international dimensions. The pintxo, which derives from the Basque verb "pinchar" (to spear), consists of a small portion of food placed on a slice of bread and held together with a wooden skewer. However, this simple description does not do justice to the complexity and variety found at the counters of Basque pintxo bars. From the classic pintxos of tortilla de patatas, bacalao al pil-pil, or anchovies from Santoña, to the most innovative creations that incorporate haute cuisine techniques, surprising textures, and unexpected combinations, pintxos are a reflection of Basque cuisine in its purest and most accessible form. The culture of txikiteo, or bar-hopping from one pintxo bar to another, is a social experience that goes beyond the simple consumption of food, becoming a ritual that strengthens community bonds and celebrates Basque hospitality.

The Cantabrian coast is home to one of the richest and most productive fishing zones in Europe, providing the northern Spanish cuisine with raw materials of extraordinary quality and variety. Cantabrian seafood includes species as prized as the percebe (goose barnacle), cigala (Langoustine), buey de mar (Norway lobster), centollo (spider crab), joystick, navajas (razor clams), berberechos (cockles), almejas (clams), vieiras (scallops), erizos de mar (sea urchins), and bogavantes (lobsters), each with its own organoleptic and culinary characteristics. The percebe, considered a delicacy par excellence, is collected from the rocks where the waves are most violent, making its harvesting a dangerous activity reserved for the perceiveiros, experts in this risky labor. The cigala, with its firm texture and sweet flavor, is perfect on the grill, in seafood broths, or simply boiled with coarse salt. The buey de mar, with its white and fine flesh, is prepared on the grill, steamed, or in green sauce, while the centollo, with its reddish shell and firm flesh, is the star ingredient of the famous spider crab sauce, emulsified with its own juice, olive oil, and a touch of garlic.

Artisanal fishing remains fundamental for maintaining the quality and sustainability of Cantabrian seafood. Seafood cuisine in the north is characterized by simplicity that respects the natural flavor of the product, using techniques such as steaming, grilling, boiling, or quick sautéing, avoiding preparations that mask the intrinsic qualities of fresh seafood.

Natural cider is much more than a simple fermented drink; it is a symbol of cultural identity, a central element of celebrations, and a reflection of the agricultural tradition of northern Spain. Cider production dates back to Roman times, although it was during the Middle Ages that this drink reached its maximum splendor, becoming the customary drink at both noble and popular tables. The main production areas are in Asturias, the Basque Country, Cantabria, and Galicia, regions where the climatic and soil conditions are optimal for the cultivation of cider apples, known as "manzanas de sidra" or "pomadas." There are over a hundred varieties of cider apples, classified as sweet, acidic, bitter, and mixed, each contributing specific characteristics to the flavor and aroma of the final product. The production process begins with the manual harvesting of apples, which are sorted and blended to achieve the perfect balance of flavors, and continues with pressing, natural fermentation, and aging in oak barrels, a process that can last several months.

The ritual of serving cider is a unique tradition in the world, combining skill, spectacle, and socialization. The escanciador, the person in charge of serving the cider, must pour the liquid from a considerable height, generally between one and two meters, so that the cider is properly aerated and the natural carbonation process is activated. This gesture, known as "escanciar" (to pour), requires practice and coordination, since the stream must fall into the center of the glass (called "zurracapote") without overflowing or wasting a single drop. The poured cider is consumed immediately, in small quantities, and the glass is shared among the diners, each one taking a sip and passing the glass to the next, a gesture that symbolizes trust and camaraderie. Cider houses, establishments specialized in cider service, are social gathering places where life is celebrated, community matters are discussed, and local gastronomy is enjoyed, accompanied by the unmistakable effervescence of freshly poured cider.

Cider has also undergone an evolution in its methods of production and consumption in recent years. Artisanal producers have recovered almost forgotten apple varieties, creating designer ciders with complex and singular flavor profiles. Brut nature ciders, without added sugar, have gained popularity among the most demanding consumers, who seek a pure and authentic product. Ice ciders, made from frozen apples that are pressed in their natural state, offer an extraordinary concentration of flavors. Contemporary cider houses have also emerged that combine tradition with innovation, offering cider pairings with seasonal products, pouring workshops for tourists, and gastronomic experiences that include visits to orchards and guided tastings. Cider, therefore, is not just a drink but a living heritage that adapts to the times without losing its essence, keeping alive the spirit of community and celebration that has accompanied it for centuries.

Gazpacho is much more than a simple cold soup; it is a concentrate of history, culture, and flavor that summarizes the essence of Andalusian cuisine in a glass. This preparation, whose origins date back to Roman and Arab times, has evolved to become the most representative dish of the Andalusian summer, a drink-food that hydrates, nourishes, and refreshes on the hottest days. The fundamental ingredients of gazpacho are ripe tomato, green pepper, cucumber, garlic, extra virgin olive oil, Jerez vinegar, and stale bread, a mixture that is blended until achieving a silky and homogeneous texture. However, the true mastery of gazpacho lies in the balance of its components: the acidity point of the tomato, the freshness of the cucumber, the gentle pungency of the garlic, the richness of the olive oil, and the neutrality of the bread, which thickens and softens the mixture. Gazpacho is served very cold, preferably between 4 and 8 degrees Celsius, and is decorated with shavings of Serrano ham, chopped hard-boiled egg, diced cucumber, pepper, and onion, or even with cooked prawns or canned tuna, depending on the region and the diner's preferences.

The history of gazpacho is linked to the history of Andalusia and Spain. The Romans already prepared a soup based on bread, garlic, oil, and vinegar, which they called "moretum." The Arabs, during their domination of the Iberian Peninsula, introduced the use of syrup and fruits in some preparations, although the gazpacho as we know it today was consolidated in the 17th and 18th centuries, when the tomato, from America, was incorporated into the original recipe. Before the arrival of the tomato, gazpacho was white or green, made exclusively with bread, garlic, oil, and vinegar, and was often flavored with herbs such as oregano or thyme. The evolution of gazpacho reflects the history of human food: adaptation to available ingredients, the influence of cultures that have passed through the region, and the creativity of generations of cooks who have perfected the recipe to reach the current formula. Gazpacho is not just a dish; it is a symbol of Andalusian identity, a social cohesion element shared in families, among friends, at popular festivals, and on any occasion when the heat invites one to refresh with this elixir of the land.

The cocido madrileño has its origins in Andalusian cocido, which in turn derives from the Arabic "kusari," a preparation of chickpeas with meat and vegetables cooked over low heat for hours. The Castilian adaptation of this dish incorporated the ingredients available on the central plateau: Pedrosillo chickpeas, Serrano ham, chorizo, morcilla (blood sausage), chicken, beef, potato, carrot, cabbage, and later, other sausages and meats that enriched the recipe. The cocido madrileño is traditionally served in clay pots, a material that retains heat evenly and imparts an earthy flavor to the stew, and is consumed during the coldest months of the year as a comforting and nutritious food that warms the body and spirit.
