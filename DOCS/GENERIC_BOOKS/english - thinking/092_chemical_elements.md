# Chemical Elements

## Chapter 1: Introduction to Chemistry

### 1.1 Chemistry as a Science

Chemistry is the science that studies the composition, structure, properties, and changes of matter. It is one of the most fundamental sciences, as it allows us to understand how the materials around us behave, from the air we breathe to the food we consume, including the medicines that cure diseases and the materials that build our cities. Chemistry is closely related to other sciences such as physics, biology, and geology, forming part of a network of knowledge that explains how the universe works.

The origin of chemistry dates back to the first civilizations, which already used chemical processes such as ceramics, metallurgy, and fermentation. The alchemists of the Middle Ages, although they pursued erroneous goals like the philosopher's stone and the elixir of longevity, laid the foundations of modern chemistry by developing experimental techniques such as distillation, sublimation, and crystallization. Modern chemistry was born in the 17th century with the work of Robert Boyle, who defined the chemical element as a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances.

Modern chemistry is divided into multiple branches, such as inorganic chemistry (which studies elements and their inorganic compounds), organic chemistry (which studies carbon compounds), physical chemistry (which applies physical principles to chemical processes), analytical chemistry (which develops methods to identify and quantify substances), and biochemistry (which studies chemical processes in living organisms). Each of these branches contributes to our understanding of matter and its transformations.

### 1.2 Matter and Its States

Matter is everything that has mass and occupies space. Matter is composed of atoms, which are the basic units of chemical elements. Atoms, in turn, are composed of subatomic particles: protons (with a positive charge), neutrons (no charge), and electrons (with a negative charge). The number of protons in an atom's nucleus determines its chemical identity, and is known as the atomic number.

Matter exists in three fundamental states: solid, liquid, and gaseous. In the solid state, particles are closely packed and can only vibrate around their fixed positions, which gives the solid a defined shape and volume. In the liquid state, particles can slide past each other, allowing the liquid to take the shape of its container while maintaining a constant volume. In the gaseous state, particles move freely at high speed, filling all available volume.

In addition to these three fundamental states, there are other states of matter such as plasma (an ionized gas found in stars and lightning), liquid crystals (which have intermediate properties between solids and liquids), and superfluids (which flow without resistance at low temperature). Phase changes of matter, such as melting (solid to liquid), vaporization (liquid to gas), solidification (liquid to solid), condensation (gas to liquid), sublimation (solid to gas), and deposition (gas to solid), are physical processes that do not alter the chemical composition of the substance.

### 1.3 Chemical Elements: Definition and Classification

A chemical element is a pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical methods. Elements are the basic constituents of all matter in the universe, and each element is formed by atoms with the same number of protons. Currently, 118 elements are known, of which 94 are found naturally on Earth and the rest have been artificially synthesized in laboratories.

Chemical elements are classified into several categories based on their properties. Metals are elements that are good conductors of heat and electricity, have a metallic luster, are malleable and ductile, and tend to lose electrons in their chemical reactions. Nonmetals are elements that are poor conductors, have no metallic luster, are brittle, and tend to gain electrons in their chemical reactions. Metalloids (or semimetals) have intermediate properties between metals and nonmetals, and are semiconductors.

Elements are also classified according to their abundance in nature. Abundant elements are those found in large quantities in the Earth's crust, such as oxygen, silicon, aluminum, iron, and calcium. Rare elements are those found in small quantities, such as gold, silver, platinum, and the rare earth elements. Some elements only exist in artificial form, created in particle accelerators or nuclear reactors, and have very short half-lives.

### 1.4 The Periodic Table of Elements

The periodic table is a systematic organization of chemical elements according to their atomic number, electron configuration, and recurring chemical properties. It was created by Dmitri Mendeleev in 1869, who arranged elements according to their atomic mass and predicted the existence of elements not yet discovered. The modern periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number and organizes them into periods (rows) and groups (columns).

The groups of the periodic table bring together elements with similar chemical properties. For example, Group 1 (alkali metals) includes lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, cesium, and francium, which are highly reactive metals that easily lose one electron. Group 2 (alkaline earth metals) includes beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium, and radium, which are less reactive metals than the alkali metals. Group 17 (halogens) includes fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, and astatine, which are highly reactive nonmetals that easily gain one electron.

The periods of the periodic table indicate the highest energy level occupied by the electrons of the elements in that period. As you move from left to right in a period, elements lose metallic character and gain nonmetallic character. The periodic table also includes the lanthanides and actinides (the inner transition elements), which are found at the bottom of the table. The periodic table is a fundamental tool in chemistry, as it allows us to predict the properties of elements and their behavior in chemical reactions.

## Chapter 2: Metals

### 2.1 Alkali Metals

Alkali metals form Group 1 of the periodic table and include lithium (Li), sodium (Na), potassium (K), rubidium (Rb), cesium (Cs), and francium (Fr). They are all soft, silvery metals with low melting points and excellent electrical conductivity. They are the least dense metals and have the lowest melting points of all metals. Their chemical reactivity increases as you go down the group, with francium being the most reactive, although it is extremely rare and radioactive.

Alkali metals react violently with water, producing gaseous hydrogen and an alkaline metal hydroxide. This reaction is so exothermic that the hydrogen produced can ignite, especially with heavier metals like potassium, rubidium, and cesium. Sodium, for example, reacts vigorously with water, producing a ball of molten sodium that moves across the water's surface. Potassium reacts even more violently, producing a characteristic purple flame.

Lithium is the lightest metal and has the lowest density of all metals. It is used in rechargeable batteries (lithium-ion batteries), in the treatment of bipolar disorder, and in alloys with aluminum and magnesium. Sodium is essential for human life and is found in blood serum. It is used in the lighting industry (sodium vapor lamps) and as a coolant in nuclear reactors. Potassium is an essential nutrient for plants and humans, and plays a fundamental role in the transmission of nerve impulses.

### 2.2 Alkaline Earth Metals

Alkaline earth metals form Group 2 of the periodic table and include beryllium (Be), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), strontium (Sr), barium (Ba), and radium (Ra). They are silvery metals, harder and denser than alkali metals, with higher melting points. They are also good conductors of heat and electricity, and react with water, although less violently than alkali metals.

Beryllium is a lightweight, strong metal with a high melting point. It is used in the aerospace industry, in the manufacturing of X-ray windows, and as a moderator in nuclear reactors. However, beryllium is toxic and can cause berylliosis, a chronic lung disease. Magnesium is the eighth most abundant element in the Earth's crust and is essential for life. It is used in alloys with aluminum (which are lightweight and strong), in the manufacturing of fireworks, as an antacid in medicine, and as a nutritional supplement.

Calcium is the fifth most abundant element in the Earth's crust and is essential for life. It is found in bones and teeth (as hydroxyapatite), in limestone, gypsum, and marble. Calcium is used in construction, in the manufacturing of cement and lime, and as a nutritional supplement. Strontium is known for producing a characteristic red flame and is used in fireworks and in the manufacturing of television glass. Barium is used in medicine as a radiological contrast agent (barium sulfate) and in oil well drilling. Radium, discovered by Marie and Pierre Curie, is a radioactive element that was used in luminous paint and in the treatment of cancer, although its use has decreased due to its dangers.

### 2.3 Transition Metals

Transition metals form Groups 3 through 12 of the periodic table and include elements such as scandium (Sc), titanium (Ti), vanadium (V), chromium (Cr), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), and all other metals in the periodic table up to zinc. They are hard metals with high melting and boiling points, good conductors of heat and electricity, and can form compounds with different oxidation states, giving them a wide variety of colors.

Iron is the most used metal in the world and the fourth most abundant element in the Earth's crust. It is found in hemoglobin in blood, which transports oxygen to tissues, and is used in the manufacturing of steel, which is the most important alloy in modern industry. Steel is used in construction, vehicle manufacturing, tools, machinery, and numerous everyday products. Iron is also essential for life, as it is part of hemoglobin and myoglobin.

Copper is one of the metals used by humanity since ancient times. It is an excellent electrical conductor and is used in the manufacturing of electrical wires, pipes, coins, and numerous electronic devices. Copper is also essential for life, as it is part of important enzymes like cytochrome c oxidase. Titanium is a lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and biocompatible metal, so it is used in the manufacturing of orthopedic implants, aircraft, and sports equipment. Chromium is used in the manufacturing of stainless steel (which resists corrosion) and in colored pigments. Nickel is used in the manufacturing of coins, rechargeable batteries, and stainless steel. Zinc is essential for life and is used in galvanization (protecting iron from corrosion), in the manufacturing of batteries, and in nutritional supplements.

### 2.4 F-Block Metals: Lanthanides and Actinides

The lanthanides and actinides form the inner part of the periodic table and are known as inner transition metals. The lanthanides, from lanthanum (La) to lutetium (Lu), are soft, silvery, reactive metals found in nature in minerals such as monazite and bastnasite. They are commonly called "rare earths" because, although they are not as scarce as their name suggests, they are difficult to separate from each other due to their similar chemical properties.

The lanthanides have multiple industrial applications. Cerium (Ce) is used in catalysts for vehicles, in the manufacturing of glass, and in lighters. Neodymium (Nd) is used in the manufacturing of high-strength permanent magnets, which are essential for wind turbines, electric vehicles, and electronic devices. Europium (Eu) is used in television and computer screens (as a red phosphor). Dysprosium (Dy) is used in permanent magnets and in nuclear reactors.

The actinides, from actinium (Ac) to lawrencium (Lr), are heavy and radioactive metals. Uranium (U) is the heaviest naturally occurring element and is used as fuel in nuclear reactors and in the manufacturing of nuclear weapons. Plutonium (Pu) is a transuranic element produced in nuclear reactors and also used in nuclear weapons and as fuel in space missions. All actinides are radioactive and most have short half-lives, which limits their practical applications.

## Chapter 3: Nonmetals

### 3.1 Halogens

Halogens form Group 17 of the periodic table and include fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br), iodine (I), and astatine (At). They are highly reactive nonmetals that tend to gain one electron to form negatively charged ions (halides). The reactivity of halogens decreases as you go down the group, with fluorine being the most reactive and astatine the least reactive (as well as being radioactive).

Fluorine is the most reactive element in the periodic table and can react with almost all elements, including some noble gases. It is used in the fluoridation of drinking water (to prevent tooth decay), in the manufacturing of Teflon (polytetrafluoroethylene), in refrigerants (chlorofluorocarbons, although these have been banned due to their effect on the ozone layer), and in aluminum production. Chlorine is a yellow-green gas with a characteristic irritating odor. It is used in the disinfection of drinking water, in the manufacturing of chemicals (such as PVC, solvents, and pesticides), and in paper production.

Bromine is one of the few elements that is liquid at room temperature, along with mercury and gallium. It is a reddish-brown liquid with a penetrating odor. It is used in the manufacturing of flame retardants (which prevent the spread of fire), in pesticides, and in medicine. Iodine is a shiny black solid that easily sublimes, forming a violet vapor. It is essential for life, as it is part of thyroid hormones. It is used in wound disinfection, as a radiological contrast agent, and in the manufacturing of dyes. Astatine is an extremely rare and radioactive element, with a half-life of only 8.1 hours for its most stable isotope. It is believed that only about 25 grams of astatine exist in the entire Earth's crust.

### 3.2 Noble Gases

Noble gases form Group 18 of the periodic table and include helium (He), neon (Ne), argon (Ar), krypton (Kr), xenon (Xe), and radon (Rn). They are colorless, odorless, monatomic, and extremely unreactive gases, which earned them the name "inert gases" or "noble gases." Their low reactivity is due to having a complete outer electron shell, which makes them chemically very stable.

Helium is the second most abundant element in the universe (after hydrogen) and is found mainly in the sun and stars. It is a colorless, odorless, and non-flammable gas. It is used in hot air balloons, in the cooling of superconducting magnets (such as those in MRI scanners), in welding, and in leak detection. Neon is known for its characteristic reddish-orange color when in electric discharge tubes. It is used in neon signs and in electronic indicators.

Argon is the third most abundant gas in the Earth's atmosphere, after nitrogen and oxygen. It is a colorless and odorless gas used in welding (to protect metal from oxidation), in glass manufacturing (where it replaces nitrogen in stainless steel production), and in food packaging. Krypton is used in the manufacturing of low-energy light bulbs and in neon signs. Xenon is used in high-end vehicle headlights, in lasers, and as an anesthetic. Radon is a radioactive gas produced by the decay of radium in the Earth's crust and is the leading cause of lung cancer not related to smoking.

### 3.3 Oxygen and Sulfur

Oxygen (O) is the most abundant element in the Earth's crust and the second most abundant in the atmosphere (after nitrogen). It is a colorless and odorless gas that is essential for the respiration of nearly all living organisms. Oxygen is part of water (H2O), the atmosphere (O2), and numerous organic and inorganic compounds. It is used in medicine (for oxygen therapy), in welding (oxyacetylene), in metallurgy, and in steel production.

Oxygen has two main allotropes: molecular oxygen (O2) and ozone (O3). Molecular oxygen is the most common form and is what we breathe. Ozone is an unstable form of oxygen found in the ozone layer of the atmosphere, where it protects the Earth from the sun's ultraviolet radiation. Ozone is also used in water disinfection and in industrial cleaning.

Sulfur (S) is a yellow nonmetal found in nature in its native form and in compounds such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S), iron sulfide (pyrite), and calcium sulfate (gypsum). Sulfur is essential for life, as it is part of amino acids such as cysteine and methionine. It is used in the manufacturing of sulfuric acid (the world's most produced chemical), in rubber vulcanization, in fungicides, and in medicine (sulfur cream for treating skin diseases).

### 3.4 Carbon, Nitrogen, and Phosphorus

Carbon (C) is the fundamental element of life, as it forms the basis of all organic compounds (proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, DNA, RNA). Carbon has the unique ability to form stable bonds with other carbon atoms, creating long and complex chains that are the basis of organic chemistry. Carbon exists in nature in allotropes such as diamond (the hardest natural material), graphite (used in pencils and lubricants), graphene (a two-dimensional material with extraordinary properties), and fullerenes (spherical carbon molecules).

Nitrogen (N) is the most abundant element in the Earth's atmosphere (78% of air). Molecular nitrogen (N2) is an inert gas that does not react easily, but nitrogen compounds are essential for life. Nitrogen is part of proteins, nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), and many other biological compounds. It is used in the manufacturing of fertilizers (ammonia, nitrates), explosives (nitroglycerin, TNT), refrigerants, and in food preservation (modified atmosphere).

Phosphorus (P) is an essential nonmetal for life, as it is part of DNA, RNA, ATP (the cell energy molecule), and bones and teeth (as calcium hydroxyapatite). Phosphorus is found in nature in compounds such as apatite and monazite. It is used in the manufacturing of fertilizers, detergents, matches (hence its name), phosphoric acid, and synthetic nucleic acids.

## Chapter 4: Metalloids and Special Elements

### 4.1 Metalloids

Metalloids (or semimetals) are elements that have intermediate properties between metals and nonmetals. The main metalloids are boron (B), silicon (Si), germanium (Ge), arsenic (As), antimony (Sb), and tellurium (Te). They are all semiconductors, meaning their electrical conductivity is intermediate between that of metals and nonmetals, and can be easily modified through the introduction of impurities (a process known as doping).

Boron (B) is a hard, black nonmetal found in nature in the form of borates (such as borax). It is used in the manufacturing of borosilicate glass (Pyrex), detergents, ceramic glazes, and as an additive in rocket fuel. Silicon (Si) is the second most abundant element in the Earth's crust (after oxygen) and is the foundation of the electronics industry. Silicon semiconductors are the basis of electronic chips, solar cells, and transistors. Silicon is also used in the manufacturing of silicone (an elastic polymer) and in construction (as an additive in cement).

Germanium (Ge) was one of the first materials used in transistor manufacturing, although it has largely been replaced by silicon. It is used in optics (infrared lenses), in fiber optics, and in the manufacturing of conductive polymers. Arsenic (As) is a toxic nonmetal that has historically been used as poison and as a pesticide. Today it is used in the manufacturing of gallium arsenide (GaAs) semiconductors, which are used in high-speed devices such as mobile phones and satellite communications. Antimony (Sb) is used in the manufacturing of flame retardants, lead-acid batteries, and solders. Tellurium (Te) is used in cadmium telluride solar cells and as an alloy with steel.

### 4.2 Post-Zinc Transition Metals

Post-zinc transition metals include elements such as gallium (Ga), indium (In), tin (Sn), thallium (Tl), lead (Pb), and bismuth (Bi). These metals have relatively low melting points and are found in nature in compounds such as sulfides, oxides, and silicates. Many of them have been used by humanity since ancient times.

Gallium (Ga) is a soft, silvery metal that melts at only 29.76 degrees Celsius, meaning it melts in your hand. It is used in the manufacturing of gallium arsenide (GaAs) semiconductors, in light-emitting diodes (LEDs), and in touch screens. Indium (In) is a soft, silvery metal used in the manufacturing of liquid crystal displays (LCDs), in solders, and in low-melting-point alloys. Tin (Sn) is a silvery, malleable metal used in the manufacturing of tinplate (the coating of steel in food cans), in solders, and in the manufacturing of bronze (an alloy of tin and copper).

Thallium (Tl) is a toxic metal that has historically been used as a rat poison and as a poison. Today it is used in optics (infrared lenses) and in semiconductor manufacturing. Lead (Pb) is a heavy, toxic metal that has been used by humanity since ancient times. It has been used in plumbing (hence its English name, "plumbing"), lead-acid batteries, ammunition, pigments, and as an additive in gasoline (tetraethyl lead, although this use has been banned in most countries due to its toxicity). Bismuth (Bi) is a heavy metal with the lowest thermal conductivity coefficient of all metals. It is used in the manufacturing of medicines (such as bismuth subsalicylate for stomach problems), in cosmetics, and in low-melting-point alloys.

### 4.3 Synthetic Elements

Synthetic elements are those that do not occur naturally on Earth and have been artificially created in laboratories. These elements are obtained by bombarding atomic nuclei with high-energy particles in particle accelerators or nuclear reactors. Most of these elements are highly unstable and have half-lives ranging from fractions of a second to a few minutes, which limits their practical applications.

The synthetic elements include the elements of period 7 starting from francium (element 87) and all elements of period 8. The most recently discovered and named element is oganesson (Og, element 118), which was synthesized in 2002 and named in honor of Yuri Oganessian, a pioneer of nuclear physics. Other notable synthetic elements include technetium (Tc, element 43), which was the first synthesized element (1937) and is used in nuclear medicine; promethium (Pm, element 61), which is used in luminous paints and atomic batteries; and superheavy elements such as dubnium (Db, 105), seaborgium (Sg, 106), bohrium (Bh, 107), hassium (Hs, 108), meitnerium (Mt, 109), darmstadtium (Ds, 110), roentgenium (Rg, 111), copernicium (Cn, 112), nihonium (Nh, 113), flerovium (Fl, 114), moscovium (Mc, 115), livermorium (Lv, 116), tennessine (Ts, 117), and oganesson (Og, 118).

The synthesis of superheavy elements is an enormous technical challenge, as it requires the collision of very heavy atomic nuclei with precise energy so that they fuse and form a new element. The half-life of these elements is extremely short, which makes their study difficult. However, research on superheavy elements is important for understanding the limits of nuclear stability and for testing theoretical models about the structure of the atomic nucleus.

### 4.4 Rare Elements and Rare Earths

Rare elements are those found in small quantities in the Earth's crust and whose extraction is difficult and costly. Rare earths are a group of 17 elements that include the 15 lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium. Despite their name, rare earths are not as scarce as other elements, but their extraction and purification are technically difficult and costly, as the 17 elements have very similar chemical properties and are difficult to separate from each other.

Rare earths are essential for multiple modern technologies. Neodymium (Nd) is used in the manufacturing of high-strength permanent magnets, which are essential for wind turbines, electric vehicles, electronic devices, and industrial machinery. Dysprosium (Dy) is added to neodymium magnets to improve their resistance to high temperatures. Europium (Eu) is used as a red phosphor in television and computer screens. Terbium (Tb) is used as a green phosphor in screens and fluorescent lamps. Lanthanum (La) is used in the manufacturing of high-quality optical glass and in vehicle catalysts.

China is the world's largest producer of rare earths, with approximately 60% of world production and more than 80% of known reserves. This market dominance has raised geopolitical concerns, as rare earths are essential for multiple strategic industries. Countries like the United States, Australia, Brazil, and several African countries are developing new rare earth deposits to reduce their dependence on China. The recycling of rare earths from electronic waste is also an active field of research, as it could provide an additional source of these critical elements.

## Chapter 5: Chemical Bonds

### 5.1 Ionic Bond

The ionic or electrovalent bond is a type of chemical bond that forms when an atom donates one or more electrons to another atom, creating oppositely charged ions that attract each other electrostatically. This type of bond generally occurs between metals and nonmetals, as metals tend to lose electrons (forming positive ions or cations) and nonmetals tend to gain electrons (forming negative ions or anions).

The most classic example of an ionic bond is sodium chloride (NaCl, commonly known as table salt). Sodium (Na) has one electron in its valence shell, which it easily loses to form the sodium ion (Na+). Chlorine (Cl) has seven electrons in its valence shell and needs to gain one more to complete its octet, forming the chloride ion (Cl-). The electrostatic attraction between the positive sodium ion and the negative chloride ion forms the ionic bond that holds the two particles together.

Ionic compounds have specific characteristics: they are solids at room temperature with high melting and boiling points, they are brittle and break when subjected to stress, they dissolve in water and other polar solvents, and they conduct electricity when dissolved or melted. Ionic crystals have a regular and ordered structure, with ions arranged in a crystal lattice that maximizes electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions and minimizes repulsion between like-charged ions.

### 5.2 Covalent Bond

The covalent bond is a type of chemical bond that forms when two or more atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. This type of bond generally occurs between nonmetals, as they tend to gain electrons rather than lose them. The sharing of electrons allows each atom to achieve a stable electron configuration (usually with eight electrons in its valence shell, known as the octet rule).

Covalent bonds can be single (one shared electron pair, as in H2), double (two shared electron pairs, as in O2), or triple (three shared electron pairs, as in N2). The strength of the covalent bond depends on the distance between the atomic nuclei and the number of shared electrons. Polar covalent bonds form when the shared electrons are shifted toward one of the atoms (the more electronegative one), creating a partial charge separation. Nonpolar covalent bonds form when the shared electrons are distributed equally among the atoms.

Covalent compounds can exist as individual molecules (such as water, H2O, or carbon dioxide, CO2) or as extended covalent networks (such as diamond or quartz). Covalent molecules generally have low melting and boiling points, because the intermolecular forces holding them together are weak compared to the covalent bonds within the molecules. Covalent compounds do not conduct electricity when pure, as they have no free ions.

### 5.3 Metallic Bond

The metallic bond is a type of chemical bond that occurs in metals and their alloys. In the metallic bond, the valence electrons of the metal atoms are not associated with any particular atom, but move freely throughout the entire crystal structure, forming what is known as a "sea of electrons." The positive metal ions (atomic nuclei without their valence electrons) are immersed in this sea of electrons and are held together by electrostatic attraction with the free electrons.

The electron sea model explains many of the characteristic properties of metals. Metals are good conductors of electricity because the free electrons can move easily through the material when an electric field is applied. Metals are good conductors of heat because thermal energy can be transferred rapidly through the vibrations of the crystal lattice and the movement of free electrons. Metals are malleable and ductile because the metal ions can slide past each other without breaking the metallic bond, as the free electrons adapt to the new positions.

The strength of the metallic bond varies according to the number of available valence electrons and the size of the atoms. Metals with more valence electrons and smaller atoms have stronger metallic bonds and, therefore, higher melting points and hardness. For example, tungsten (W), with six valence electrons and a relatively small atom, has the highest melting point of all metals (3,422 degrees Celsius). Mercury (Hg), on the other hand, has a very weak metallic bond and is liquid at room temperature.

### 5.4 Intermolecular Bonds and Van der Waals Forces

In addition to the primary chemical bonds (ionic, covalent, and metallic), there are weaker interactions known as intermolecular bonds or Van der Waals forces. These forces are responsible for many of the physical properties of substances, such as their melting and boiling points, their solubility, and their behavior in different states of matter.

Van der Waals forces include several types of interactions. London dispersion forces are weak attractive forces that occur between all molecules, regardless of their polarity. These forces arise from momentary fluctuations in a molecule's electron distribution, which create instantaneous dipoles that induce dipoles in neighboring molecules. Dipole-dipole forces occur between polar molecules, where the positive and negative ends of the molecules attract each other. The hydrogen bond is a special type of dipole-dipole interaction that occurs when a hydrogen atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (such as oxygen, nitrogen, or fluorine) is attracted to a lone pair of electrons on a nearby electronegative atom.

The hydrogen bond is particularly important in water, where each water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds with neighboring molecules. These hydrogen bonds are responsible for many of the abnormal properties of water, such as its high boiling point (100 degrees Celsius compared to -60 degrees Celsius for H2S), its high heat capacity, its lower density as a solid than as a liquid (ice floats), and its ability to dissolve a wide variety of polar substances. Hydrogen bonds are also fundamental to the structure of DNA, where they hold together the two strands of the double helix, and to the structure of proteins, where they help maintain their three-dimensional shape.

## Chapter 6: Chemical Reactions

### 6.1 Definition and Types of Chemical Reactions

A chemical reaction is a process in which one or more substances (reactants) are transformed into one or more new substances (products) through the breaking and forming of chemical bonds. Chemical reactions differ from physical changes in that they produce new substances with different properties from those of the reactants. Signs that a chemical reaction has taken place include color changes, gas production, precipitate formation, temperature changes, and light production.

Chemical reactions are classified into several types based on how the reactants combine. Synthesis (or combination) reactions are those in which two or more reactants combine to form a single product: A + B -> AB. Examples include the formation of water from hydrogen and oxygen (2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O) and the formation of sodium chloride from sodium and chlorine (2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl). Decomposition reactions are those in which a compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances: AB -> A + B. Examples include the electrolysis of water (2H2O -> 2H2 + O2) and the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate (CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2).

Single replacement (or displacement) reactions are those in which one element replaces another in a compound: A + BC -> AC + B. Examples include the reaction of zinc with hydrochloric acid (Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2) and the reaction of iron with copper sulfate (Fe + CuSO4 -> FeSO4 + Cu). Double replacement (or exchange) reactions are those in which two compounds exchange ions: AB + CD -> AD + CB. Examples include the reaction between silver nitrate and sodium chloride (AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3) and the neutralization between hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide (HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O). Combustion reactions are those in which a substance reacts with oxygen, generally producing carbon dioxide and water.

### 6.2 Energy in Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions are accompanied by energy changes, which can manifest as heat, light, sound, or changes in pressure. Exothermic reactions are those that release energy to the surroundings, generally in the form of heat. In these reactions, the energy of the products is less than that of the reactants, and the difference is released as heat. Examples of exothermic reactions include the combustion of fuels (such as gasoline or natural gas), the reaction between acids and bases (neutralization), and the oxidation of metals.

Endothermic reactions are those that absorb energy from the surroundings, generally in the form of heat. In these reactions, the energy of the products is greater than that of the reactants, and the absorbed energy is stored in the chemical bonds of the products. Examples of endothermic reactions include the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate, the dissolution of ammonium nitrate in water (used in cold packs), and photosynthesis (which absorbs light energy from the sun).

The enthalpy of reaction (delta H) is the amount of energy absorbed or released during a chemical reaction at constant pressure. If delta H is negative, the reaction is exothermic; if delta H is positive, the reaction is endothermic. The activation energy is the minimum energy required for a chemical reaction to occur. Even in exothermic reactions, an initial amount of energy is needed to break the bonds of the reactants before the bonds of the products can form. Catalysts are substances that reduce the activation energy of a reaction without being consumed in it, allowing the reaction to proceed more quickly.

### 6.3 Reaction Rate and Factors Affecting It

The reaction rate is the speed at which reactants are consumed or products are formed in a chemical reaction. It can be measured as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit of time. The reaction rate depends on several factors such as the concentration of the reactants, the temperature, the pressure (in gaseous reactions), the surface area (in heterogeneous reactions), the presence of catalysts, and the nature of the reactants.

The concentration of reactants affects the reaction rate because at higher concentrations, there are more reactant particles per unit volume, which increases the probability of effective collisions between them. The rate law states that the rate of a reaction is proportional to the concentration of the reactants raised to a power (the order of reaction). For example, in a first-order reaction, the rate is directly proportional to the concentration of the reactant; in a second-order reaction, the rate is proportional to the square of the concentration.

Temperature affects the reaction rate because as temperature increases, particles move faster and with more energy, which increases the frequency and energy of collisions between them. The empirical Arrhenius rule states that the rate of a reaction doubles for every 10 degrees Celsius increase in temperature. Catalysts increase the reaction rate by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. Catalysts are widely used in the chemical industry, such as iron in the Haber-Bosch process for ammonia synthesis, platinum in vehicle catalysts, and enzymes in biological processes.

### 6.4 Redox Reactions and Electrochemistry

Oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions are reactions in which there is a transfer of electrons between chemical species. Oxidation is the loss of electrons by a chemical species, and reduction is the gain of electrons by another. In every redox reaction, the oxidation of one species is always accompanied by the reduction of another, as the electrons lost by one species are gained by another.

Redox reactions are fundamental in nature and in technology. Combustion is a redox reaction in which a fuel (which is oxidized) reacts with oxygen (which is reduced). The corrosion of metals is a redox reaction in which the metal is oxidized by the action of oxygen and water. Cellular respiration is a series of redox reactions in which glucose is oxidized and oxygen is reduced, producing energy usable by the cell. Photosynthesis is the reverse redox reaction, in which carbon dioxide is reduced and water is oxidized, using the energy of sunlight.

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies redox reactions that produce or use electrical energy. Galvanic cells (or batteries) are devices that convert chemical energy into electrical energy through spontaneous redox reactions. Electrolytic cells use electrical energy to drive non-spontaneous redox reactions. Electroplating is an electrochemical process that uses electrodeposition to coat objects with a thin layer of metal (such as chrome plating or silver plating). Corrosion is an electrochemical process that can be prevented through techniques such as galvanization (coating with zinc), painting, corrosion inhibitors, and cathodic protection.

## Chapter 7: Chemical Equations

### 7.1 Chemical Symbolism and Notation

Chemical symbolism is a system for representing elements, compounds, and chemical reactions using standardized symbols and notations. Each chemical element is represented by a one- or two-letter symbol, where the first letter is always capitalized and, if there is a second letter, it is lowercase. For example, H represents hydrogen, O represents oxygen, Na represents sodium (from the Latin natrium), Fe represents iron (from the Latin ferrum), and Au represents gold (from the Latin aurum).

Chemical compounds are represented by chemical formulas, which indicate the elements that make up the compound and the number of atoms of each. Empirical formulas show the simplest ratio of atoms in a compound (for example, CH2O for glucose). Molecular formulas show the actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule of the compound (for example, C6H12O6 for glucose). Structural formulas show the arrangement of atoms in the molecule, indicating the bonds between them.

Chemical equations represent chemical reactions using chemical formulas. A balanced chemical equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides of the equation, reflecting the conservation of mass during the reaction. Reactants are written to the left of an arrow indicating the direction of the reaction, and products to the right. Stoichiometric coefficients indicate the molar proportions of reactants and products in the reaction. For example, the balanced equation for the combustion of methane is: CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O.

### 7.2 Stoichiometric Calculations

Stoichiometry is the part of chemistry that studies the quantitative relationships between reactants and products in a chemical reaction. Stoichiometric calculations are based on the conservation of mass (the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products) and the law of definite proportions (a compound always contains the same elements in the same proportions).

To perform stoichiometric calculations, it is necessary to know the molecular mass of the compounds involved. The molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in a molecule of the compound. For example, the molecular mass of water (H2O) is 2(1) + 16 = 18 g/mol, since hydrogen has an atomic mass of 1 u and oxygen of 16 u. Moles are the unit of measurement for the amount of substance, and one mole contains 6.022 x 10^23 particles (Avogadro's number).

Stoichiometric calculations allow us to determine the amount of reactant needed to produce a given amount of product, or the amount of product obtained from a known amount of reactant. For example, in the reaction N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3 (the Haber-Bosch process for ammonia synthesis), if you have 28 g of nitrogen (1 mole), you need 6 g of hydrogen (3 moles) and you obtain 34 g of ammonia (2 moles). Excess and limiting problems are used to determine which reactant is consumed first (the limiting reactant) and which remains in excess.

### 7.3 Solutions and Concentration

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute (the substance being dissolved) in a solvent (the substance doing the dissolving). The concentration of a solution is the amount of solute present per unit volume of solution. There are several units of concentration measurement, such as molarity (moles of solute per liter of solution), molality (moles of solute per kilogram of solvent), normality (equivalents of solute per liter of solution), and mass percent (grams of solute per 100 grams of solution).

Solutions are classified by concentration as concentrated solutions (high solute proportion) and dilute solutions (low solute proportion). Dilution is the process of reducing the concentration of a solution by adding more solvent. The dilution law states that the amount of solute remains constant during dilution: M1V1 = M2V2, where M is molarity and V is volume. Buffer solutions are solutions that resist changes in pH when acids or bases are added, and are important in analytical chemistry, biochemistry, and medicine.

The dissolution process can be exothermic or endothermic, depending on the intermolecular forces involved. The dissolution of ionic solids in water (such as table salt) is usually exothermic, because the hydration energy of the ions is greater than the energy needed to break the crystal lattice. The dissolution of covalent solids in water can be endothermic (such as the dissolution of ammonium nitrate) or exothermic (such as the dissolution of sulfuric acid). The solubility of a solute in a solvent depends on temperature, pressure (for gases), and the nature of the solute and solvent (the "like dissolves like" rule).

### 7.4 Acids, Bases, and pH

Acids are substances that donate protons (H+ ions) in solution, according to the Brønsted-Lowry definition. Strong acids, such as hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and nitric acid (HNO3), dissociate completely in water, donating all their protons. Weak acids, such as acetic acid (CH3COOH) and carbonic acid (H2CO3), only partially dissociate in water. Acids have a sour taste, change the color of litmus indicator to red, and react with metals to produce hydrogen gas.

Bases are substances that accept protons in solution, according to the Brønsted-Lowry definition. Strong bases, such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and potassium hydroxide (KOH), dissociate completely in water, producing OH- ions. Weak bases, such as ammonia (NH3), only partially dissociate. Bases have a bitter taste, a soapy texture, change the color of litmus indicator to blue, and react with acids to form salts and water (neutralization).

The pH scale is a measure of the acidity or alkalinity of a solution, ranging from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral (equal concentration of H+ and OH- ions). A pH below 7 indicates acidity (higher concentration of H+ ions), and a pH above 7 indicates alkalinity (higher concentration of OH- ions). The pH scale is logarithmic, meaning each unit change in pH represents a tenfold change in H+ ion concentration. pH is a fundamental parameter in chemistry, biology, medicine, agriculture, and the environment, as it affects the solubility of substances, enzyme activity, organism health, and many industrial processes.

## Chapter 8: States of Matter

### 8.1 The Solid State

The solid state is one of the three fundamental states of matter. In the solid state, particles (atoms, molecules, or ions) are closely packed and can only vibrate around their fixed positions in the crystal lattice. This ordered arrangement of particles gives the solid a defined shape and volume, and resistance to deformation. Solids can be crystalline (with an ordered, periodic structure) or amorphous (with a disordered structure).

Crystalline solids are classified according to the nature of the forces that hold the particles together in the crystal lattice. Molecular crystals are formed by molecules held together by Van der Waals forces or hydrogen bonds (such as ice, sugar, and naphthalene). Ionic crystals are formed by ions held together by ionic bonds (such as table salt, sodium chloride). Covalent crystals (or covalent networks) are formed by atoms held together by covalent bonds in an extended network (such as diamond, quartz, and graphene). Metallic crystals are formed by metal ions immersed in a sea of electrons (such as iron, copper, and gold).

The properties of solids depend on their crystal structure and the nature of the intermolecular forces. The melting point of a solid is the temperature at which the thermal energy is sufficient to overcome the forces holding the particles together in the crystal lattice. Solids with strong intermolecular forces have high melting points (such as diamond, with a melting point of 3,550 degrees Celsius), while solids with weak forces have low melting points (such as ice, which melts at 0 degrees Celsius). The hardness of a solid depends on the strength of the bonds in the crystal lattice: diamond is the hardest natural material, while soft metals like sodium and potassium can be cut with a knife.

### 8.2 The Liquid State

The liquid state is a state of matter in which particles can slide past each other, allowing the liquid to take the shape of its container while maintaining a constant volume. In the liquid state, particles are close to each other but are not fixed in fixed positions, as in solids. The cohesive forces between particles hold the liquid together, while the adhesive forces between the liquid and the surfaces of its surroundings determine the behavior of the liquid in contact with other materials.

Liquids exhibit interesting properties such as surface tension, viscosity, and capillarity. Surface tension is the tendency of a liquid's surface to behave as if it were an elastic membrane. This phenomenon occurs because particles on the liquid's surface experience a net force toward the interior of the liquid, since they have no particles above them. Surface tension is responsible for the spherical shape of water droplets and for the ability of some insects (such as water striders) to walk on the surface of water.

Viscosity is the resistance of a liquid to flow. Viscous liquids, like honey or oil, flow slowly, while low-viscosity liquids, like water or gasoline, flow easily. Viscosity decreases with increasing temperature, as thermal energy allows particles to more easily overcome cohesive forces. Capillarity is the ability of a liquid to rise or fall in a narrow tube (capillary) due to the combined action of cohesive and adhesive forces. Capillarity is responsible for the absorption of water by papers and fabrics, and for the transport of water from roots to leaves in plants.

### 8.3 The Gaseous State

The gaseous state is a state of matter in which particles move freely at high speed, filling all available volume. In the gaseous state, particles are very far apart from each other and the forces of interaction between them are negligible (except during collisions). This makes gases compressible, low in density, and able to expand to fill any container that holds them.

The properties of gases are described by empirical laws such as Boyle's law, Charles's law, and Gay-Lussac's law. Boyle's law states that, at constant temperature, the volume of a gas is inversely proportional to its pressure (P1V1 = P2V2). Charles's law states that, at constant pressure, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (V1/T1 = V2/T2). Gay-Lussac's law states that, at constant volume, the pressure of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (P1/T1 = P2/T2). These three laws combine in the ideal gas equation: PV = nRT, where P is the pressure, V the volume, n the number of moles, R the ideal gas constant, and T the absolute temperature.

Real gases deviate from ideal gas behavior, especially at high pressures and low temperatures, when intermolecular forces and particle volume are no longer negligible. The Van der Waals equation corrects the ideal gas equation to account for these effects: (P + a(n/V)^2)(V - nb) = nRT, where 'a' and 'b' are constants specific to each gas that depend on intermolecular forces and molecular volume, respectively. Real gases liquefy when compressed and cooled sufficiently, indicating that intermolecular forces become significant when particles are close enough together.

### 8.4 Phase Changes and Phase Diagrams

Phase changes are transitions between different states of matter (solid, liquid, gas). The main phase changes are melting (solid to liquid), vaporization (liquid to gas), sublimation (solid to gas), solidification (liquid to solid), condensation (gas to liquid), and deposition (gas to solid). All these changes are physical processes in which the chemical composition of the substance does not change, only the aggregation state of the particles changes.

The melting point is the temperature at which a solid melts and becomes a liquid at constant pressure. The boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid vaporizes and becomes a gas at constant pressure. The triple point is the temperature and pressure at which all three phases (solid, liquid, and gaseous) coexist in equilibrium. The critical point is the temperature and pressure above which it is impossible to distinguish between the liquid and gaseous phases, and the substance is in a supercritical fluid state.

Phase diagrams (or state diagrams) are graphical representations that show the temperature and pressure conditions under which each phase of a substance is stable. In a typical phase diagram, the lines separating the regions represent the conditions under which two phases coexist in equilibrium. The slope of the melting line can be positive (most substances) or negative (such as water, whose solid phase is less dense than its liquid phase). The study of phase diagrams is important for chemistry, physics, geology, and materials science, as it allows us to predict the behavior of substances under different conditions.

## Chapter 9: Organic Chemistry

### 9.1 Organic Compounds: Definition and Classification

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies compounds containing carbon, with the exception of some simple compounds such as carbon dioxide, sodium carbonate, and cyanides. Organic compounds are the basis of life, as they are part of all biological molecules such as proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleic acids, vitamins, and hormones. The richness of organic chemistry is due to carbon's unique ability to form stable bonds with other carbon atoms, creating long and complex chains.

Organic compounds are classified into several categories based on the functional groups they contain. Hydrocarbons are compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen, and are subdivided into alkanes (single bonds, CnH2n+2), alkenes (at least one double bond, CnH2n), alkynes (at least one triple bond, CnH2n-2), and aromatic hydrocarbons (such as benzene). Alcohols contain the hydroxyl group (-OH), aldehydes contain the formyl group (-CHO), ketones contain the carbonyl group (-CO-), and carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl group (-COOH).

Nitrogen-containing organic compounds include amines (ammonia derivatives), amides (containing the -CONH- group), amino acids (containing both an amino group and a carboxyl group), and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). Oxygen-containing organic compounds include ethers (with an oxygen atom bonded to two alkyl groups), esters (carboxylic acid derivatives), and carbohydrates (sugars and polysaccharides). Sulfur-containing organic compounds include thiols (with the -SH group), thioethers, and sulfonic acids.

### 9.2 Hydrocarbons and Their Derivatives

Hydrocarbons are the simplest organic compounds, as they contain only carbon and hydrogen. Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons (only single bonds) with the general formula CnH2n+2. Methane (CH4) is the simplest alkane and the main component of natural gas. Alkanes are important fuels: methane is used as domestic and industrial fuel, propane (C3H8) and butane (C4H10) are used in heating and portable stoves, and long carbon chains (C5-C12) form gasoline.

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons that contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond. Ethylene (CH2=CH2) is the simplest alkene and is a plant hormone that stimulates fruit ripening. Alkenes are important materials in the chemical industry, as the double bond is a center of reactivity that allows multiple chemical transformations. Polyethylene, the world's most produced plastic, is obtained by polymerizing ethylene. Alkynes are hydrocarbons that contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond. Acetylene (CH≡CH) is the simplest alkyne and is used in oxyacetylene welding, as it burns with a very high temperature flame (3,500 degrees Celsius).

Aromatic hydrocarbons contain a benzene ring, a hexagonal ring with six delocalized electrons that give it special stability. Benzene (C6H6) is the simplest aromatic hydrocarbon. Benzene derivatives include toluene (used as a solvent), phenol (used in resin and plasticizer manufacturing), styrene (polystyrene monomer), and xylene (used as a solvent). Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, such as naphthalene and pyrene, are found in coal tar and are used in dye and drug production.

### 9.3 Polymers and Synthetic Materials

Polymers are large molecules formed by the repetition of basic units called monomers. Polymerization is the process by which many monomers join to form a polymer chain. There are two main types of polymerization: addition polymerization (where monomers are added one by one to a growing chain) and condensation polymerization (where monomers react by eliminating a small molecule, such as water).

Plastics are synthetic polymers that can be molded by heat and pressure. The most common plastics include polyethylene (used in bags, containers, and pipes), polypropylene (used in furniture, automobiles, and textiles), polyvinyl chloride or PVC (used in pipes, windows, and flooring), polystyrene (used in foam packaging and electronic devices), and polyethylene terephthalate or PET (used in water bottles and sportswear). Plastics are extremely versatile materials, but their accumulation in the environment is a significant environmental problem.

Synthetic fibers are polymers spun into thin filaments used in textile production. The most common fibers include nylon (a strong and elastic fiber), polyester (a wrinkle-resistant and colorfast fiber), and acrylic (a lightweight fiber that imitates wool). Elastomers are elastic polymers that can stretch and return to their original shape. Synthetic rubber, such as styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR), is used in tires and shoe soles. Composite materials, such as carbon fiber reinforced with epoxy resin, are lightweight and extremely strong materials used in aeronautics, automotive, and sports equipment.

### 9.4 Green Chemistry and Sustainability

Green chemistry is an approach to chemistry that seeks to design chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. The principles of green chemistry include waste prevention, atom economy (maximizing the incorporation of all materials used into the final product), designing less hazardous solvents, energy efficiency, using renewable raw materials, and reducing toxicity.

Industrial biotechnology uses living organisms or enzymes to produce chemicals in a more sustainable way. Examples include the production of bioethanol from plant biomass, the synthesis of polylactic acid (PLA, a biodegradable plastic) from corn starch, and the production of fatty acids from algae. Biocatalysts (immobilized enzymes) are used in the pharmaceutical and chemical industries to catalyze reactions selectively and with low energy consumption.

Renewable energy and energy storage are areas where chemistry plays a fundamental role. Solar cells convert light energy into electrical energy using semiconductor materials such as silicon or perovskites. Lithium-ion batteries store electrical energy in the form of chemical energy and are used in electronic devices and electric vehicles. Hydrogen fuel is produced by water electrolysis and can be used in fuel cells to generate electricity. The development of new materials for these technologies is an active area of research in green chemistry.

## Chapter 10: Chemistry in Everyday Life

### 10.1 Chemistry in Food

Chemistry plays a fundamental role in food production, preservation, and safety. Nutrition is essentially a chemical process in which the nutrients in food are broken down into their basic components (amino acids, fatty acids, glucose) and used by the body's cells to produce energy, build tissues, and regulate biological processes. The three main nutrients are carbohydrates (which provide quick energy), proteins (which build and repair tissues), and lipids (which store energy and are part of cell membranes).

Food preservation uses chemical principles to prevent the proliferation of microorganisms and slow chemical degradation. Refrigeration slows chemical reactions and bacterial growth, while freezing almost completely stops both processes. Pasteurization heats foods to specific temperatures to eliminate pathogenic microorganisms without significantly altering nutritional properties. Modified atmosphere packaging controls the gas composition in the package (reducing oxygen and increasing carbon dioxide) to extend the food's shelf life.

Food additives are chemical substances added to foods to improve their properties. Preservatives (such as benzoic acid and sulfites) prevent the growth of microorganisms. Antioxidants (such as vitamin C and vitamin E) prevent food oxidation. Artificial and natural colorants improve visual appearance. Artificial sweeteners (such as aspartame and saccharin) provide sweetness without calories. Emulsifiers (such as lecithin) keep immiscible mixtures (such as oil and water) in a uniform state.

### 10.2 Chemistry in Medicine

Pharmaceutical chemistry is the discipline that designs, synthesizes, and develops drugs. Drugs are chemical molecules that interact with biological targets (such as enzymes, receptors, or nucleic acids) to produce a therapeutic effect. Drug discovery involves identifying a biological target, designing molecules that interact with it, chemical synthesis of those molecules, efficacy and safety testing, and the development of appropriate pharmaceutical formulations.

Analytical chemistry is fundamental in diagnostic medicine. Blood and urine analyses use chemical techniques such as spectrophotometry, chromatography, and electrophoresis to measure the concentration of metabolites, enzymes, hormones, and other biological substances. Tumor markers are chemical substances that are elevated in the presence of certain types of cancer. Medical imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) use chemical contrast agents to visualize organs and tissues in the body.

Medicinal chemistry also addresses problems such as antibiotic resistance, which is a growing public health concern. Antibiotic resistance occurs when bacteria develop mechanisms to survive antibiotics, such as producing enzymes that degrade the antibiotic, modifying the antibiotic's target, or actively expelling the antibiotic from the bacterial cell. The development of new antibiotics with different mechanisms of action is an active area of research in medicinal chemistry. Nanomedicine uses chemical nanoparticles to deliver drugs to specific targets in the body, improving efficacy and reducing side effects.

### 10.3 Chemistry in the Environment

Environmental chemistry studies the chemical processes that occur in the environment and their effects on human health and ecosystems. Air pollution is caused by emissions of gases such as sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx), carbon monoxide (CO), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). These gases react in the atmosphere to form smog (chemical fog), acid rain (precipitation with pH below 5.6 due to dissolved acids), and greenhouse gases (such as CO2 and methane) that contribute to climate change.

Water pollution is caused by wastewater discharge, agricultural chemicals (fertilizers and pesticides), heavy metals (lead, mercury, cadmium), and plastic waste. Fertilizers containing nitrogen and phosphorus cause eutrophication of lakes and rivers (excessive algae growth that depletes oxygen and kills aquatic life). Heavy metals are toxic and accumulate in the food chain (bioaccumulation and biomagnification). Plastic waste breaks down into microparticles that pollute oceans and aquatic organisms.

Green chemistry and environmental technology offer solutions to these problems. Catalytic converters reduce vehicle emissions by converting toxic gases into less harmful ones. Wastewater treatment systems use chemical processes such as flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection (with chlorine, ozone, or ultraviolet light) to purify water. Solar panels and wind turbines convert renewable energy into electricity without emitting greenhouse gases. The development of biodegradable materials and the circular economy (reuse, repair, and recycle) are strategies that seek to reduce waste generation and the consumption of natural resources.

### 10.4 Chemistry and Future Technology

Chemistry is at the center of many technologies that will define the future. Artificial intelligence and machine learning are being applied to the discovery of new materials and drugs, allowing the properties of molecules to be predicted before synthesizing them. Synthetic biology uses principles of chemistry and biology to design organisms with new capabilities, such as bacteria that produce biofuels or drought-resistant crops.

Nanotechnology manipulates matter at the nanometer scale (1-100 nanometers) to create materials with unique properties. Nanomaterials have applications in medicine (nanoparticles for drug delivery), electronics (carbon nanotubes), energy (nanocatalysts), and environment (nanofilters for water purification). Two-dimensional materials, such as graphene (a single layer of carbon atoms), have extraordinary properties such as high electrical conductivity, high mechanical strength, and flexibility.

Sustainability is the most important challenge for 21st-century chemistry. The transition to a low-carbon economy requires the development of new energy sources (hydrogen, fuel cells, energy storage), sustainable materials (bioplastics, recyclable materials), and more efficient chemical processes that consume less energy and generate less waste. Chemistry has the potential to contribute significantly to solving global problems such as climate change, water scarcity, food security, and disease, but it also has the responsibility of ensuring that its applications are safe and sustainable for the environment and human health.

## Chapter 11: Metals in Modern Technology

### 11.1 Metals in Electronics

Metals are fundamental to the modern electronics industry. Copper is the most used metal in the manufacturing of electrical wires and printed circuits, due to its excellent electrical conductivity and relatively low cost. Aluminum is used in the manufacturing of heat sinks for processors and electronic components, as it is lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and a good heat conductor. Gold is used in the manufacturing of high-quality electrical connectors and contacts, as it does not corrode and maintains low electrical resistance.

Rare earth metals are essential for the modern electronics industry. Neodymium is used in the manufacturing of high-strength permanent magnets, which are essential for computer hard drives, speakers, headphones, and electric vehicle motors. Dysprosium is added to neodymium magnets to improve their resistance to high temperatures. Europium is used as a red phosphor in television and computer screens. Terbium is used as a green phosphor in screens and fluorescent lamps.

Indium and gallium are fundamental to the semiconductor industry. Indium is used in the manufacturing of liquid crystal displays (LCDs) and indium-gallium-selenide (CIGS) solar cells. Gallium is used in the manufacturing of light-emitting diodes (LEDs), gallium arsenide (GaAs) lasers, and gallium arsenide solar cells. Tantalum is used in the manufacturing of tantalum capacitors, which are essential for electronic devices such as mobile phones, laptop computers, and digital cameras. Tin is used in the solders that connect electronic components on printed circuit boards.

### 11.2 Metals in Energy

Metals play a fundamental role in the production, storage, and distribution of energy. Lithium is the key metal for the energy transition, as it is the main component of lithium-ion batteries that power electric vehicles, electronic devices, and renewable energy storage systems. Demand for lithium has increased dramatically in recent years, driven by the growing production of electric vehicles and the installation of solar panels and wind turbines.

Copper is essential for energy distribution, as it is the predominant material in electrical wires, transformers, and distribution networks. The transition to renewable energy and electric vehicles will significantly increase copper demand, as these systems require more electrical wires than traditional systems. Aluminum is used in high-voltage transmission lines, as it is lighter than copper and has a lower cost, although its conductivity is lower.

Rare earth metals are fundamental to modern wind turbines, which use neodymium-dysprosium magnets to generate electricity. Each megawatt-capacity wind turbine contains approximately 500 kilograms of rare earth magnets. Platinum and palladium are used in vehicle catalysts, which reduce emissions of polluting gases. Gallium and indium are used in thin-film solar cells, which are an alternative to silicon solar cells.

### 11.3 Metals in Construction

Metals are fundamental materials in modern construction, used in structures, coatings, pipes, and decorative elements. Steel is the most used metal in construction, as it is strong, durable, and relatively economical. Steel is used in the construction of buildings, bridges, stadiums, skyscrapers, and industrial structures. Steel beams, steel columns, and steel reinforcement (which strengthens reinforced concrete) are fundamental elements of modern construction.

Aluminum is used in the manufacturing of windows, doors, facades, and decorative elements, as it is lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and easy to mold. Aluminum windows are popular in modern construction for their durability, easy maintenance, and aesthetic appearance. Copper is used in the manufacturing of plumbing pipes, roofs, and cladding, as it is corrosion-resistant, antimicrobial, and has a very long lifespan. Copper roofs, which develop a green patina over time, are an iconic architectural feature in many cities.

Titanium is used in the construction of structures exposed to corrosive environments, such as coastal bridges, port facilities, and buildings in coastal areas. Titanium is extremely corrosion-resistant, stronger than steel and lighter than aluminum, making it an ideal material for applications where durability is critical. Zinc is used in steel galvanization (coating with zinc to protect it from corrosion) and in the manufacturing of gutters, pipes, and coatings. Lead has historically been used in plumbing pipes, but its use has decreased due to its toxicity.

### 11.4 Metals in Medicine

Metals are fundamental to modern medicine, used in implants, surgical instruments, diagnosis, and treatment. Titanium is the most used metal in orthopedic and dental implants, as it is biocompatible (not rejected by the body), corrosion-resistant, and has mechanical properties similar to bone. Titanium implants are used in artificial joints (hips, knees), bone screws, fixation plates, and artificial teeth.

Platinum is used in the manufacturing of pacemakers, electrodes, and implantable medical devices, as it is biocompatible, corrosion-resistant, and a good electrical conductor. Cobalt is used in the manufacturing of alloys for orthopedic implants (such as chromium-cobalt), as it is wear-resistant and corrosion-resistant. Gold is used in dentistry for the manufacturing of crowns, bridges, and dental fillings, as it is biocompatible, corrosion-resistant, and has a pleasing aesthetic appearance. Mercury, although toxic, has historically been used in dental amalgams (although its use is decreasing due to concerns about its toxicity).

Metals are also used in medical diagnosis. Gadolinium, a rare earth metal, is used as a contrast agent in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), as it alters the magnetic signal of tissues and allows better visualization of organs and lesions. Technetium, a radioactive element artificially synthesized, is used in nuclear medicine for the diagnosis of cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and bone problems. Iron, an essential metal for life, is used in the production of artificial hemoglobin and in the manufacturing of nutritional supplements to treat anemia.

## Chapter 12: Chemistry and Future Materials

### 12.1 Advanced Materials and Nanotechnology

Nanotechnology is the manipulation of matter at the nanometer scale (1-100 nanometers) to create materials and devices with unique properties. At this scale, the physical and chemical properties of materials change significantly due to quantum and surface effects. Nanomaterials have applications in multiple fields, such as medicine, electronics, energy, and the environment. Carbon nanotubes, for example, are carbon cylinders with a diameter of a few nanometers that have mechanical strength 100 times greater than steel and electrical conductivity greater than copper.

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice, with extraordinary properties such as high electrical conductivity (200 times greater than copper), high mechanical strength (200 times greater than steel), high thermal conductivity, and flexibility. Graphene has potential applications in flexible displays, high-capacity batteries, water filters, structural composites, and next-generation electronic devices. The industrial production of graphene remains a challenge, but multiple companies and research centers are working to make it a reality.

Composite materials are materials formed by combining two or more materials with different properties that, when combined, produce a material with properties superior to those of its individual components. Carbon fiber composites, for example, are lightweight and extremely strong, and are used in aeronautics (aircraft such as the Boeing 787 Dreamliner and the Airbus A350), automotive (racing car bodies like F1), and sports equipment (tennis rackets, bicycles, golf clubs). Composite materials are fundamental to the aerospace industry, as they allow the weight of aircraft to be reduced and their energy efficiency to be improved.

### 12.2 Materials for Future Energy

The development of new materials is fundamental to the transition to a sustainable energy future. Hydrogen fuel cells use platinum catalysts to convert hydrogen and oxygen into electricity and water. However, the high cost of platinum limits the adoption of fuel cells. Researchers are working on the development of alternative catalysts based on less expensive metals such as iron, cobalt, and nickel, which could significantly reduce the cost of fuel cells.

Solid-state batteries are an emerging technology that promises to revolutionize energy storage. Unlike current lithium-ion batteries, which use a liquid electrolyte, solid-state batteries use a solid electrolyte (such as ceramic or polymer), making them safer (non-flammable), with higher energy density and longer lifespan. Companies like Toyota, Samsung, and QuantumScape are developing solid-state batteries that could be commercially available in the coming years.

New-generation solar panel materials include perovskites, which are crystalline materials with a structure similar to the mineral perovskite. Perovskite solar cells are cheaper to produce than silicon cells and can be deposited on flexible surfaces, making them ideal for applications such as solar windows, solar roofs, and portable electronic devices. However, the stability of perovskites remains a challenge, as they degrade rapidly in the presence of moisture and light. Researchers are working on developing more stable perovskites that can compete with silicon solar cells in terms of cost and efficiency.

### 12.3 Biocompatible Materials and Regenerative Medicine

Biocompatible materials are materials that can be used in the human body without causing significant adverse reactions. Titanium and its alloys are the most used materials in orthopedic and dental implants, as they are biocompatible, corrosion-resistant, and have mechanical properties similar to bone. Titanium implants are used in artificial joints (hips, knees), bone screws, fixation plates, and artificial teeth.

Hydrogels are polymeric materials that can absorb large amounts of water and have properties similar to biological tissues. Hydrogels are used in medical applications such as burn dressings, drug delivery systems, tissue engineering scaffolds, and contact lenses. Biodegradable hydrogels, which gradually break down in the body, are especially promising for regenerative medicine, as they can be used as scaffolds for the growth of new tissues.

3D printing of biocompatible materials is revolutionizing regenerative medicine. Researchers are using 3D printers to create customized implants, prosthetics, artificial organs, and scaffolds for tissue growth. 3D printing allows complex structures to be created with a precision that is not possible with traditional manufacturing techniques. In the future, 3D printing of organs could solve the problem of organ shortages for transplants, allowing customized organs to be created from the patient's own cells.

### 12.4 Sustainable Materials and Circular Economy

Sustainability is the most important challenge for 21st-century materials. The production of traditional materials (steel, aluminum, plastic) generates large amounts of CO2 and waste, contributing to climate change and environmental pollution. Sustainable materials seek to reduce the environmental impact of material production and use, using renewable raw materials, less polluting production processes, and recyclable or biodegradable materials.

Bioplastics are plastics produced from renewable raw materials such as corn, sugar cane, cellulose, or starch. The most common bioplastics include polylactic acid (PLA, produced from corn starch), polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA, produced by bacteria), and bioethylene (produced from sugar cane). Bioplastics are biodegradable and have a smaller carbon footprint than traditional plastics, but their industrial-scale production remains costly and presents limitations in terms of mechanical properties.

The circular economy is an economic model that seeks to eliminate waste and keep materials in use for as long as possible through reuse, repair, remanufacturing, and recycling. In a circular economy, materials are designed to be easily recyclable or reusable, and products are designed to have a long lifespan and be repairable. The circular economy is fundamental to reducing dependence on virgin raw materials and minimizing the environmental impact of industrial production. Companies like Patagonia, Interface, and Renault are leading the transition to a circular economy in their respective sectors.

## Chapter 13: Environmental Chemistry and Climate Change

### 13.1 Atmospheric Chemistry

The Earth's atmosphere is a mixture of gases that protects the Earth from excessive solar radiation and maintains the conditions necessary for life. The composition of the atmosphere includes nitrogen (78%), oxygen (21%), argon (0.93%), carbon dioxide (0.04%), and other trace gases. The ozone layer, located in the stratosphere at an altitude of between 15 and 50 kilometers, protects living organisms from the sun's ultraviolet radiation. However, human activity has significantly altered the composition of the atmosphere, causing problems such as air pollution, ozone layer depletion, and climate change.

Atmospheric pollutants include gases such as sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx), carbon monoxide (CO), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and tropospheric ozone (O3). These gases are produced by human activities such as fossil fuel combustion, industry, agriculture, and transportation. SO2 and NOx are the main causes of acid rain, which acidifies soils and water bodies, damaging forests, crops, and aquatic ecosystems.

Greenhouse gases (GHGs) are gases that absorb and emit infrared radiation, contributing to the warming of the atmosphere. The main GHGs are carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), and fluorinated gases. The concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere has increased from 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm today, a 50% increase that is responsible for the observed global warming. Climate change is one of humanity's greatest challenges, and chemistry plays a fundamental role in understanding and mitigating its effects.

### 13.2 Water Pollution

Water pollution is one of the most serious environmental problems in the world, with significant impacts on human health, ecosystems, and the economy. The main sources of water pollution include domestic and industrial wastewater, agricultural chemicals (fertilizers and pesticides), heavy metals (lead, mercury, cadmium), plastic waste, and oil spills.

Eutrophication is a process in which excess nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) in water cause excessive growth of algae and aquatic plants, which depletes oxygen and kills aquatic life. Eutrophication is primarily caused by agricultural fertilizer runoff and untreated wastewater. Heavy metals are toxic to aquatic organisms and accumulate in the food chain (bioaccumulation and biomagnification), causing health problems in humans who consume contaminated fish and shellfish.

Microplastics are plastic fragments less than 5 millimeters in diameter that pollute oceans, rivers, and lakes around the world. Microplastics come from the degradation of plastic objects, from synthetic fibers in clothing, and from cosmetics containing microbeads. Microplastics are ingested by aquatic organisms and can accumulate in the food chain, with potentially negative effects on human health. Wastewater treatment uses chemical processes such as flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection (with chlorine, ozone, or ultraviolet light) to purify water before discharging it into the environment.

### 13.3 Greenhouse Gases and Global Warming

Global warming is the increase in the Earth's average temperature due to the accumulation of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. The Earth's average temperature has increased by approximately 1.1 degrees Celsius since pre-industrial times, and is projected to continue rising if GHG emissions are not significantly reduced. The effects of climate change include the melting of glaciers, rising sea levels, extreme weather events (hurricanes, droughts, floods), ocean acidification, and biodiversity loss.

Chemistry plays a fundamental role in understanding and mitigating climate change. Scientists are developing new materials and processes to reduce GHG emissions, such as catalysts for carbon capture and storage (CCS), materials for more efficient solar panels, higher-capacity batteries, and hydrogen as a clean fuel. Chemistry is also important for developing second-generation biofuels (produced from agricultural and forestry waste) that can partially replace fossil fuels without competing with food production.

International agreements such as the Paris Agreement (2015) seek to limit the increase in global temperature to less than 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, with the goal of avoiding the worst effects of climate change. The transition to a low-carbon economy requires significant changes in the way we produce and consume energy, food, and products. Chemistry has the potential to contribute significantly to this transition, but it also has the responsibility of ensuring that its applications are sustainable and do not contribute to other environmental problems such as chemical pollution or biodiversity loss.

### 13.4 Chemical Waste Management

Chemical waste management is a significant environmental challenge, as many chemical wastes are toxic, persistent, and can contaminate soil, water, and air. Industrial chemical wastes include solvents, acids, bases, heavy metals, pesticides, and pharmaceutical products. Improper disposal of these wastes can cause groundwater contamination, soil pollution, and health problems in nearby communities.

The waste management hierarchy establishes a priority order for waste treatment: prevention (reducing waste generation), reuse (using waste again), recycling (transforming waste into new products), energy recovery (burning waste to produce energy), and disposal (burying waste in controlled landfills). Prevention is the most effective strategy for reducing the amount of chemical waste, and is achieved through the optimization of production processes, the use of less toxic raw materials, and the substitution of hazardous chemicals with safer alternatives.

Green chemistry seeks to design processes and products that minimize the generation of hazardous chemical waste. The principles of green chemistry include the use of catalysts (which reduce the amount of reagents needed), less hazardous solvents (such as water or supercritical CO2), atom economy (maximizing the incorporation of all materials into the final product), and toxicity reduction. The circular economy also contributes to chemical waste management by promoting recycling and reuse of materials, reducing the need to produce new materials from virgin raw materials.

## Chapter 14: Nuclear Chemistry and Nuclear Energy

### 14.1 Isotopes and Radioactivity

Isotopes are forms of the same chemical element that have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons in the nucleus. Isotopes can be stable (not undergoing radioactive decay) or unstable (decaying by emitting particles or electromagnetic radiation). Radioactivity is the process by which unstable atomic nuclei decay to reach a more stable state, emitting alpha particles (helium nuclei), beta particles (electrons or positrons), and gamma rays (high-energy electromagnetic radiation).

Radioactive isotopes are used in multiple applications, including nuclear medicine, material dating, industry, agriculture, and scientific research. In nuclear medicine, radioactive isotopes are used for diagnosis (such as technetium-99m, which is used in more than 40 million diagnostic procedures per year worldwide) and for cancer treatment (such as cobalt-60, which is used in radiotherapy). Carbon-14 dating is used to determine the age of organic materials up to 50,000 years, and has been fundamental for archaeology and paleontology.

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for half of the atoms in a sample to decay. Isotopes with short half-lives are used in medical applications (since radioactivity disappears quickly), while isotopes with long half-lives are used in industrial applications (since they provide a long-lasting source of radiation). Uranium-238, with a half-life of 4.5 billion years, is the heaviest naturally occurring isotope and is used as fuel in nuclear reactors. Plutonium-239, with a half-life of 24,100 years, is produced in nuclear reactors and is used as fuel and in nuclear weapons.

### 14.2 Nuclear Energy

Nuclear energy is a form of energy obtained from nuclear reactions, which can be fission (the splitting of heavy nuclei) or fusion (the joining of light nuclei). Nuclear energy is one of the most energy-dense sources that exists: one kilogram of uranium-235 can produce as much energy as 2,500 tons of coal, making it an extremely efficient energy source. Nuclear energy provides approximately 10% of the world's electricity and is one of the energy sources with the lowest greenhouse gas emissions.

Nuclear fission is the process used by current nuclear reactors. In fission, a uranium-235 or plutonium-239 nucleus is bombarded with neutrons, causing it to split into lighter nuclei, releasing energy and additional neutrons that can cause new fissions, creating a chain reaction. Nuclear reactors control the chain reaction to produce heat, which is used to generate steam that drives turbines and produces electricity. The main types of nuclear reactors include light water reactors (PWR and BWR), heavy water reactors (CANDU), and gas-graphite reactors.

Nuclear fusion is the process that occurs in the sun and stars, where hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy. Nuclear fusion promises to be a virtually inexhaustible energy source, as the fuel (deuterium and tritium) is abundant in seawater, and the waste is much less radioactive than that from fission. However, nuclear fusion is technically very difficult to achieve, as it requires temperatures of millions of degrees to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between nuclei. The ITER project (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor) in France is the world's largest nuclear fusion experiment, and is expected to demonstrate the technical viability of nuclear fusion in the coming decades.

### 14.3 Nuclear Waste and Its Management

Nuclear waste management is one of the most important challenges of the nuclear industry. Nuclear wastes are classified as low-level waste (containing low levels of radioactivity and can be disposed of in industrial landfills), intermediate-level waste (requiring shielding and temporarily stored), and high-level waste (containing high levels of radioactivity and requiring safe storage for thousands of years).

High-level waste, which includes spent fuel from nuclear reactors, contains radioactive isotopes with half-lives ranging from decades to thousands of years. The disposal of this waste is a significant technical and political challenge, as it requires safe storage for periods that exceed the duration of any human civilization. Nuclear countries are investigating deep geological disposal (storage in stable rock formations hundreds of meters deep) as the safest solution for high-level waste.

Finland is the leading country in deep geological disposal of nuclear waste, with the Onkalo repository (which means "cave" in Finnish), which is expected to begin operations in 2025. Sweden, France, Canada, and other countries are developing similar projects. Nuclear waste management is a significant public debate issue, as many citizens oppose the storage of radioactive waste near their communities. Transparency, public participation, and evidence-based decision-making are fundamental to addressing this challenge effectively.

### 14.4 Nuclear Safety and Nuclear Accidents

Nuclear safety is an absolute priority for the industry and governments, as nuclear accidents can have devastating consequences for human health and the environment. The main nuclear accidents in history include the Three Mile Island accident in the United States (1979), the Chernobyl disaster in the Soviet Union (1986), and the Fukushima Daiichi disaster in Japan (2011). These accidents have had significant consequences on public opinion and nuclear policies in multiple countries.

The Chernobyl disaster was the worst nuclear accident in history, when an explosion at reactor number 4 of the Chernobyl nuclear power plant (Ukraine) released large amounts of radioactive material into the atmosphere. The accident caused the evacuation of more than 350,000 people, the contamination of vast areas of Ukraine, Belarus, and Russia, and a significant increase in thyroid cancer cases in the region. The consequences of the Chernobyl disaster are still felt today, and the exclusion zone around the plant remains uninhabited.

The Fukushima Daiichi disaster was caused by a magnitude 9.0 earthquake and tsunami that caused the failure of cooling systems in three nuclear reactors, leading to partial meltdown of the cores and the release of radioactive material. The accident caused the evacuation of more than 150,000 people and the contamination of agricultural and coastal areas. Japan has invested billions of euros in decontamination and decommissioning of the plant, and the accident has generated significant debate about the future of nuclear energy in the country. These accidents have led to significant improvements in nuclear safety standards worldwide, but have also generated public concern about the safety of nuclear energy.

## Chapter 15: Food Chemistry and Nutrition

### 15.1 Macronutrients: Proteins, Carbohydrates, and Lipids

The macronutrients are the basic components of food that provide energy and materials for growth and tissue repair. The three main macronutrients are proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids. Each of them plays specific functions in the body, and a balanced diet should include all three in the proper proportions.

Proteins are large molecules formed by chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. There are 20 different amino acids that combine in multiple ways to form the different proteins in the human body. Nine of these amino acids are essential (cannot be synthesized by the body and must be obtained from the diet): histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine. Proteins are found in foods such as meat, fish, eggs, dairy products, legumes, and nuts. Proteins are essential for building and repairing tissues, producing enzymes and hormones, and the functioning of the immune system.

Carbohydrates are the body's main source of energy. They are classified as simple carbohydrates (sugars such as glucose, fructose, and sucrose) and complex carbohydrates (starch and fiber). Simple carbohydrates are absorbed quickly and provide quick energy, while complex carbohydrates are absorbed more slowly and provide sustained energy. Foods rich in carbohydrates include grains (rice, wheat, corn), tubers (potatoes, cassava), fruits, and vegetables. Dietary fiber, a type of indigestible complex carbohydrate, is important for digestive health and the prevention of chronic diseases.

Lipids are the body's most concentrated form of energy, providing 9 kilocalories per gram (compared to 4 kilocalories per gram of protein or carbohydrate). Lipids are essential for the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), hormone production, organ protection, and body temperature maintenance. Foods rich in lipids include vegetable oils, butter, cream, nuts, seeds, fatty fish, and fatty meat. Lipids are classified as saturated (found in animal products), monounsaturated (such as olive oil), and polyunsaturated (such as omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, found in fish and seeds).

### 15.2 Vitamins and Minerals

Vitamins are essential organic compounds that the body needs in small amounts to function properly. Vitamins are classified as water-soluble (B-complex vitamins and vitamin C), which dissolve in water and are not stored in the body, and fat-soluble (vitamins A, D, E, K), which dissolve in fat and are stored in the liver and adipose tissue. Each vitamin has specific functions: vitamin A is essential for vision, vitamin C for the immune system, vitamin D for calcium absorption, vitamin E as an antioxidant, and vitamin K for blood clotting.

Minerals are inorganic elements that the body needs for multiple functions, such as building bones and teeth, transporting oxygen, nerve function, and regulating metabolism. The main minerals include calcium (essential for bones and teeth), iron (essential for oxygen transport in hemoglobin), zinc (essential for the immune system and wound healing), potassium (essential for cardiac function and blood pressure regulation), and sodium (essential for fluid balance). Foods rich in minerals include dairy products (calcium), red meat (iron), nuts (magnesium, zinc), green leafy vegetables (iron, calcium), and bananas (potassium).

Vitamin and mineral deficiencies can cause significant health problems. Scurvy (vitamin C deficiency) causes weakness, joint pain, and bleeding. Iron-deficiency anemia causes fatigue, pallor, and difficulty breathing. Rickets (vitamin D deficiency) causes bone weakening in children. Avitaminosis (deficiency of multiple vitamins) can cause serious health problems. On the other hand, excess vitamins and minerals (hypervitaminosis or hypermineralosis) can also be harmful, so it is important to maintain adequate balance through a varied and balanced diet.

### 15.3 Antioxidants and Health

Antioxidants are compounds that protect cells from damage caused by free radicals, unstable molecules produced during normal metabolism and in response to environmental factors such as pollution, tobacco, ultraviolet radiation, and stress. Free radicals can cause oxidative damage to cells, which is associated with premature aging, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and other chronic diseases.

The main antioxidants include vitamin C (found in citrus fruits, peppers, strawberries, and kiwi), vitamin E (found in vegetable oils, nuts, and seeds), carotenoids such as beta-carotene (found in carrots, pumpkin, and spinach), polyphenols (found in green tea, dark chocolate, grapes, and red wine), and lycopene (found in processed tomatoes). These antioxidants are found in plant-based foods and are most effective when consumed as part of a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains.

Antioxidant supplements are not necessary for most people who follow a balanced diet, and some studies have suggested that high-dose antioxidant supplements may be harmful. The best strategy for obtaining antioxidants is to consume a varied diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and healthy fats. Processed foods, on the other hand, tend to be poor in antioxidants and may contain pro-oxidant substances such as trans fatty acids and chemical preservatives.

### 10.4 The Chemistry of Food Cooking

Food cooking is a chemical process that transforms the physical and chemical properties of foods, making them more digestible, safe, and appetizing. The chemical reactions that occur during cooking include protein denaturation (which makes foods softer and more digestible), starch gelatinization (which makes foods softer and creamier), the Maillard reaction (which produces the characteristic flavors and aromas of fried, roasted, and toasted foods), and caramelization (which produces the golden color and sweet taste of toasted foods).

The Maillard reaction is one of the most important chemical reactions in cooking. It occurs between amino acids and reducing sugars at temperatures above 140 degrees Celsius, and produces hundreds of chemical compounds responsible for the characteristic flavors, colors, and aromas of fried, roasted, toasted, and baked foods. The Maillard reaction is responsible for the taste of toasted bread, the crust of roasted meat, the golden color of French fries, and the aroma of freshly roasted coffee. However, at very high temperatures (above 180 degrees Celsius), the Maillard reaction can produce acrylamide and other potentially harmful compounds, so it is important to control cooking temperature.

Lipid oxidation is another important chemical process during cooking. When oils are heated to high temperatures, fatty acids react with oxygen to form oxidized compounds that can be harmful to health. Oils with high smoke points (such as extra virgin olive oil, with a smoke point of 190-210 degrees Celsius) are more stable at high temperatures than oils with low smoke points (such as sunflower oil, with a smoke point of 100-110 degrees Celsius). Deep frying (at temperatures of 170-190 degrees Celsius) produces crispy foods on the outside and juicy on the inside, but the frying oil must be changed regularly to avoid the accumulation of oxidized compounds.

## Chapter 16: Cosmic Chemistry and the Origins of Life

### 16.1 The Chemistry of Stars

Stars are giant nuclear furnaces where chemical elements are synthesized through nuclear fusion reactions. Hydrogen, the simplest and most abundant element in the universe, fuses to form helium in the cores of stars. More massive stars can synthesize heavier elements up to iron, while elements heavier than iron are synthesized in supernova explosions. A supernova is the explosion of a massive star at the end of its life, which releases enormous amounts of energy and synthesized elements into interstellar space.

The chemical composition of stars is determined through spectroscopy, a technique that analyzes the light emitted or absorbed by a star to identify the elements present. Each chemical element produces a unique spectrum of absorption or emission lines, which allows the identification of elements present in the star. Stellar spectroscopy has revealed that stars contain the same elements found on Earth, confirming that all chemical elements in the universe were synthesized in stars and supernovae.

Chemical cosmology studies the chemical evolution of the universe, from the formation of the first elements (hydrogen and helium) in the Big Bang to the synthesis of heavier elements in stars and supernovae. The lightest elements (hydrogen, helium, lithium) formed in the first minutes after the Big Bang, while heavier elements gradually formed in stars over billions of years. The abundance of elements in the universe is a record of the history of star formation and the chemical evolution of the cosmos.

### 16.2 The Chemical Origins of Life

The origins of life on Earth is one of the deepest mysteries of science. Life, as we know it, is based on carbon molecules, which have the unique ability to form long and complex chains that can store information, catalyze chemical reactions, and replicate themselves. Carbon is the fundamental element of life because it can form four covalent bonds, allowing it to create molecules with an enormous variety of structures and properties.

The RNA world hypothesis suggests that the earliest forms of life were based on RNA (ribonucleic acid) rather than DNA. RNA can store genetic information (like DNA) and catalyze chemical reactions (like enzymes), making it a plausible candidate for the first self-replicating molecule. Laboratory experiments have shown that the nitrogenous bases of RNA (adenine, guanine, cytosine, uracil) can be synthesized from simple compounds under conditions similar to those of the primitive Earth.

The Miller-Urey experiment (1953) simulated the conditions of the primitive Earth (methane, ammonia, hydrogen, and water vapor) and demonstrated that amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) can form spontaneously through chemical reactions. This experiment was a fundamental step in understanding the chemical origins of life, as it showed that the basic components of life can form from inorganic materials under natural conditions. However, the step from simple molecules to complex cells remains a mystery that science is actively investigating.

### 16.3 Astrochemistry and the Search for Extraterrestrial Life

Astrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the chemical composition of the universe, including the atoms, molecules, and materials present in stars, planets, comets, and interstellar space. Astrochemistry has discovered more than 200 molecules in interstellar space, including water, ethanol, amino acids, and complex organic compounds. These discoveries suggest that the chemical ingredients necessary for life are common in the universe, increasing the probability of finding extraterrestrial life.

The search for extraterrestrial life is one of the priorities of modern astrophysics. Space telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) are searching for biological molecules (such as oxygen, methane, and water) in the atmospheres of exoplanets (planets orbiting other stars). The detection of these molecules in exoplanet atmospheres could be evidence of the existence of life, as these molecules are produced by biological processes on Earth.

NASA's Mars Perseverance mission is searching for evidence of past or present life on Mars, using chemical instruments to analyze the composition of Martian rocks and soil. The mission has found evidence that Mars had liquid water in the past and that its atmosphere contained gases that could have been produced by living organisms. However, evidence of life on Mars remains inconclusive, and the search will continue in the coming decades with future missions.

### 16.4 Chemistry and Space Nanotechnology

Space nanotechnology is an emerging field that uses nanomaterials and nanodevices for space applications. Nanomaterials have unique properties that make them ideal for space applications, such as high mechanical strength, low density, high electrical conductivity, and radiation resistance. Carbon nanotubes, for example, are candidates for the construction of space elevators, structures that would connect the Earth's surface with geostationary orbit.

Nanotechnology is also being used to develop advanced propulsion systems, such as ion engines that use electromagnetic fields to accelerate charged particles. Ion engines are much more efficient than traditional chemical engines, but produce very low thrust, making them suitable for long-term missions to outer planets. Nanotechnology is also being used to develop more efficient solar panels, high-sensitivity sensors, and materials for radiation protection.

The future of cosmic chemistry is extraordinarily promising, with discoveries that are transforming our understanding of the universe and the origins of life. The combination of astrochemistry, nanotechnology, and molecular biology is opening new possibilities for the search for extraterrestrial life, space exploration, and the understanding of fundamental chemical processes in the universe. Cosmic chemistry is a field that combines the most advanced science with the boldest imagination, and its discoveries will continue to surprise us in the coming decades.

## Chapter 17: Chemistry and the Protection of Cultural Heritage

### 17.1 Chemistry in the Conservation of Works of Art

The conservation and restoration of works of art is a field where chemistry plays a fundamental role. Art conservators use chemical principles to stabilize, clean, and restore paintings, sculptures, textiles, and other works of art. The cleaning of paintings, for example, requires the use of solvents that remove dirt and aged varnish without damaging the original paint layer. The most commonly used solvents include ethanol, acetone, toluene, and carefully selected solvent mixtures.

The restoration of frescoes, such as those in the Sistine Chapel by Michelangelo, requires a deep knowledge of the chemistry of pigments and binders. Frescoes are created by applying pigments to wet plaster, producing a chemical reaction (carbonation) that fixes the pigments to the wall. The restoration of frescoes involves the removal of layers of dirt, varnish, and previous paint, and the stabilization of the original paint layer. Conservators use techniques such as consolidation (applying adhesives to stabilize the paint layer), chromatic retouching (applying paint to fill gaps), and final varnishing (applying a protective varnish).

The conservation of historical textiles, such as tapestries, embroideries, and archaeological fabrics, requires the use of chemical techniques to stabilize fibers, remove dirt, and prevent degradation. Ancient textiles are particularly vulnerable to degradation from light, humidity, temperature, and atmospheric pollution. Conservators use techniques such as chemical washing (with mild, controlled detergents), fiber stabilization (with adhesives and consolidants), and storage under controlled conditions (temperature, humidity, and light).

### 17.2 Chemistry in Archaeology

Chemistry is a fundamental tool in archaeology, used to date materials, analyze the composition of artifacts, identify pigments, and determine the provenance of materials. Carbon-14 dating is the most used chemical technique in archaeology, which allows determining the age of organic materials up to 50,000 years. Carbon-14 dating is based on the fact that carbon-14 (a radioactive isotope of carbon) decays at a known rate, and the proportion of carbon-14 in an organic material indicates its age.

X-ray fluorescence analysis (XRF) is a non-invasive technique that allows determining the chemical composition of materials without the need to take samples. XRF is used to identify pigments in paintings, metals in metal objects, and ceramics in pottery. Mass spectrometry is a technique that identifies molecules by their mass and fragmentation pattern, and is used to analyze organic residues in archaeological artifacts, such as fats, oils, and proteins.

Ancient DNA analysis is a technique that has revolutionized archaeology, allowing the identification of human and animal remains, the determination of kinship, and the study of migration and evolution of human populations. Ancient DNA is extracted from bones, teeth, and preserved soft remains, and is analyzed using next-generation sequencing techniques. Ancient DNA analysis has revealed surprising information about the history of human populations, such as the origin of animal domestication, prehistoric migrations, and admixture between populations.

### 17.3 Chemistry in the Protection of Monuments

The protection of historical monuments against environmental degradation is a challenge that requires the use of chemical principles. Stone monuments, such as cathedrals, castles, and sculptures, are vulnerable to acid rain, atmospheric pollution, freeze-thaw cycles, and biological growth (moss, lichen, algae). The protection of these monuments involves cleaning, consolidation, and the application of surface treatments that protect the stone from degradation.

The consolidation of deteriorated stone is carried out by applying consolidants that penetrate the stone and reinforce it from within. The most commonly used consolidants include silicone-based products (such as silane and siloxane), which create a hydrophobic network within the stone that protects it from water without blocking its porosity. Acrylic consolidants are more flexible but less durable, and are used in stones that experience thermal movement.

The application of surface water repellents protects the stone from water penetration without altering its visual appearance. Silicone-based water repellents create an invisible layer on the stone surface that repels water but allows moisture to escape. Repair mortars are used to fill cracks and voids in the stone, using mortars with physical properties similar to those of the original stone. Monument cleaning is carried out using techniques such as chemical cleaning (with solvents and mild detergents), mechanical cleaning (with brushes and compressed air), and laser cleaning (which removes dirt without damaging the stone).

### 17.4 Chemistry and the Authentication of Works of Art

The authentication of works of art is a field where chemistry plays a fundamental role, as it allows determining whether a work is authentic or a forgery. Chemical authentication techniques include pigment analysis, support analysis, dating, and computer-assisted stylistic analysis. Forgers use modern pigments in works that claim to be ancient, and chemical analysis of pigments can reveal these anachronisms.

Carbon-14 dating is used to date organic works of art (such as panel paintings, textiles, and paper), and can reveal whether a work is earlier or later than the claimed date. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) allows the identification of pigments without taking samples, and modern pigments (such as titanium white, which was synthesized in 1921) reveal that a painting is a forgery. Raman spectroscopy identifies molecules by their vibration pattern, and can distinguish between natural and synthetic pigments.

The authentication of works of art is a field where science and art meet, and requires a combination of chemical, artistic, and historical knowledge. Museums, collectors, and auction houses use chemical authentication techniques to protect themselves from forgeries, which represent an estimated market of billions of dollars annually. Chemistry is a powerful tool for protecting cultural heritage and ensuring the authenticity of works of art.

## Chapter 18: Chemistry and the Pharmaceutical Industry

### 18.1 Drug Discovery

Drug discovery is a complex process that combines chemistry, biology, pharmacology, and medicine to develop new molecules with therapeutic activity. The typical drug discovery process includes identifying a biological target (such as an enzyme, receptor, or nucleic acid), designing molecules that interact with it, chemical synthesis of those molecules, efficacy and safety testing, and the development of appropriate pharmaceutical formulations. The process of discovering and developing a new drug can take between 10 and 15 years and cost more than 1 billion dollars.

Computer-aided drug design (CADD) methods use molecular modeling and artificial intelligence to design molecules with desired properties. CADD algorithms can predict a molecule's affinity for its biological target, its potential toxicity, its absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME), and its interactions with other molecules. High-throughput screening (HTS) methods allow thousands or millions of compounds to be tested against a biological target in a short period of time, accelerating the discovery process.

Combinatorial chemistry is a synthesis method that allows the creation of large libraries of compounds with a variety of chemical structures. These libraries are tested against biological targets to identify compounds with therapeutic activity. Diversity-oriented synthesis is an approach that uses chemical reactions to create molecules with great structural diversity from a few components. These methods have significantly accelerated the drug discovery process, but the selection of promising compounds remains a challenge that requires biological and pharmacological evaluation of thousands of compounds.

### 18.2 Chemical Synthesis of Drugs

The chemical synthesis of drugs is the process by which drug molecules are created from chemical raw materials. Chemical synthesis can be simple (few steps) or complex (many steps), depending on the structure of the molecule and the availability of raw materials. Synthetic drugs are classified as small-molecule drugs (such as aspirin, ibuprofen, and paracetamol) and large-molecule drugs (such as therapeutic proteins, monoclonal antibodies, and vaccines).

The synthesis of small-molecule drugs may involve chemical reactions such as alkylation, acylation, halogenation, nitration, reduction, oxidation, and condensation. Asymmetric synthesis is a technique that allows the creation of molecules with a specific spatial configuration (enantiomers), which is important for drugs where only one enantiomer has therapeutic activity (such as ibuprofen and naproxen). The synthesis of large-molecule drugs, such as therapeutic proteins, requires biotechnology techniques such as expression in genetically modified organisms (bacteria, yeast, mammalian cells) and chromatographic purification.

Drug quality is fundamental to patient safety. Drugs must meet strict standards of purity, potency, stability, and absence of impurities. The pharmacopoeia is an official document that establishes quality standards for drugs, including analysis methods, impurity limits, and labeling requirements. The inspection of drug manufacturers by regulatory agencies such as the FDA (United States), EMA (Europe), and AEMPS (Spain) is fundamental to ensuring drug quality and safety.

### 18.3 Vaccines and Biotechnology

Vaccines are one of the most important tools of modern medicine, having saved millions of lives by preventing infectious diseases such as smallpox, measles, polio, and hepatitis. Vaccines work by stimulating the body's immune system to produce antibodies against a specific pathogen, without causing the disease. The main types of vaccines include inactivated vaccines (containing killed pathogens), subunit vaccines (containing parts of the pathogen), vector vaccines (using a modified virus to deliver the antigen), and RNA vaccines (using messenger RNA to instruct cells to produce the antigen).

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the development of RNA vaccines, a technology that had been in development for decades but had never been used on a global scale. The Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna RNA vaccines were developed in less than a year, an unprecedented achievement that demonstrates the power of modern biotechnology. RNA vaccines work by introducing messenger RNA into the body's cells, which instruct the cells to produce the spike protein of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, stimulating an immune response. RNA vaccines are safer and faster to produce than traditional vaccines, and have the potential to be used to prevent other diseases such as cancer, malaria, and HIV.

Modern biotechnology has revolutionized the pharmaceutical industry, enabling the production of biopharmaceuticals such as monoclonal antibodies, therapeutic proteins, next-generation vaccines, and gene therapies. Monoclonal antibodies are molecules designed to bind to specific targets in the body, and are used in the treatment of cancer, autoimmune diseases, and infectious diseases. Gene therapy uses viral vectors to introduce functional genes into patient cells, correcting genetic defects that cause hereditary diseases. Biotechnology is a rapidly evolving field that promises to transform medicine in the coming decades.

### 18.4 Pharmacology and Personalized Medicine

Pharmacology is the science that studies how drugs interact with the body, including their absorption, distribution, metabolism, effects, and mechanisms of action. Clinical pharmacology deals with the safe and effective use of drugs in patients, including the selection of appropriate doses, monitoring of effects, and prevention of drug interactions. Pharmacogenomics is an emerging field that studies how individual genetics affect drug response, enabling personalized treatment.

Personalized medicine is a medical approach that seeks to tailor treatment to the individual characteristics of each patient, including their genetics, microbiome, lifestyle, and medical history. Pharmacogenomics allows prediction of which patients will respond well to a drug and which will experience adverse effects, enabling the selection of the most appropriate treatment for each patient. Personalized medicine has the potential to improve drug efficacy, reduce adverse effects, and optimize the use of healthcare resources.

Biomarkers are biological indicators that can be measured in blood, urine, tissues, or other body fluids to diagnose diseases, evaluate disease progression, and monitor treatment response. Biomarkers are fundamental to personalized medicine, as they allow the identification of patient subgroups that will benefit from specific treatments. Advances in genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics are discovering new biomarkers that can improve the diagnosis and treatment of multiple diseases, from cancer to neurodegenerative diseases.

## Chapter 19: Chemistry and Renewable Energy

### 19.1 The Chemistry of Solar Cells

Solar cells convert light energy into electrical energy using semiconductor materials. Crystalline silicon solar cells are the most common and use pure or doped silicon wafers to generate electric current when light hits them. Silicon is the most used semiconductor material because it is abundant, stable, and has suitable electronic properties. However, the production of high-purity silicon is a chemically intensive process that requires high temperatures and consumes a lot of energy.

Perovskite solar cells are an emerging technology that promises to revolutionize solar energy. Perovskites are crystalline materials with a structure similar to the mineral perovskite, and can be synthesized from relatively inexpensive chemical components through low-temperature processes. Perovskite solar cells have achieved efficiencies of 25% in the laboratory, making them comparable to silicon cells. However, the stability of perovskites remains a challenge, as they degrade rapidly in the presence of moisture, light, and oxygen.

Thin-film solar cells use materials such as cadmium telluride (CdTe), indium-gallium selenide (CIGS), and gallium arsenide (GaAs) deposited on flexible substrates. These cells are cheaper to produce than silicon cells and can be manufactured in flexible rolls, making them ideal for applications such as solar roofs, building facades, and portable devices. Gallium arsenide (GaAs) is the material with the highest solar conversion efficiency (over 29%), but its high cost limits its use to niche applications such as satellite and rocket solar panels.

### 19.2 The Chemistry of Batteries and Energy Storage

Batteries are electrochemical devices that store energy in the form of chemical energy and release it as electrical energy. Lithium-ion batteries are the most used in electronic devices and electric vehicles, due to their high energy density, low weight, and long lifespan. Lithium-ion batteries use a lithium electrode (which is oxidized during discharge) and a graphite electrode (where lithium ions intercalate during discharge), separated by a liquid electrolyte.

Solid-state batteries are an emerging technology that replaces the liquid electrolyte with a solid electrolyte (such as ceramic, glass, or polymer). Solid-state batteries are safer (non-flammable), with higher energy density and longer lifespan than lithium-ion batteries. Companies like Toyota, Samsung, and QuantumScape are developing solid-state batteries that could be commercially available in the coming years. Sodium-ion batteries are another emerging alternative, as sodium is much more abundant and cheaper than lithium.

Flow batteries are a type of battery that stores energy in liquid tanks containing electrolytes. Flow batteries are ideal for large-scale energy storage, as their storage capacity depends on the volume of the liquid tanks, not the surface area of the electrodes. Vanadium flow batteries are the most technologically mature, but their high cost limits their use. Zinc-bromine and iron-chlorine flow batteries are cheaper alternatives being developed for renewable energy storage.

### 19.3 The Chemistry of Hydrogen as a Fuel

Hydrogen is a clean fuel that produces only water when combined with oxygen, making it an ideal candidate to replace fossil fuels. Hydrogen can be produced from water through electrolysis (using electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen), from natural gas through reforming (using steam to extract hydrogen from methane), or from biomass through gasification. Hydrogen produced from renewable energy (electrolysis with solar or wind energy) is known as "green hydrogen" and is the cleanest and most sustainable.

Hydrogen fuel cells convert the chemical energy of hydrogen into electrical energy through an electrochemical reaction. PEM (proton exchange membrane) fuel cells are the most common and use a platinum catalyst to facilitate the reaction between hydrogen and oxygen. Fuel cells are more efficient than internal combustion engines (60% efficiency versus 30-40%) and produce only water as waste. However, the high cost of platinum and the lack of hydrogen distribution infrastructure currently limit their use.

The hydrogen economy is a concept that seeks to create an energy system based on hydrogen as an energy carrier. In a hydrogen economy, renewable electricity is used to produce green hydrogen through electrolysis, which is stored and transported for use in fuel cells, combustion engines, and industrial processes. Countries like Germany, Japan, South Korea, and Australia are implementing national hydrogen strategies with the hope of becoming leaders in the hydrogen economy. The hydrogen economy is a promising concept but presents significant technical and economic challenges that must be addressed in the coming decades.

### 19.4 The Chemistry of Tidal and Wave Energy

Tidal energy is energy obtained from the movement of ocean tides, which is caused by the gravitational attraction of the moon and sun. Tidal power plants use the difference in water level between high tide and low tide to drive turbines and generate electricity. The world's largest tidal power plants include the La Rance plant in France (240 MW) and the Sihwa Lake plant in South Korea (254 MW). Tidal energy is a predictable and clean energy source, but its development is limited by the availability of sites with sufficiently large tides and its environmental impact on coastal ecosystems.

Wave energy is energy obtained from the movement of ocean waves, which is caused by wind blowing over the ocean surface. Wave energy devices convert the movement of waves into electrical energy using oscillating floats, oscillating water columns, attenuation devices, or devices that harness pressure changes. Wave energy devices are in the development phase, and most prototypes are in the testing phase. Wave energy has enormous potential, as ocean waves contain a significant amount of energy that could be used to generate electricity.

Chemistry plays a fundamental role in the development of renewable energies, from the synthesis of materials for solar cells and batteries to the development of catalysts for fuel cells and electrolyzers. Research in materials chemistry, electrochemistry, and catalysis is fundamental to improving the efficiency, reducing the cost, and increasing the sustainability of renewable energy technologies. The combination of advances in chemistry, physics, engineering, and public policies is necessary to achieve a successful transition to a sustainable, low-carbon energy system.

## Chapter 20: Chemistry and Environmental Protection

### 20.1 Carbon Capture Technologies

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a technology that seeks to reduce CO2 emissions from power plants and industrial processes, capturing CO2 before it is released into the atmosphere and storing it permanently in deep geological formations. The main CO2 capture methods include post-combustion capture (separating CO2 from exhaust gases after combustion), pre-combustion capture (converting fuel into a mixture of hydrogen and CO2 before combustion), and oxy-fuel combustion (combustion in a pure oxygen environment that produces a stream of pure CO2).

Chemical solvents are the most used for CO2 capture in power plants. Amine-based solvents (such as monoethanolamine, MEA) absorb CO2 from exhaust gases when they come into contact with them, and release CO2 when heated, allowing their recycling. Amine-based solvents are effective but have limitations, such as the high cost of regeneration, solvent degradation, and the need for large amounts of energy for regeneration. New solvents, such as sterically hindered amines, amino acid-based solvents, and flexible-temperature solvents, are being developed to overcome these limitations.

Solid adsorbent materials are porous materials that capture CO2 by adsorption on their surface. The most commonly used adsorbents include zeolites (porous minerals with an aluminosilicate structure), microporous organic materials (MOFs, by their English acronym), and solid amines. MOFs are synthetic materials with an extremely high surface area (up to 7,000 m2/g) that can be designed with specific functionalities to capture CO2. Solid adsorbents have advantages over liquid solvents, such as lower energy consumption for regeneration and greater stability, but their industrial-scale production remains costly.

CO2 storage is carried out by injecting captured CO2 into deep geological formations, such as depleted saline aquifers, depleted gas fields, and carbonate formations. The injected CO2 dissolves in groundwater and eventually reacts with rock minerals to form stable carbonates, ensuring long-term storage. Geological CO2 storage is a mature technology that has been tested in projects such as Sleipner (Norway), which has been storing one million tons of CO2 per year since 1996.

### 20.2 The Chemistry of Environmental Remediation

Environmental remediation uses chemical principles to clean up soils and waters contaminated by industrial, agricultural, and mining activities. The main contaminants include petroleum hydrocarbons, heavy metals, chlorinated solvents, pesticides, and pharmaceutical products. Remediation can be in situ (treating the contaminant in place) or ex situ (extracting the contaminant and treating it off-site).

Bioremediation uses microorganisms to degrade organic contaminants in soil and water. Microorganisms can use contaminants as a source of carbon and energy, breaking them down into less toxic substances such as CO2 and water. Bioremediation is an environmentally friendly and economically viable technology, but its effectiveness depends on factors such as the type of contaminant, environmental conditions, and the presence of suitable microorganisms. Phytoremediation uses plants to absorb or stabilize contaminants in the soil. Hyperaccumulator plants, such as sunflowers and tobacco, can absorb large amounts of heavy metals from the soil, which are then removed through the collection and safe disposal of the plants.

In situ chemical remediation uses chemical reagents to transform contaminants into less toxic substances. The most commonly used reagents include potassium permanganate (KMnO4) for oxidizing chlorinated hydrocarbons, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for oxidizing organic compounds, and sodium dithionite (Na2S2O4) for reducing heavy metals to less mobile forms. Chemical remediation is fast and effective, but can have adverse effects on soil and soil organisms if not applied correctly.

### 20.3 Green Chemistry

Green chemistry is the set of chemical technologies that reduce the environmental impact of industrial processes and resource consumption. The principles of green chemistry include waste prevention, atom economy, the use of less hazardous solvents, energy efficiency, the use of renewable raw materials, and toxicity reduction. Green chemistry is applied in multiple sectors, including the chemical, pharmaceutical, food, and construction industries.

Green synthesis seeks to design chemical processes that minimize waste generation and resource use. Catalysts are fundamental to green synthesis, as they allow chemical reactions to be accelerated without being consumed, reducing the amount of reagents needed and the waste generated. Biological catalysts (enzymes) are especially promising, as they operate at low temperatures and normal pressures, are highly selective, and are biodegradable. The immobilization of enzymes on solid supports allows their reuse, reducing costs and waste.

Green solvents are solvents that have a low environmental impact. Water is the green solvent par excellence, but many chemical processes require organic solvents. Green solvents include supercritical CO2 (CO2 compressed above its critical point, which has intermediate properties between liquid and gas), ionic liquids (salts in liquid state at room temperature), and bio-solvents (solvents derived from renewable raw materials, such as biomass ethanol). The adoption of green solvents is an active area of research that promises to significantly reduce chemical pollution from industry.

### 20.4 Chemistry and Ozone Layer Protection

The ozone layer is a layer of the stratosphere that contains a high concentration of ozone (O3), which protects the Earth from the sun's ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), which were used as refrigerants, solvents, and aerosol propellants, are the main cause of ozone layer depletion. CFCs, once released into the atmosphere, rise to the stratosphere where UV radiation breaks them down, releasing chlorine atoms that destroy ozone molecules in a chain reaction.

The Montreal Protocol (1987) is an international agreement that seeks to eliminate the production and use of CFCs and other gases that deplete the ozone layer. The protocol has been a significant success, with a 99% reduction in CFC production since its adoption. CFC substitutes include hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs), which have a lower ozone depletion potential, and hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), which do not deplete the ozone layer but are potent greenhouse gases. The Kigali Agreement (2016) amends the Montreal Protocol to reduce the use of HFCs, which are greenhouse gases thousands of times more potent than CO2.

The recovery and recycling of refrigerants is fundamental to the protection of the ozone layer. Refrigerants must be recovered from refrigeration and air conditioning equipment before disposal, and can be recycled or safely destroyed. The destruction of stored CFCs is carried out through processes such as pyrolysis (thermal decomposition) and hydrolysis (decomposition by water). The protection of the ozone layer is an example of successful international cooperation to address a global environmental problem, and demonstrates that joint action can have a significant positive impact.

## Chapter 21: Chemistry and the Textile Industry

### 21.1 Chemical and Synthetic Fibers

The textile industry uses a wide variety of fibers, from natural fibers such as cotton, wool, and silk, to chemical fibers such as polyester, nylon, and acrylic. Chemical fibers are divided into regenerated fibers (such as viscose and modal, which are obtained from wood cellulose) and synthetic fibers (such as polyester, nylon, and acrylic, which are obtained from petroleum-derived synthetic polymers). Synthetic fibers are the most produced in the world, representing more than 60% of global fiber production.

Polyester is the most produced synthetic fiber in the world, used in the manufacturing of clothing, furniture, carpets, and building materials. Polyester is obtained from polyethylene terephthalate (PET), which is also used in the manufacturing of water bottles. Nylon, invented by DuPont in 1935, was the first completely synthetic fiber. Nylon is strong, elastic, and wear-resistant, and is used in the manufacturing of stockings, sportswear, ropes, and tires. Acrylic is a synthetic fiber that imitates wool, and is used in the manufacturing of sweaters, carpets, and outdoor fabrics.

Elastic fibers, such as spandex (Lycra), are highly elastic synthetic fibers used in the manufacturing of sportswear, underwear, and form-fitting clothing. Spandex can stretch up to 5 times its original length and return to its original shape, making it an ideal fiber for garments that require comfort and mobility. Smart fibers are an emerging area that includes fibers with properties such as thermal regulation, UV protection, antimicrobial properties, and electrical conductivity. These fibers are used in sportswear, medical, and military clothing.

### 21.2 Chemical Dyes

Dyes are chemical substances used to color textile fibers, plastics, foods, and other materials. Dyes are classified into several categories based on their chemical structure and application method. Reactive dyes form covalent bonds with textile fibers, giving them high resistance to washing and light. Azo dyes are the most used in the textile industry, but some of them can release carcinogenic aromatic amines, which has raised concerns about their safety.

Natural dyes are obtained from plant, animal, and mineral sources. The most common plant dyes include indigo (blue, obtained from the plant Indigofera tinctoria), cochineal (red, obtained from an insect that lives on cacti), turmeric (yellow, obtained from the plant Curcuma longa), and madder (red, obtained from the plant Rubia tinctorum). Natural dyes are more sustainable than synthetic dyes, but their resistance to washing and light is generally lower, and their production is more costly and limited in availability.

Textile dyeing is a chemical process that involves applying dyes to textile fibers through various methods, such as solution dyeing (the dye is added to the polymer before fiber extrusion), batch dyeing (the dye is dissolved in water and the fibers are immersed in the solution), pad dyeing (the dye is applied dry and fixed with heat), and printing (the dye is selectively applied to specific areas of the fabric). The choice of dyeing method depends on the type of fiber, the type of dye, and the desired properties of the final product.

### 21.3 Chemical Treatments for Textiles

Textiles receive multiple chemical treatments to improve their properties, such as softness, flame resistance, wrinkle resistance, water resistance, and UV protection. Chemical finishes include stiffening (which gives rigidity to the fabric), mercerizing (which improves the luster and color absorption of cotton), sanforizing (which prevents shrinkage), and laminating (which adds a protective layer to the fabric).

Anti-wrinkle treatments use formaldehyde resins or silicones to prevent wrinkles in garments. However, formaldehyde-based treatments can release formaldehyde residues, which is a respiratory irritant and potentially carcinogenic. Alternative treatments, such as those based on soft silicones and biodegradable polymers, are increasingly popular due to their lower environmental impact. Flame-retardant treatments use phosphorus, nitrogen, and bromine compounds to reduce the flammability of textiles, and are mandatory in garments such as children's pajamas and household textiles.

Antimicrobial treatments use silver, copper, or zinc compounds to inhibit the growth of bacteria and fungi in textiles. These treatments are used in sportswear, medical clothing, and household textiles to reduce odors and extend the lifespan of garments. Hydrophobic treatments use silicones or fluorocarbons to make textiles waterproof but permeable to water vapor. These treatments are used in rainwear, sportswear, and outdoor textiles.

### 21.4 Sustainability in the Textile Industry

The textile industry is one of the most polluting industries in the world, generating enormous amounts of waste, water pollution, and greenhouse gas emissions. Cotton production uses large amounts of pesticides and water, polyester production depends on petroleum, and dyeing and finishing processes use toxic chemicals. Discarded clothing is one of the world's biggest environmental problems, with millions of tons of clothing ending up in landfills each year.

Sustainable fashion is a movement that seeks to reduce the environmental impact of the textile industry, promoting the use of sustainable materials, clean production processes, and circular design. Sustainable materials include organic cotton (produced without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers), recycled polyester (obtained from plastic bottles and used clothing), lyocell (a regenerated fiber produced from wood cellulose in a closed-loop process), and bio-based fibers (such as bamboo cotton and pineapple fibers).

The circular textile economy is a model that seeks to keep materials in use for as long as possible through the design of durable, repairable, and recyclable garments. Sustainable fashion companies, such as Patagonia, Eileen Fisher, and Stella McCartney, are implementing clothing repair, recycling, and reuse programs to reduce the amount of textile waste. Mixed fiber recycling technology (such as polyester-cotton blends) is an active area of research that promises to solve one of the biggest challenges of textile recycling. Sustainability in the textile industry is a challenge that requires cooperation between manufacturers, consumers, governments, and international organizations.
