### Cuneiform Writing

Cuneiform writing was the first form of writing in history and one of the most important contributions of Mesopotamia to human civilization since it allowed the Mesopotamians to record commercial transactions, laws, religious texts, historical narratives, poems, hymns, prayers, and other texts that preserved the knowledge, culture, and history of their civilization. Cuneiform writing developed around 3400 B.C. in the Sumer region in southern Mesopotamia and was used for more than three thousand years until around 100 A.D. Cuneiform writing was characterized by the use of wedge-shaped signs engraved on wet clay tablets that represented words, syllables, or sounds and that were organized in lines and columns. Cuneiform writing evolved from a pictographic system that represented objects and concepts with simple drawings to a phonetic system that represented sounds with abstract signs that allowed writing any word or idea in any language. Cuneiform writing was used to write in several languages including Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian, Elamite, Hurrian, and Hittite which made it one of the most versatile and widespread forms of writing in antiquity. Cuneiform tablets were found at thousands of archaeological sites in Mesopotamia, Turkey, Syria, Iran, and other regions of the Near East and contain millions of texts that include legal documents, religious texts, poems, historical narratives, medical texts, astronomical texts, mathematical texts, agricultural texts, commercial texts, and other types of documents that provide a unique and privileged window into the life, culture, religion, and society of ancient Mesopotamia.

### Hieroglyphic Writing

Hieroglyphic writing was the form of writing of ancient Egypt that was used for more than three thousand years and that was one of the most important contributions of Egypt to human civilization since it allowed the Egyptians to record commercial transactions, laws, religious texts, historical narratives, poems, hymns, prayers, and other texts that preserved the knowledge, culture, and history of their civilization. Hieroglyphic writing developed around 3200 B.C. and was used for more than three thousand years until around 400 A.D. Hieroglyphic writing was characterized by the use of signs that represented objects, animals, people, and concepts that were engraved on monuments, temples, tombs, clay tablets, papyri, and other surfaces. Hieroglyphic writing evolved from a pictographic system that represented objects with simple drawings to a phonetic system that represented sounds with abstract signs that allowed writing any word or idea in any language. Hieroglyphic writing was used to write in the Egyptian language which was the spoken and written language in ancient Egypt for more than three thousand years and that belongs to the Afroasiatic language family. The most important hieroglyphic texts include the Book of the Dead, a religious text that described the journey of the soul through the underworld, the Pyramid Texts, religious texts that were engraved on the walls of the pyramids, the Coffin Texts, religious texts that were engraved on coffins, the Rosetta Stone, a bilingual inscription in hieroglyphics and Greek that made it possible to decipher hieroglyphic writing in 1822, and medical texts such as the Edwin Smith Papyrus and the Ebers Papyrus which contain descriptions of diseases, treatments, and remedies that show the advanced medical knowledge of the Egyptians.

### The Four Great Inventions of China

The four great inventions of China were printing, gunpowder, the compass, and paper which were invented by the Chinese during antiquity and the Middle Ages and that transformed world history by enabling the dissemination of knowledge, long-distance navigation, warfare, construction, and culture. Printing was invented by the Chinese around the 7th century A.D. when block printing was developed which allowed printing texts and images on paper and which was perfected in the 11th century A.D. when Bi Sheng invented movable type printing with clay types which allowed printing texts in a faster and more flexible manner. Gunpowder was invented by the Chinese around the 9th century A.D. when Chinese alchemists discovered that mixing saltpeter, sulfur, and charcoal produced an explosion that could be used to make fireworks, bombs, and weapons. The compass was invented by the Chinese around the 11th century A.D. when Chinese navigators discovered that a magnetic needle suspended in water always pointed toward magnetic north which allowed orientation on the open sea without the visual reference of the coast. Paper was invented by the Chinese around the 2nd century B.C. when Cai Lun, a eunuch of the imperial court, developed a process for making paper from tree bark, rags, and other vegetable materials that allowed producing a writing material cheaper, more flexible, and lighter than papyrus, parchment, or silk. These four inventions were not only products of Chinese genius but also the result of a process of cumulative innovation that developed in the context of a society that valued science, technology, and the search for practical solutions to everyday problems.

## Chapter 8: Agriculture and River Civilizations

### The River as a Source of Life

Agriculture was the foundation of river civilizations since rivers provided fresh water, fertile soil from annual floods, transportation, communication, and energy that allowed the development of agriculture, livestock, fishing, craftsmanship, trade, urbanization, and culture. The agriculture of river civilizations was characterized by the use of irrigation systems such as canals, dikes, reservoirs, weirs, ditches, and water wheels that allowed controlling the water from the rivers and distributing it equitably among the agricultural fields. The agriculture of river civilizations was also characterized by the use of agricultural tools such as plows, sickles, hoes, hooks, and rakes that allowed preparing the soil, sowing, harvesting, and storing agricultural products. The most cultivated agricultural products by river civilizations included wheat, barley, rice, corn, millet, sorghum, legumes, vegetables, fruits, flax, hemp, cotton, and other products that provided food, fiber, raw materials for crafts, and products for trade. The agriculture of river civilizations was not only an economic activity but also a social, cultural, and religious activity that organized community life, defined the calendar, marked festivals, and connected human beings with nature, water, land, and the sky.

### The Code of Hammurabi

The Code of Hammurabi is one of the oldest and most complete sets of laws preserved from antiquity and constitutes a fundamental piece for understanding the legal and social system of Mesopotamia. It was compiled by order of King Hammurabi of Babylon approximately in the year 1754 B.C. and was engraved on a black diorite stele more than two meters tall that was found in 1901-1902 during the excavations of Susa, in present-day Iran, by the French archaeological mission directed by Jacques de Morgan. The stele is crowned by a sculpture representing Hammurabi receiving the laws from the god Shamash, god of justice and the sun, which gave the laws a divine and indisputable legitimacy. The code contains 282 laws that cover a wide variety of topics such as property rights, commercial contracts, family law, labor law, violent crimes, thefts, and the corresponding penalties. The laws are organized in an "eye for an eye" system called lex talionis, which established proportional punishments to the damage caused, although with differences according to the social class of the perpetrator and the victim. For example, if a free man killed another free man, he was punished with death, but if he killed a slave, he only had to pay economic compensation to the owner. The code also regulated the obligations of physicians, architects, and craftsmen, establishing professional responsibilities and penalties for negligence. The importance of the Code of Hammurabi lies in the fact that it demonstrates that Mesopotamian civilizations had already developed a sophisticated concept of social justice, individual responsibility, and regulation of daily life, laying the foundations of legal thought that would reach our days.

### Mesopotamian Religion

Mesopotamian religion was a complex and multifaceted belief system that permeated all aspects of daily life, from agriculture to war, from medicine to architecture. The Mesopotamians conceived a universe dominated by a multiplicity of gods who personified the forces of nature, celestial phenomena, and human activities. The main gods of the Sumerian pantheon included Anu, god of the sky and father of the gods; Enlil, god of the wind, the storm, and agriculture, considered the ruler of the world; Enki (called Ea in Akkadian mythology), god of fresh water, wisdom, and magic; Ninhursag, goddess of the earth and fertility; and Nanna (or Sin), god of the moon. The gods lived in the sky and communicated with humans through prophets, oracles, and divine signs. The temples, called ziggurats, were impressive stepped structures that served as the earthly dwelling of the gods and as centers of administrative worship. Priests and priestesses played a fundamental role in society, performing daily rituals of feeding, worship, and purification of divine statues. Mesopotamian mythology includes one of the oldest known flood narratives, preserved in the Epic of Gilgamesh, where the god Ea warns the man Utnapishtim of the imminent destruction of the world by a flood sent by the god Enlil. This narrative, dating from approximately 1800 B.C., presents notable similarities with the later biblical narrative of Noah's flood, suggesting direct cultural transmission between Mesopotamia and the Hebrew tradition. The Mesopotamians also believed in a dark and somber underworld called Kur, where the souls of the dead led a gloomy and insipid existence, a considerably more pessimistic view than Egyptian conceptions of the afterlife.

### Mesopotamian Architecture: Temples and Ziggurats

Mesopotamian architecture constitutes one of the most impressive expressions of human ingenuity in antiquity and reflects the organizational, religious, and political complexity of these civilizations. The ziggurat was the most emblematic architectural structure of Mesopotamia, a massive stepped temple built with sun-dried mud bricks and externally coated with fired bricks and bitumen. The most famous ziggurat is that of Babylon, known as the Tower of Babel in the biblical tradition, which according to ancient sources reached a height of approximately 91 meters in seven stepped levels, each dedicated to a different god. Ziggurats were not hollow structures but solid, with chambers and rooms built inside and on the upper part, where the cella or sanctuary of the patron god of the city was located. The city of Ur, excavated by Sir Leonard Woolley in the 1920s-1930s, preserves one of the best-preserved ziggurats, the Great Ziggurat of Ur, built by King Ur-Nammu around 2100 B.C. Mesopotamian cities also included colossal royal palaces, such as the Palace of Sennacherib in Nineveh, which contained more than seventy rooms decorated with narrative reliefs depicting scenes of war, hunting, and religious ceremonies. The palaces of the city of Mari, excavated by Andre Parrot in the 1930s, revealed more than three hundred rooms and a sophisticated sewer system that demonstrates the high level of hydraulic engineering reached by these civilizations. Mesopotamian architects developed innovations such as the arch, the vault, and the dome, constructive elements that would be perfected by the Romans and that remain fundamental in current architecture. ### Science and Astronomy in Mesopotamia

The Mesopotamians developed extraordinary scientific knowledge that anticipated in many aspects the discoveries of later civilizations. In astronomy, the priest-astronomers made systematic observations of the night sky for centuries, recording the positions of stars, planets, eclipses, and other celestial phenomena on cuneiform tablets that constitute the oldest astronomical records in the world. They developed a mathematical astronomical system that allowed predicting lunar and solar eclipses with remarkable precision, identified the five planets visible to the naked eye (Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn) and developed the celestial coordinate system that would be adopted by Greek astronomers. The Babylonians divided the circle into 360 degrees, the hour into 60 minutes, and the minute into 60 seconds, a sexagesimal system that is still used today to measure angles, time, and geographic coordinates. In mathematics, the Babylonians developed a positional numeral system in base 60, solved quadratic equations, calculated square roots with considerable precision, knew what we today call the Pythagorean theorem more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born, and developed multiplication, division, and power tables that are preserved on cuneiform tablets. The Plimpton 322 tablet, dated approximately to 1800 B.C., contains a table of Pythagorean triples that demonstrates advanced mathematical knowledge. In medicine, Mesopotamian physicians combined empirical practices with religious incantations and exorcism rituals, classified diseases into three categories (from the god, from the demon, or natural) and used a pharmacopoeia that included hundreds of plants, minerals, and animal substances. ### The Assyrian Empire

The Assyrian Empire was one of the most powerful, expansive, and organized empires of all antiquity, dominating Mesopotamia, the Levant, parts of Anatolia, and Egypt for more than five centuries. Assyria developed initially in the city of Assur, located on the western bank of the Tigris River in northern Mesopotamia, and reached its maximum splendor between the 9th and 7th centuries B.C. under sovereigns such as Ashurnasirpal II (883-859 B.C.), Shalmaneser V (727-722 B.C.), Sargon II (722-705 B.C.), Sennacherib (705-681 B.C.), Esarhaddon (681-669 B.C.), and Ashurbanipal (669-627 B.C.). The Assyrians were known for their formidable military apparatus, which included heavy and light infantry, cavalry, war chariots, and an extraordinarily effective siege machinery that employed ramps, battering rams, siege towers, and mining tunnels. The palace reliefs of Nineveh, preserved in the British Museum, depict in detail scenes of battles, sieges, royal hunts, and bloody sports that provide a vivid picture of Assyrian society. The Assyrian Empire was also notable for its administrative system, which divided the territory into provinces governed by royal officials, established a network of roads and relay stations for rapid communication, and maintained an organized tribute system. Ashurbanipal built in Nineveh one of the first libraries in the world, which contained more than thirty thousand cuneiform tablets that included literary, scientific, religious, medical, and administrative texts, constituting an encyclopedia of Mesopotamian knowledge. The library of Ashurbanipal was discovered in 1850 by the British archaeologist Austen Henry Layard and provided invaluable information about the culture, history, and science of Mesopotamia. ### The Pyramids and Funerary Architecture

The pyramids of Egypt are the most recognizable and emblematic architectural structures of the ancient world, and constitute one of the most extraordinary engineering achievements in human history. Imhotep designed a structure of six stepped levels that reached 62 meters in height, a revolutionary innovation that transformed traditional funerary mastabas into stepped platforms that evoked the primordial mountain of creation. The evolution of pyramids continued with the pyramids of Meidum, of Dahshur (the Bent Pyramid and the Red Pyramid), until culminating in the three pyramids of Giza, built during the 4th Dynasty (approximately 2580-2510 B.C.). The Great Pyramid of Giza, also known as the Pyramid of Khufu, is the largest and oldest of the seven wonders of the ancient world and the only one that is practically intact. With an original height of 146.5 meters and a base of 230 meters on each side, the Great Pyramid is composed of approximately 2.3 million blocks of limestone and granite, with an average weight of 2.5 tons each, which were transported and placed with astonishing precision that has baffled engineers for centuries. ### Egyptian Religion

The religion of ancient Egypt was an extraordinarily complex and refined system of beliefs that survived for more than three thousand years and profoundly influenced the religious traditions of the ancient Mediterranean. The Egyptians conceived a universe governed by a vast assembly of gods and goddesses whose stories, conflicts, and alliances explained natural phenomena, the seasons of the year, the floods of the Nile, and social order. Among the main gods were Ra (or Re), the sun god who sailed across the sky in a solar barque and who was considered the father of all pharaohs; Osiris, god of death and resurrection, who according to Egyptian mythology was murdered and resurrected by his wife Isis, a myth that became the center of one of the most popular religions of the ancient world; Isis, goddess of magic, motherhood, and protection, whose cult spread throughout the Mediterranean until imperial Rome; Horus, god of the sky and royalty, represented as a falcon or as a man with a falcon's head, who was the protector of the pharaoh; Anubis, god of mummification and tombs, represented as a black jackal; Thot, god of writing, wisdom, and the moon; and Hathor, goddess of love, beauty, and music. Egyptian religion included an elaborate theology that encompassed cosmogonies (myths of the creation of the universe), eschatologies (beliefs about life after death), and daily rituals that maintained the cosmic order called Maat, a concept that encompassed justice, truth, balance, and divine order. The Book of the Dead, a collection of spells and magical formulas written on papyrus and placed in tombs, guided the deceased through the dangers of the underworld toward the Judgment of Osiris, where their heart was weighed against the feather of justice on a scale. ### Medicine in Ancient Egypt

The medicine of ancient Egypt was one of the most advanced and systematic in the ancient world, combining empirical observational knowledge with magical-religious practices that reflected the Egyptian conception of diseases as manifestations of divine will or the influence of evil spirits. The most important medical papyri preserved include the Edwin Smith Papyrus, dated approximately to 1600 B.C. but based on much older texts, which contains an extraordinarily detailed surgical treatise with forty-eight clinical cases describing traumatic injuries, fractures, dislocations, and war wounds, along with diagnoses, prognoses, and treatments for each case. This papyrus is notable for its rational and empirical approach, since it describes procedures such as wound suturing, fracture immobilization with linen splints soaked in resin, wound cauterization, and even cranial trepanation, all without resorting to magic in most cases. The Ebers Papyrus, dated approximately to 1550 B.C., is one of the most extensive medical texts of antiquity, with more than seven hundred recipes and remedies that cover diseases of the digestive, respiratory, circulatory, urinary, and reproductive systems, as well as conditions of the skin, eyes, ears, and teeth. The Egyptians used a pharmacopoeia that included more than seven hundred substances of plant, animal, and mineral origin, among which were aloe vera, myrrh, incense, honey, salt, vinegar, olive oil, and various medicinal plants. The Indus Valley civilization possesses a writing system that constitutes one of the greatest enigmas of modern archaeology, since despite more than a century of research, the Indus script still has not been deciphered. ## Chapter 16: Conclusions

### The Enduring Lesson of Rivers

River civilizations teach us that the relationship between human beings and water has been and continues to be the determining factor in the development of societies. From the earliest Neolithic agricultural villages to the great metropolises of the 21st century, the ability of societies to manage water resources has determined their prosperity, political stability, and cultural longevity. The civilizations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, and China demonstrated that the mastery of hydraulic engineering, agricultural planning, and administration of shared resources are the foundations upon which complex and enduring societies are built. The legacy of these civilizations is not only archaeological or historical: it is alive and current in every irrigation canal, every sewer system, every law regulating water use, and every international treaty that seeks to manage shared river resources. Understanding how ancient civilizations adapted to climate changes, managed floods and droughts, and built irrigation systems that transformed deserts into gardens is fundamental for facing the environmental challenges of the 21st century. The rivers that gave origin to human civilization continue to flow, carrying with them the memory of thousands of years of human ingenuity, perseverance, and adaptation, and their preservation is a responsibility that we must assume as heirs of a legacy that belongs to all humanity.
