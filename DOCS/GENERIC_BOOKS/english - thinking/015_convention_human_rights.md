# The European Convention on Human Rights

## Chapter 1: History and Context

### 1.1 Origins of the Convention

The European Convention on Human Rights was adopted on 4 November 1950 in Rome by the member states of the Council of Europe, as a response to the atrocities committed during the Second World War. The Convention was born from the will of European countries to ensure that the horrors of the past would not be repeated, establishing an international legal framework for the protection of human rights. The adoption of the Convention was a historic milestone that marked the beginning of the European system of human rights protection.

The historical context in which the Convention was adopted was decisive for its content and its structure. The Second World War had left a legacy of massive human rights violations, including the Holocaust, the bombing of civilian populations, concentration and extermination camps, and other atrocities. European leaders recognised the need to establish an international system of human rights protection that would prevent governments from committing violations of fundamental rights.

The Council of Europe, founded in 1949, was the institutional framework in which the Convention was drafted and adopted. The Council of Europe had the aim of promoting democracy, human rights and the Rule of Law in Europe, and the adoption of the Convention was one of its first and most important initiatives. The Convention was drafted by a committee of experts that included jurists from the member states of the Council of Europe, and was adopted by the Consultative Assembly of the Council of Europe.

The Convention entered into force on 3 September 1953, and since then has been ratified by all 46 member states of the Council of Europe. The Convention has proved to be one of the most successful international human rights instruments in history, and has laid the foundations for the development of human rights protection systems throughout the world. The influence of the Convention extends beyond Europe, as it has inspired the adoption of similar international instruments in other regions of the world.

### 1.2 The Council of Europe and the protection of human rights

The Council of Europe is the international organisation responsible for the adoption and supervision of the European Convention on Human Rights. Founded in 1949, the Council of Europe has the aim of promoting democracy, human rights and the Rule of Law in Europe. The Council of Europe brings together 46 member states that share common values of democracy, human rights and the Rule of Law, and that have committed themselves to respecting the fundamental rights of all persons.

The Council of Europe plays a fundamental role in the promotion and protection of human rights in Europe. The Council has adopted a series of international instruments in the field of human rights, which include the European Convention on Human Rights, its additional protocols and other conventions in specific areas, such as torture, discrimination and the protection of personal data. These instruments establish common standards for the protection of human rights and create supervision mechanisms that allow their compliance to be ensured.

The Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe is the executive body of the Council and has the responsibility of supervising the execution of the judgments of the European Court of Human Rights. The Committee of Ministers is composed of the foreign ministers of the member states, or their representatives, and meets periodically to debate matters related to human rights and to supervise the execution of the judgments of the Court. The Committee of Ministers also adopts recommendations to the member states on the improvement of human rights protection.

The Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe is the parliamentary assembly of the Council, composed of representatives from the national parliaments of the member states. The Assembly plays a supervisory and recommendation role in matters of human rights, and adopts reports and resolutions on the situation of human rights in the member states. The Assembly also supervises the execution of the recommendations of the Committee of Ministers and formulates recommendations on the improvement of human rights protection.

### 1.3 Ratification and entry into force

The ratification of the European Convention on Human Rights is the process by which a state manifests its consent to be bound by the Convention. Ratification is a solemn act that involves the state's commitment to respecting the rights recognised in the Convention and submitting to the jurisdiction of the European Court of Human Rights. The ratification of the Convention is a prerequisite for accession to the Council of Europe and for participation in the European system of human rights protection.

The ratification process of the Convention varies according to the constitutional legislation of each state. In some states, ratification requires the approval of the national parliament, while in other states, ratification is the responsibility of the executive power. Ratification is carried out by depositing the instrument of ratification with the Secretary General of the Council of Europe, who is the depositary of the Convention. Once the instrument of ratification has been deposited, the Convention enters into force for the ratifying state.

The Convention entered into force on 3 September 1953, following ratification by a sufficient number of states. Since then, the Convention has been ratified by all 46 member states of the Council of Europe, which guarantees uniform protection of human rights throughout Europe. The ratification of the Convention is a serious commitment that involves the obligation to adapt national legislation to be compatible with the Convention and to ensure that national authorities respect the rights recognised in the Convention.

The ratification of the Convention also involves the acceptance of the jurisdiction of the European Court of Human Rights. The ratifying states accept the competence of the Court to receive and examine applications from individual persons, non-governmental organisations and groups of individuals alleging a violation of their rights by a state. This acceptance of the jurisdiction of the Court is a fundamental commitment that enables the effective protection of the rights recognised in the Convention.

### 1.4 Evolution of the Convention over time

The evolution of the European Convention on Human Rights over time reflects the adaptation of the Convention to changes in the social, political and technological environment. Since its adoption in 1950, the Convention has undergone a series of reforms and adaptations that have expanded its scope and improved its effectiveness. These developments include the adoption of additional protocols, the expansion of the number of protected rights and the improvement of supervision mechanisms.

The adoption of additional protocols has been one of the main avenues of evolution of the Convention. Protocol No. 1, adopted in 1952, added rights such as the right to property, the right to education and the right to free elections. Protocol No. 4, adopted in 1963, added rights such as the prohibition of detention for debt and freedom of movement. Protocol No. 6, adopted in 1983, abolished the death penalty in peacetime. Protocol No. 12, adopted in 2000, generalized the prohibition of discrimination. These protocols have progressively expanded the catalogue of rights recognised by the Convention.

The case law of the European Court of Human Rights has been another fundamental factor in the evolution of the Convention. The Court has interpreted the provisions of the Convention in a dynamic manner, adapting them to changes in the social and technological environment. The Court has recognised rights not explicitly provided for in the Convention, such as the right to privacy of communications, the right to the protection of personal data and the right to information. This dynamic interpretation has allowed the Convention to remain relevant and effective in a constantly changing world.

The enlargement of the number of member states of the Council of Europe has been another factor in the evolution of the Convention. Since its adoption with 10 member states, the Convention has been ratified by 46 states, including the states of Eastern Europe that joined the Council of Europe after the fall of the Berlin Wall. This enlargement has posed a challenge for the human rights protection system, as the new member states had different legal traditions and levels of human rights protection. However, the enlargement has also strengthened the protection system by extending the coverage of the Convention to the entire European continent.

## Chapter 2: The Protected Rights

### 2.1 Right to life

The right to life is the most basic and essential fundamental right recognised by the European Convention on Human Rights. Article 2 of the Convention establishes that the right to life of every person is protected by law. No one shall be intentionally deprived of life, save in the execution of a sentence capital passed by a court in case of an offence punishable by this penalty under the law. The right to life is an absolute right that admits no exceptions, and its protection is the foundation of all the other rights recognised by the Convention.

The protection of the right to life implies the obligation of states to adopt measures to prevent the death of persons, both through the action of public authorities and through the action of private individuals. States have the obligation to investigate all suspicious deaths, including deaths caused by the security forces, deaths in custody and violent deaths. The investigation of suspicious deaths is fundamental for the protection of the right to life and for the prevention of impunity.

The right to life also raises questions related to euthanasia and assisted suicide. The European Court of Human Rights has addressed these questions in a series of judgments, recognising that states have a margin of appreciation to regulate these questions. The Court has established that euthanasia and assisted suicide are not protected by Article 2 of the Convention, but that states must ensure that persons who wish to put an end to their lives receive adequate and respectful care.

Article 2 of the Convention also regulates the use of lethal force by the authorities. Article 2(2) establishes that death shall not be regarded as inflicted intentionally when it results from the use of force which is absolutely necessary: (a) in defence of any person from unlawful violence; (b) in order to effect a lawful arrest; (c) in action lawfully taken for the purpose of quelling a riot or insurrection. This provision establishes strict limits on the use of lethal force by the authorities and ensures that the use of force is proportionate and necessary.

### 2.2 Prohibition of torture and inhuman treatment

The prohibition of torture and of inhuman or degrading treatment is one of the most fundamental rights recognised by the Convention. Article 3 of the Convention establishes that no one shall be subjected to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment. This prohibition is absolute and unconditional, which means that it admits no exceptions or justifications, regardless of the circumstances. The prohibition of torture and of inhuman or degrading treatment is a right that reflects the inherent dignity of every human being and that lies at the heart of the human rights protection system.

The concept of torture is defined as the intentional infliction of severe physical or suffering on a person for purposes such as obtaining information, punishment or intimidation. The concept of inhuman treatment refers to treatment that causes severe suffering, even if it does not reach the level of torture. The concept of degrading treatment refers to treatment that humbles or degrades a person, undermining their dignity. The distinction between these concepts is often subtle and depends on the circumstances of each case, such as the severity of the suffering, the intention of the perpetrator and the circumstances of the victim.

The prohibition of torture and of inhuman or degrading treatment has important implications in the field of criminal justice. States have the obligation to ensure that detained or imprisoned persons are not subjected to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment, and to investigate and punish cases of torture or inhuman treatment. This obligation extends to all forms of detention, including police detention, pre-trial detention and imprisonment. The European Court has developed extensive case law on detention conditions, establishing standards for personal space, sanitary facilities, food, medical care and contact with the outside world.

The prohibition of torture and of inhuman or degrading treatment also has implications in the field of asylum and migration policy. States have the obligation not to expel persons to countries where they run the risk of being subjected to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment. This obligation derives from the principle of non-refoulement, which is a fundamental principle of international refugee law and has been incorporated into the Convention. The European Court has developed significant case law on the principle of non-refoulement, establishing the criteria for assessing the risk of torture or inhuman treatment in the country of destination.

### 2.3 Liberty and security

The right to liberty and security is a fundamental right of the Convention that protects persons against arbitrary detention and imprisonment. Article 5 of the Convention establishes that everyone has the right to liberty and security. No one shall be deprived of his liberty save in the following cases and in accordance with a procedure prescribed by law: lawful detention after conviction by a competent court, non-compliance with a lawful order of a court, arrest or detention for the purpose of bringing a person before a competent legal authority on reasonable suspicion of having committed an offence, detention of a minor for educational supervision, detention for the prevention of the spreading of infectious diseases, detention of a person who is drunk or on drugs, arrest or detention with a view to deportation or extradition.

Article 5 of the Convention also establishes a series of guarantees for detained persons. Every detained person must be informed, at the time of detention, of the reasons for his detention and of any charges against him. Every detained person must be brought promptly before a judge or other officer authorised by law to exercise judicial functions. Every detained person has the right to trial within a reasonable time or to release pending proceedings. These guarantees are fundamental for the protection against arbitrary detention and for the guarantee of a fair trial.

The European Court of Human Rights has developed extensive case law on the right to liberty and security, establishing criteria for assessing the legality of detention, its duration and the conditions in which it is carried out. The Court has established that detention must be prescribed by law, must pursue a legitimate aim and must be necessary and proportionate in a democratic society. The Court has also established that detained persons have the right to a speedy judicial review of the lawfulness of their detention.

### 2.4 Right to a fair trial

The right to a fair trial is one of the most important rights of the Convention that ensures that all persons enjoy fair treatment before the courts. Article 6 of the Convention establishes that everyone has the right to have his cause heard fairly and publicly by a tribunal established by law. The right to a fair trial includes the right to be heard, the right to a defence, the right to the presumption of innocence and the right to an impartial judge.

Article 6(1) of the Convention establishes that everyone has the right to have his cause heard fairly, within a reasonable time and publicly, by an independent and impartial tribunal established by law. The tribunal must be independent of the executive and legislative powers, and impartial in the sense that it has no personal interest in the outcome of the proceedings. The right to an independent and impartial tribunal is fundamental for the confidence of citizens in the judicial system and for the legitimacy of judicial decisions.

Article 6(2) of the Convention establishes that everyone charged with an offence is presumed innocent until his guilt has been legally established. The presumption of innocence imposes on public authorities the obligation to treat charged persons as innocent until their guilt is proved. This principle implies that authorities cannot prejudge the guilt of charged persons, that charged persons are not obliged to prove their innocence and that the burden of proof lies with the prosecution.

Article 6(3) of the Convention establishes the minimum rights of the defence in criminal proceedings. These rights include the right to be informed of the nature and cause of the charge, the right to have adequate time and facilities for the preparation of a defence, the right to legal assistance of one's own choosing, the right to examine witnesses for the prosecution and to obtain the summoning and examination of witnesses for the defence, the right to the free assistance of an interpreter if one cannot understand or speak the language used in court. These rights are fundamental for the equality of arms between the parties in a criminal trial and for the guarantee of a fair trial.

## Chapter 3: The European Court of Human Rights

### 3.1 Structure and organisation of the Court

The European Court of Human Rights is the judicial body responsible for the interpretation and application of the European Convention on Human Rights. It was established in 1959 and is based in Strasbourg, France. The Court is composed of a number of judges equal to the number of states party to the Convention, which currently stands at 46. The judges are elected by the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe from a list of three candidates nominated by each state party.

The organisation of the Court is structured around different bodies and formations. The Plenary Court is the formation that brings together all the judges and meets for matters of institutional importance. The Sections are formations of seven judges responsible for the examination of cases. The Committees are formations of three judges that may declare an application inadmissible or accept it for examination. The Chambers are formations of seven judges that examine cases that are not decided by the Committees or the Sections.

The President of the Court is elected by the judges of the Court for a period of three years, renewable once. The President performs a role of representation and direction of the Court, and presides over the sessions of the Plenary Court and the Grand Chamber. The Court also has a Registrar, who is responsible for the administration of the Court and the management of cases.

The Court has a hierarchical structure that allows for the efficient distribution of cases. The Committees are the lowest formation and are responsible for the initial examination of applications. The Sections examine cases that are not decided by the Committees. The Chambers examine the most important cases. The Grand Chamber is the highest formation and examines cases of special importance or that raise fundamental legal questions. This hierarchical structure allows the Court to manage its workload efficiently and ensure the quality of its decisions.

### 3.2 Procedure before the Court

The procedure before the European Court of Human Rights is structured in several phases that include admissibility, examination of the merits and judgment. The procedure begins with the filing of an application before the Court by an individual person, a non-governmental organisation or a group of individuals alleging a violation of their rights recognised by the Convention. The application must be filed within a period of four months from the date of the final internal decision.

The first phase of the procedure is the examination of the admissibility of the application. The Court examines whether the application meets the admissibility requirements established in the Convention. These requirements include the exhaustion of domestic remedies, the filing of the application within the prescribed time limit, the absence of abuse of the right of application and the absence of a significant violation of the rights recognised by the Convention. If the application is declared inadmissible, the Court rules on its inadmissibility and the proceedings are terminated.

If the application is declared admissible, the Court proceeds to examine the merits of the case. The examination of the merits includes the collection of evidence, the submissions of the parties, the reports of the governments and the opinions of third parties. The Court examines whether there has been a violation of the Convention and, if so, adopts a judgment in which it declares the violation and, where appropriate, awards just satisfaction to the victim.

The judgment of the Court is binding on the states party to the Convention. Article 46 of the Convention establishes that the states party undertake to abide by the final judgments of the Court in the cases to which they are parties. The execution of judgments is supervised by the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe, which verifies that states adopt the necessary measures to comply with the judgments. The execution of judgments is fundamental for the effectiveness of the human rights protection system and for the confidence of citizens in the Court.

### 3.3 Case law of the Court

The case law of the European Court of Human Rights is one of the richest and most developed in the world in the field of human rights. The Court has adopted thousands of judgments that have interpreted and developed the provisions of the Convention, establishing principles and standards that guide the protection of human rights in Europe. The case law of the Court has had a significant impact on the legislation and practice of the states party to the Convention.

The Court has developed case law on the right to life that has established the standards for the protection of this right. The Court has established that states have the obligation to investigate all suspicious deaths, to prevent the death of persons and to ensure that those responsible for unlawful deaths are tried and convicted. The Court has also established standards for the use of lethal force by the authorities, which include necessity, proportionality and legality.

The Court has developed case law on the prohibition of torture that has established the standards for the protection of this right. The Court has established that the prohibition of torture is absolute and unconditional, and admits no exceptions or justifications. The Court has also established standards for detention conditions, which include personal space, sanitary facilities, food, medical care and contact with the outside world. These standards have had a significant impact on the improvement of detention conditions in the states party.

The Court has developed case law on the right to a fair trial that has established the standards for the protection of this right. The Court has established that courts must be independent and impartial, that the parties have the right to be heard and to present evidence, and that judicial proceedings must last a reasonable time. The Court has also established standards for the rights of the defence in criminal proceedings, which include the right to legal assistance, the right to examine witnesses and the right to the presumption of innocence.

### 3.4 Committee of Ministers and execution of judgments

The Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe is the body responsible for supervising the execution of the judgments of the European Court of Human Rights. The Committee of Ministers is composed of the foreign ministers of the states party to the Convention, or their representatives, and meets periodically to debate matters related to human rights and to supervise the execution of judgments. The supervision of the execution of judgments is a fundamental function of the Committee of Ministers, as it ensures that states adopt the necessary measures to remedy violations of the Convention.

The procedure for the execution of judgments begins with the adoption of the judgment by the Court. Once the judgment has been adopted, the Committee of Ministers examines the measures taken by the condemned state to comply with the judgment. The Committee of Ministers may request information from the state on the measures taken, may formulate recommendations and may adopt resolutions on the execution of the judgment. The Committee of Ministers supervises the execution of the judgment until it considers that the state has adopted all necessary measures.

The measures that states must adopt to comply with the Court's judgments include the amendment of national legislation to be compatible with the Convention, the compensation of victims of violations, the investigation and punishment of those responsible for the violations and the adoption of individual and general measures to prevent future violations. These measures are fundamental for the reparation of the damage caused to victims and for the prevention of future violations of the Convention.

The effectiveness of the execution of the Court's judgments has been the subject of debate. Some argue that the execution of judgments is slow and ineffective, and that states do not always adopt the necessary measures to comply with judgments. Others argue that the execution of judgments is satisfactory and that states generally comply with the Court's judgments. The Committee of Ministers has adopted measures to improve the effectiveness of the execution of judgments, such as the creation of simplified procedures for repetitive judgments and the adoption of follow-up measures for the most important judgments.

## Chapter 4: The Additional Protocols

### 4.1 Overview of additional protocols

The additional protocols to the European Convention on Human Rights are legal instruments that expand or modify the provisions of the Convention. Since the adoption of the Convention in 1950, sixteen additional protocols have been adopted that have expanded the catalogue of recognised rights, modified the procedures of the Court and adapted the Convention to changes in the environment. The additional protocols are an integral part of the Council of Europe's human rights protection system.

Protocol No. 1, adopted in 1952, is the most important and most widely ratified protocol. This protocol added rights such as the right to property, the right to education and the right to free elections. Protocol No. 1 has been ratified by all states party to the Convention, except one, which reflects its fundamental importance for the protection of human rights. Protocol No. 1 has been the basis of significant case law of the Court on the right to property and the right to education.

Protocol No. 4, adopted in 1963, added rights such as the prohibition of detention for debt, freedom of movement, the prohibition of collective expulsions and the right not to be expelled from one's own state. This protocol is particularly important for the protection of the rights of migrants and stateless persons. The Court has developed significant case law on the provisions of this protocol, particularly in relation to freedom of movement and the prohibition of collective expulsions.

Protocol No. 6, adopted in 1983, abolished the death penalty in peacetime. This protocol reflects the commitment of the states party to the Convention to the abolition of the death penalty, which is considered to be inhuman and degrading treatment. Protocol No. 13, adopted in 2002, abolished the death penalty in all circumstances, including in time of war. These protocols have contributed significantly to the abolition of the death penalty in Europe.

Protocol No. 12, adopted in 2000, generalized the prohibition of discrimination. This protocol establishes that the enjoyment of the rights and freedoms recognised in the Convention must be ensured without discrimination on any ground, such as sex, race, colour, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, association with a national minority, property, birth or any other status. Protocol No. 12 has expanded protection against discrimination beyond the scope of the Convention.

### 4.2 Protocol 1: Right to property, education and elections

Protocol No. 1 to the European Convention on Human Rights is one of the most important and most widely ratified of the additional protocols. Adopted in 1952, this protocol added to the Convention three fundamental rights: the right to property, the right to education and the right to free elections. These rights are essential for the functioning of a democratic society and for the protection of the dignity and freedom of persons.

Article 1 of Protocol No. 1 establishes that every natural or legal person is entitled to the peaceful enjoyment of his possessions. No one shall be deprived of his possessions except in the public interest and in the conditions provided for by law. This provision protects the right to property as a fundamental right that must be respected by the public authorities. The Court has interpreted the right to property broadly, including not only physical property but also proprietary interests, legitimate expectations and contractual rights.

Article 2 of Protocol No. 1 establishes that no state shall deny the right to education. In the exercise of the functions which it assumes in the field of education and teaching, the State shall respect the right of parents to ensure such education and teaching in conformity with their own religious and philosophical convictions. This provision protects the right to education as a fundamental right that must be guaranteed by the public authorities, and also recognises the right of parents to choose the education of their children.

Article 3 of Protocol No. 1 establishes that the states party undertake to hold free elections at reasonable intervals by secret ballot, under conditions which will ensure the free expression of the opinion of the people in the choice of the legislature. This provision protects the right to free elections as a fundamental right that is essential for the functioning of democracy. The Court has interpreted the right to free elections broadly, including the right to the free expression of the opinion of the people, the right to equal opportunities for candidates and the right to transparency of the electoral process.

### 4.3 Protocols on substantive rights

The protocols on substantive rights are those that add new rights to the Convention's catalogue or that expand existing rights. These protocols include Protocol No. 4 (freedom of movement and prohibition of collective expulsions), Protocol No. 6 (abolition of the death penalty in peacetime), Protocol No. 7 (additional procedural rights), Protocol No. 12 (general prohibition of discrimination) and Protocol No. 13 (abolition of the death penalty in all circumstances).

Protocol No. 7, adopted in 1984, added additional procedural rights to the Convention. Article 1 establishes the right to reciprocity in the event of expulsion, Article 2 establishes the right of appeal in criminal matters, Article 3 establishes the right to compensation in the event of a miscarriage of justice, and Article 4 establishes the right not to be tried or punished twice for the same offence. These procedural rights are fundamental for the guarantee of a fair trial and for the protection of the rights of citizens.

Protocol No. 14, adopted in 2004, introduced important reforms to the European Court of Human Rights. This protocol created the figure of a single judge for the admissibility of applications, introduced the possibility of declaring repetitive applications inadmissible and strengthened the supervision of the execution of judgments by the Committee of Ministers. These reforms aim to improve the efficiency of the Court and reduce the accumulated backlog of cases.

Protocol No. 15, adopted in 2013, introduced amendments to the Convention to reinforce the principle of subsidiarity and the margin of appreciation of states. This protocol added a preamble to the Convention that recognises the role of national courts in the protection of human rights and the margin of appreciation of states. Protocol No. 15 also reduced the time limit for the filing of applications from six to four months, with the aim of improving the efficiency of the Court.

### 4.4 Protocols on procedures

The protocols on procedures are those that modify the procedures of the European Court of Human Rights. These protocols include Protocol No. 2 (competence of the Court to interpret the Convention), Protocol No. 3 (revision of judgments), Protocol No. 8 (certificates of judgments), Protocol No. 9 (right of states to refer cases to the Court), Protocol No. 10 (renunciation of the right of application), Protocol No. 11 (reform of the control system), Protocol No. 14 (reform of the Court) and Protocol No. 16 (advisory opinions).

Protocol No. 11, adopted in 1994 and in force since 1998, was one of the most important reforms of the Court. This protocol restructured the control system of the Convention, replacing the commission and tribunal system with a single and permanent Court. Protocol No. 11 also made the jurisdiction of the Court compulsory for all states party, which eliminated the possibility of states objecting to the jurisdiction of the Court. These reforms had a significant impact on the effectiveness of the human rights protection system.

Protocol No. 16, adopted in 2013, introduced the possibility for the supreme courts of the states party to request advisory opinions from the European Court of Human Rights. Advisory opinions are not binding, but provide guidance to national courts on the interpretation of the Convention. Protocol No. 16 aims to strengthen the dialogue between the Court and national courts, and to improve the understanding and application of the Convention at national level.

## Chapter 5: The Relevant Case Law

### 5.1 Foundational judgments

The foundational judgments of the European Court of Human Rights are those that have established fundamental principles for the interpretation and application of the Convention. These judgments have laid the foundations of the Court's case law and have had a significant impact on the protection of human rights in Europe. Some of the most important foundational judgments include Handyside v. United Kingdom, Golder v. United Kingdom, Marckx v. Belgium, Soering v. United Kingdom and Tyrer v. United Kingdom.

The judgment in Handyside v. United Kingdom (1976) is one of the most cited judgments of the Court. In this judgment, the Court established the principle that the Convention is a living instrument that must be interpreted in the light of present-day conditions. The Court also established the concept of the margin of appreciation, which recognises that the states party have a margin of discretion to implement the rights of the Convention in their domestic legal orders. The margin of appreciation is a fundamental principle of the Court's case law that allows for a balance between the uniform protection of human rights and the diversity of legal traditions of the states party.

The judgment in Golder v. United Kingdom (1975) established that the right of access to a court is implicit in Article 6 of the Convention (right to a fair trial). The Court established that the right to a fair trial includes the right of access to a court to enforce civil rights. This judgment was fundamental for the development of the right of access to justice in Europe.

The judgment in Marckx v. Belgium (1979) established that the Convention protects family life and that national legislations must not discriminate between children born within and outside of marriage. This judgment was fundamental for the development of the right to equal treatment in family relations and had a significant impact on the legislation of the states party.

The judgment in Soering v. United Kingdom (1989) established the principle of non-refoulement in the context of the Convention. The Court established that Article 3 of the Convention (prohibition of torture and inhuman treatment) prohibits the extradition or expulsion of a person to a country where he or she runs a real risk of being subjected to torture or inhuman treatment. This judgment was fundamental for the protection of the rights of migrants and asylum seekers.

### 5.2 Judgments on civil and political rights

The judgments on civil and political rights are those that interpret and apply the provisions of the Convention relating to civil and political rights. These judgments cover areas such as freedom of expression, freedom of assembly, protection of private life, the right to vote and the prohibition of discrimination. The judgments on civil and political rights are fundamental for the protection of the fundamental rights of citizens in Europe.

Freedom of expression is an area of case law particularly developed by the Court. The Court has established that freedom of expression is one of the essential foundations of a democratic society and is protected by Article 10 of the Convention. The Court has established that restrictions on freedom of expression must be necessary in a democratic society and proportionate to the aim pursued. The Court has developed significant case law on the protection of journalists, the protection of artistic expression and the protection of political expression.

The protection of private life is another important area of case law. The Court has established that Article 8 of the Convention protects private life, family life, the home and correspondence. The Court has interpreted the concept of private life broadly, including sexual identity, sexual orientation, gender identity, health, physical appearance and reputation. The Court has also established that restrictions on private life must be necessary in a democratic society and proportionate to the aim pursued.

The prohibition of discrimination is another important area of case law. The Court has established that Article 14 of the Convention prohibits discrimination on any ground, such as sex, race, colour, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, association with a national minority, property, birth or any other status. The Court has established that indirect discrimination is also prohibited and that differences in treatment must be justified by objective and reasonable reasons.

### 5.3 Judgments on social rights

The judgments on social rights are those that interpret and apply the provisions of the Convention relating to social rights, such as the right to education, the right to the protection of health, the right to housing and the right to work. Although the Convention does not expressly recognise these rights, the Court has interpreted them in the context of other articles of the Convention, such as Article 2 (right to life), Article 3 (prohibition of torture) and Article 8 (right to private life).

The right to education has been interpreted by the Court in the context of Article 2 of Protocol No. 1, which establishes that no state shall deny the right to education. The Court has established that the right to education includes the right to access quality education, the right to equal educational opportunities and the right to academic freedom. The Court has also established that states have the obligation to ensure that education is compatible with democratic values and human rights.

The protection of health has been interpreted by the Court in the context of Article 2 (right to life) and Article 3 (prohibition of torture). The Court has established that states have the obligation to guarantee the right to the protection of health, which includes access to adequate health services, protection against health risks and the prevention of disease. The Court has also established that detention conditions must be compatible with the right to the protection of health.

The right to housing has been interpreted by the Court in the context of Article 8 (right to private life). The Court has established that the right to private life includes the right to housing, which ensures that persons have a place to live with dignity. The Court has also established that states have the obligation to guarantee the right to housing, particularly for vulnerable persons, such as the homeless, refugees and persons with disabilities.

### 5.4 Judgments on economic and social rights

The judgments on economic and social rights are those that interpret and apply the provisions of the Convention relating to economic and social rights, such as the right to property, the right to work, social protection and equal treatment in employment. Although the Convention does not expressly recognise the majority of these rights, the Court has interpreted them in the context of other articles of the Convention, such as Protocol No. 1 (right to property) and Article 14 (prohibition of discrimination).

The right to property is a fundamental economic right recognised by Article 1 of Protocol No. 1. The Court has established that the right to property protects the peaceful enjoyment of possessions and that deprivations of property must be prescribed by law, pursue a public interest aim and be proportionate. The Court has also established that the right to property includes proprietary interests, legitimate expectations and contractual rights.

## Chapter 9: The Protected Rights in Detail

### 9.1 Right to life (Article 2)

The right to life is the most fundamental human right recognised by the European Convention on Human Rights, since without the right to life there can be no effective exercise of any other right. Article 2 of the Convention establishes that the right to life of every person is protected by law, and that no one shall be intentionally deprived of his life, save in the execution of a sentence passed by a competent court. This article establishes a positive obligation for the states party to protect the life of persons under their jurisdiction, and a negative obligation not to intentionally deprive persons of their lives.

The case law of the European Court of Human Rights has developed important principles on the right to life, including the definition of life, legitimate exceptions to the prohibition of killing and the positive obligations of states. The Court has established that the right to life begins with birth and that the State has the obligation to protect the life of unborn persons, but that this obligation is not absolute and must be balanced with other fundamental rights. The Court has also established that deprivation of life is only lawful when it is strictly necessary to repel an unlawful attack against a person.

The protection of the right to life implies positive obligations for states, such as the investigation of cases of suspicious death, the protection of persons in situations of risk and the adoption of measures to prevent violence. The Court has established that states have the obligation to effectively and thoroughly investigate all deaths that may be attributable to public authorities, and that the failure to conduct an effective investigation may constitute a violation of Article 2 of the Convention. These positive obligations are fundamental for ensuring that the right to life is effective and not merely theoretical or illusory.

### 9.2 Prohibition of torture (Article 3)

Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights establishes that no one shall be subjected to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment. This prohibition is absolute and admits no exceptions, which makes it one of the most important provisions of the Convention. Article 3 protects human dignity and the physical and moral integrity of persons, and its application is supervised by the European Court of Human Rights.

The case law of the Court has developed an important distinction between torture, inhuman treatment and degrading treatment. Torture involves severe suffering intentionally inflicted for purposes such as obtaining information or confessions, the imposition of punishments or intimidation. Inhuman treatment is that which causes severe suffering without reaching the level of torture, while degrading treatment is that which humbles or intimidates the person. The Court has established that the determination of the level of suffering depends on circumstances such as the duration of the treatment, the state of health of the victim and the gender of the victim.

The absolute prohibition of torture has important implications for the obligations of states. The Court has established that states have the obligation not only to refrain from inflicting torture or inhuman or degrading treatment, but also to effectively investigate allegations of torture and to punish those responsible. The Court has also established that states have the obligation not to expel persons to countries where they run a real risk of being subjected to torture or inhuman treatment, known as the prohibition of expulsion (non-refoulement). This obligation is particularly important in the context of migration and asylum.

### 9.3 Liberty and security (Article 5)

Article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights establishes the right to liberty and security of every person. This article guarantees that no one shall be deprived of his liberty except in the cases and in accordance with the procedures prescribed by law. Article 5 enumerates the legitimate exceptions to the deprivation of liberty, which include detention for the purpose of bringing a person before a competent legal authority, detention for the enforcement of a lawful court order, detention for the prevention of the spreading of infectious diseases and detention for the protection of public order.

The case law of the Court has established important principles on the lawfulness of the deprivation of liberty. The Court has established that every deprivation of liberty must be prescribed by law, must pursue a legitimate aim and must be necessary in a democratic society. The Court has also established that the duration of pre-trial detention must be reasonable and that detained persons have the right to be brought before a judge within a reasonable time. The Court has developed extensive case law on the duration of pre-trial detention, establishing that indefinite pre-trial detention is incompatible with Article 5 of the Convention.

The right to liberty also includes the right not to be arbitrarily detained. The Court has established that arbitrary detention is incompatible with Article 5 of the Convention, and that states have the obligation to ensure that detention is carried out in accordance with the law and the established procedures. The Court has also established that detained persons have the right to be informed of the reasons for their detention, to challenge the lawfulness of their detention and to receive effective reparation for arbitrary detention.

### 9.4 Right to a fair trial (Article 6)

Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights establishes the right to a fair trial, which is one of the most important rights of the Convention and the one that generates the largest number of applications before the European Court. Article 6 guarantees that everyone has the right to have his cause heard fairly, publicly and within a reasonable time, by an independent and impartial tribunal established by law. Article 6 applies in both civil and criminal matters, and establishes procedural guarantees that are fundamental for the protection of fundamental rights.

The procedural guarantees of Article 6 include the presumption of innocence, the right to a defence, the right to an interpreter, the right to examine witnesses and the right to legal assistance. The Court has established that these guarantees apply from the moment a person is formally charged or when his situation has been substantially affected as a result of a suspicion against him. The right to an independent and impartial tribunal is another fundamental component of Article 6. The Court has also established that the impartiality of the tribunal must be assessed both from a subjective standpoint, that is, the personal conviction of the judge, and from an objective standpoint, that is, the sufficient guarantees to exclude any legitimate doubt as to impartiality.

Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights establishes freedom of thought, conscience and religion, which includes the right of everyone to change his religion or belief, and the right of everyone to manifest his religion or belief, individually or in public or private, through worship, teaching, practice and observance. Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights establishes freedom of expression, which includes the freedom to receive and impart information and ideas without interference by public authority. The Court has developed case law on freedom of teaching that covers questions such as the protection of teachers against retaliation for their academic opinions, the right of educational institutions to establish their own curricula and the right of teachers to use innovative teaching methods. The Court has also established that freedom of teaching is not absolute and may be subject to restrictions that are necessary to protect other fundamental rights, such as the right to equality and the right to non-discrimination. The Court has covered questions such as the teaching of history, the teaching of evolution, the teaching of sexuality and the teaching of religion.

## Chapter 12: Right to Free Elections and Private Life

### 12.1 Right to property (Article 1 Protocol 1)

Article 1 of Protocol No. 1 to the European Convention on Human Rights establishes that every person is entitled to the peaceful enjoyment of his possessions. No one shall be deprived of his possessions except in the public interest and in the conditions provided for by law, for reasons of general interest and in the conditions provided for by the general principles of international law. This article protects the right to property and establishes a balance between the individual interest in the enjoyment of possessions and the general interest in the regulation of property.

The Court has developed extensive case law on Article 1 of Protocol No. 1, establishing important principles on the protection of possessions. The Court has established that the right to the peaceful enjoyment of possessions has three components: a general rule of peaceful enjoyment of possessions, a rule on the deprivation of possessions and a rule on the control of the use of possessions. The Court has also established that the deprivation of possessions must be prescribed by law, pursue a legitimate aim in the public interest and be proportionate to the aim pursued. The Court has covered questions such as the expropriation of land, the regulation of rents, the confiscation of property and liability for environmental damage.

The Court has established that the concept of "possessions" is broad and includes not only material property, but also intangible property, such as intellectual property rights, social security benefits and contractual rights. The Court has also established that legal persons, including companies and non-governmental organisations, also enjoy the protection of Article 1 of Protocol No. 1. This protection is fundamental for the functioning of the market economy and for the assurance that investors and companies can rely on the stability of the legal and regulatory framework.

### 12.2 Protection of private life (Article 8)

Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights establishes the right to respect for private and family life, the home and correspondence. This article is one of the most frequently invoked before the European Court and protects a wide range of personal interests, including personal identity, the development of personality, family relations, health, education and sexual life. Article 8 is a right that has a dual dimension: a negative dimension, which protects against interference by public authorities, and a positive dimension, which obliges states to adopt measures to protect private life.

The Court has established that private life is a broad concept that cannot be defined exhaustively, and includes the right to identity, the right to personal development, the right to intimacy, the right to the protection of health and the right to the protection of reputation. The Court has also established that interferences with private life must be prescribed by law, pursue a legitimate aim and be necessary in a democratic society. The Court has covered questions such as the surveillance of communications, the protection of personal data, the right to intimacy in the workplace, the right to intimacy in public spaces and the right to the protection of health.

The protection of personal data is an important aspect of Article 8 of the Convention. The Court has established that the processing of personal data constitutes an interference with private life that must comply with the requirements of Article 8.2 of the Convention, including the existence of a legal basis, a legitimate aim and the proportionality of the interference. The Court has also established that states have the obligation to ensure that the processing of personal data is carried out in a transparent, secure manner and with the consent of the data subject, except in the cases provided for by law.

## Chapter 13: Freedom of Conscience and Conscientious Objection

### 13.1 Freedom of conscience (Article 9)

Freedom of conscience is one of the most profound fundamental rights recognised by the European Convention on Human Rights, as it protects the capacity of each person to determine their own beliefs and moral values. Article 9 of the Convention establishes that everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion, and that this right includes the freedom to change one's religion or belief, and the freedom to manifest one's religion or belief, individually or in public or private, through worship, teaching, practice and observance. Freedom of conscience is the foundation of other fundamental rights, such as freedom of expression, freedom of assembly and freedom of association.

The European Court of Human Rights has developed important case law on freedom of conscience that covers questions such as conscientious objection to military service, conscientious objection to euthanasia, conscientious objection to genetic manipulation and conscientious objection to participation in activities that violate the moral convictions of the person. The Court has established that freedom of conscience is a right that enjoys special protection, and that restrictions on this right must be exceptional and justified by compelling reasons of general interest. The Court has also established that conscientious objection is a legitimate manifestation of freedom of conscience that must be respected by states, provided that the person invoking conscientious objection has sincere and well-founded convictions.

The Court has addressed the question of conscientious objection to military service in several cases, including *Bayatyan v. Armenia* (2011), in which the Court established that conscientious objection to military service is protected by Article 9 of the Convention, and that states have the obligation to provide an alternative to military service for persons who have sincere and well-founded conscientious objections. This case is particularly important because it established a precedent that affects all states party to the Convention and strengthens the protection of freedom of conscience in the context of military service.

### 13.2 Conscientious objection and third-party rights

Conscientious objection raises complex questions when it conflicts with the rights of third parties. The European Court has had to address questions such as the conscientious objection of health professionals to participate in euthanasia, the conscientious objection of education professionals to teach subjects that contradict their moral convictions and the conscientious objection of public officials to perform acts that contradict their religious beliefs. In these cases, the Court has sought a balance between the freedom of conscience of the individual and the rights of third parties that may be affected by the conscientious objection.

The Court has established that conscientious objection cannot be invoked to justify discrimination or to deny services to third parties, and that states have the obligation to ensure that conscientious objection does not prejudice the rights of third parties. The Court has also established that states have a margin of appreciation to regulate conscientious objection, and that this margin must be compatible with the Convention and the principles of a democratic society. The Court has covered questions such as the conscientious objection of doctors to the prescription of contraceptives, the conscientious objection of teachers to the teaching of evolution and the conscientious objection of officials to the solemnisation of same-sex marriages.

## Chapter 14: Non-Discrimination and Equality

### 14.1 Principle of non-discrimination (Article 14)

Article 14 of the European Convention on Human Rights establishes that the enjoyment of the rights and freedoms recognised in the Convention shall be secured without discrimination on any ground, such as sex, race, colour, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, association with a national minority, property, birth or any other status. Article 14 is a provision of general scope that applies to all rights of the Convention and aims to ensure equal treatment and non-discrimination.

The European Court of Human Rights has developed extensive case law on Article 14 of the Convention, establishing important principles on discrimination. The Court has established that discrimination occurs when a difference in treatment has no objective and reasonable justification, or when the difference in treatment is not proportionate to the aim pursued. The Court has also established that the burden of proof lies with the State, which must demonstrate that the difference in treatment has an objective and reasonable justification. The Court has covered questions such as discrimination on the grounds of sex, discrimination on the grounds of sexual orientation, discrimination on the grounds of disability, discrimination on the grounds of age and discrimination on the grounds of ethnic origin.

The Court has established that discrimination may be direct or indirect. Direct discrimination occurs when a person is treated less favourably than another in a comparable situation, on a ground prohibited by the Convention. Indirect discrimination occurs when a provision, criterion or practice which is apparently neutral has an adverse effect on a group protected by the Convention, and which is not justified by a legitimate aim and is not proportionate to the aim pursued. The Court has also established that discrimination on the grounds of sexual orientation is prohibited by Article 14 of the Convention, and that states have the obligation to ensure equal treatment of homosexual persons in all spheres of life.

### 14.2 Equality between men and women (Article 5 Protocol 7)

Article 5 of Protocol No. 7 to the European Convention on Human Rights establishes equality between spouses in respect of rights and duties in the family, marriage and family relations. This article guarantees that spouses have the same rights and duties in relation to children, the administration of the household and the management of family assets. Article 5 of Protocol No. 7 is particularly important for the protection of the rights of women in the family sphere, as historically women have been subject to restrictions in the exercise of their rights in the family sphere.

The Court has established that equality between spouses is a fundamental principle of the Convention that must be respected in all spheres of family life. The Court has covered questions such as the right of women to participate in family decision-making, the right of women to administer their own property and the right of women not to be discriminated against in the dissolution of marriage. The Court has also established that states have the obligation to ensure equality between spouses in the field of family legislation, including legislation on marriage, dissolution of marriage, parental authority and maintenance.

## Chapter 15: Deportation and Expulsion

### 15.1 Prohibition of arbitrary expulsion

The European Convention on Human Rights establishes important prohibitions against the arbitrary expulsion of aliens, including the prohibition of collective expulsion and the prohibition of expulsion to countries where there is a risk of torture or inhuman treatment. Article 4 of Protocol No. 4 to the Convention prohibits the collective expulsion of aliens, which means that public authorities cannot expel a group of aliens collectively without an individual examination of each case. This prohibition is fundamental for the protection of the rights of refugees and asylum seekers.

Article 3 of the Convention, which prohibits torture and inhuman or degrading treatment, has an important dimension in the context of the expulsion of aliens. The Court has established that the expulsion of an alien to a country where there is a real risk of being subjected to torture or inhuman or degrading treatment constitutes a violation of Article 3 of the Convention. This principle, known as the principle of non-refoulement, is fundamental for the protection of the rights of refugees and asylum seekers, and applies both to the expulsion and the return of aliens. The Court has established that the determination of risk must be based on an individual assessment of the circumstances of the case, including the general situation of the country of destination, the personal situation of the alien and the reliability of the guarantees provided by the country of destination.

The Court has covered questions such as the expulsion of refugees, the expulsion of asylum seekers, the expulsion of aliens in an irregular situation and the expulsion of aliens who pose a risk to national security. In the case *Soering v. United Kingdom* (1989), the Court established that the extradition of an alien to a country where there is a real risk of being subjected to the death penalty constitutes a violation of Article 3 of the Convention, as the death penalty and the conditions of detention associated with it may constitute inhuman or degrading treatment. This case is particularly important because it established a precedent that affects the extradition and expulsion of aliens to countries that apply the death penalty.

### 15.2 Rights of refugees and asylum seekers

Refugees and asylum seekers enjoy special protection under the European Convention on Human Rights, which includes protection against expulsion to countries where they would face persecution, torture or inhuman treatment. The European Convention on Human Rights, in combination with the Geneva Convention relating to the Status of Refugees of 1951, establishes a framework of protection for persons fleeing persecution, armed conflicts or serious violations of human rights. The Court has established that refugees and asylum seekers have the right to have their case examined individually, and that public authorities cannot expel a person without having carefully examined their personal situation and the risks they would face in the event of expulsion.

The Court has developed case law on the rights of refugees that covers questions such as the right to legal assistance, the right to an interpreter, the right to medical care and the right to education. The Court has also established that refugees and asylum seekers have the right to a fair and equitable asylum procedure, which includes the right to be heard, the right to present evidence and the right to a reasoned decision. The Court has covered questions such as the detention of asylum seekers, the concentration of asylum seekers in reception centres and access to the labour market for asylum seekers.

## Chapter 16: Environment and Human Rights

### 16.1 Protection of the environment and Article 8 of the Convention

The European Convention on Human Rights does not contain an explicit right to a healthy environment, but the European Court of Human Rights has developed case law that connects the protection of the environment with the right to respect for private and family life, the home and correspondence, established in Article 8 of the Convention. The Court has established that environmental degradation may adversely affect the enjoyment of the right to private and family life, and that states have the obligation to adopt measures to protect the environment when environmental degradation may affect the health, quality of life or well-being of persons.

The Court has covered questions such as air pollution, water pollution, noise pollution, hazardous waste, soil degradation and the loss of biodiversity. In the case *López Ostra v. Spain* (1994), the Court established that pollution from a waste treatment plant may affect the right to private and family life, and that the State has the obligation to adopt measures to protect persons from the negative effects of environmental pollution. This case is particularly important because it established a precedent that recognises the connection between the protection of the environment and fundamental human rights.

The Court has established that the protection of the environment is a matter of general interest that may justify restrictions on other rights, such as the right to property and the freedom of enterprise. The Court has also established that states have a margin of appreciation to determine the measures that must be taken to protect the environment, but that this margin must be compatible with the Convention and the principles of a democratic society. The Court has covered questions such as public participation in environmental decision-making, access to environmental information and reparation for environmental damage.

### 16.2 Liability for environmental damage

Liability for environmental damage is an important aspect of the relationship between the European Convention on Human Rights and the protection of the environment. The Court has established that states have the obligation to ensure that persons affected by environmental damage can obtain effective reparation. The Court has covered questions such as liability for industrial pollution, liability for water pollution, liability for soil degradation and liability for the loss of biodiversity. The Court has also established that the failure to provide effective reparation for environmental damage may constitute a violation of Article 8 of the Convention.

The Court has developed case law on reparation for environmental damage that covers questions such as compensation for material damage, compensation for moral damage, the restoration of the environment and the adoption of measures to prevent future environmental damage. The Court has also established that states have the obligation to ensure that persons affected by environmental damage can access the courts effectively, and that judicial proceedings for reparation for environmental damage must be fair, equitable and accessible. The Court has covered questions such as the standing of non-governmental organisations to act on behalf of persons affected by environmental damage, the burden of proof in cases of environmental damage and reparation for transboundary environmental damage.

## Chapter 17: Prison and Deprivation of Liberty

### 17.1 Conditions of detention (Article 3)

Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which prohibits torture and inhuman or degrading treatment, has a particularly important application in the context of detention conditions. The European Court of Human Rights has established that public authorities have the obligation to ensure that detained or imprisoned persons are treated with dignity and respect, and that detention conditions must not inflict suffering that goes beyond the inevitable inherent in detention. The Court has developed extensive case law on detention conditions, covering questions such as overcrowding, lack of space, lack of natural light, lack of ventilation, inadequate sanitary facilities, lack of access to medical care and violence between inmates.

The Court has established that the determination of whether detention conditions constitute inhuman or degrading treatment depends on circumstances such as the duration of detention, the age and state of health of the detainee, the available space, hygiene conditions, access to natural light and fresh air, and access to recreational and educational activities. The Court has also established that detention conditions must be assessed against international human rights standards, including the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners (Nelson Mandela Rules) and the Council of Europe Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners.

The Court has covered questions such as detention in overcrowded cells, detention in unsanitary conditions, detention of persons with disabilities, detention of minors and detention of pregnant women. In the case *Kalashnikov v. Russia* (2002), the Court established that detention in a cell of 2.5 square metres for twenty hours a day, for a period of almost one year, constituted degrading treatment in violation of Article 3 of the Convention. In the case *M.S.S. v. Belgium and Greece* (2011), the Court established that detention conditions in an overcrowded reception centre in Greece constituted inhuman and degrading treatment in violation of Articles 3 and 13 of the Convention.

### 17.2 Rights of prisoners

Prisoners in prisons enjoy a series of rights recognised by the European Convention on Human Rights, which include the right to physical and moral integrity, the right to medical care, the right to education, the right to work and the right to correspondence. The Court has established that prisoners do not lose all their rights by virtue of being imprisoned, and that states have the obligation to ensure that prisoners can enjoy their rights to the extent compatible with security and public order. The Court has covered questions such as the right of prisoners to receive visits, the right of prisoners to communicate with their lawyers, the right of prisoners to participate in recreational activities and the right of prisoners to express their opinions.

The Court has established that prisoners have the right to adequate food, adequate clothing, adequate accommodation and adequate access to health services. The Court has also established that prisoners have the right to have their dignity respected, and that prison authorities cannot treat prisoners in a humiliating or degrading manner. The Court has covered questions such as the use of force against prisoners, prison discipline, corporal punishment and prolonged solitary confinement. The Court has established that prolonged solitary confinement, especially when combined with a lack of activities and a lack of human contact, may constitute inhuman or degrading treatment in violation of Article 3 of the Convention.

## Chapter 18: Jurisdiction of the European Court

### 18.1 Contentious jurisdiction

The contentious jurisdiction of the European Court of Human Rights is the main mechanism for the protection of human rights under the European Convention on Human Rights. The Court has jurisdiction to hear cases in which a violation of a right recognised by the Convention or its additional protocols is alleged by a state party to the Convention. The contentious jurisdiction of the Court is exercised on the basis of individual applications, inter-state applications and references from national courts. Individual applications are the most used mechanism and represent the majority of cases heard by the Court.

The Court has jurisdiction to hear individual applications against all states party to the Convention, including states that are not members of the European Union. The competence of the Court extends to all questions relating to the interpretation and application of the Convention, including questions relating to the interpretation of the additional protocols. The Court also has jurisdiction to hear cases in which a violation of the Convention is alleged by a state in the exercise of its extraterritorial jurisdiction, which includes cases in which the state exercises effective control over a territory or over persons outside its borders.

The admissibility of individual applications is subject to procedural requirements that include the exhaustion of domestic remedies, the filing of the application within the four-month time limit from the final internal decision and the absence of substantial identity with a case already examined by the Court. The Court also has requirements of relevance, gravity and significance that determine whether an application is admissible. The Court has established a doctrine of "effective domestic compensatory remedies" that allows states to establish internal mechanisms to remedy violations of the Convention, which may affect the admissibility of applications before the Court.

### 18.2 Advisory jurisdiction

The advisory jurisdiction of the European Court of Human Rights is a mechanism that allows the supreme courts of the states party to request advisory opinions from the Court on the interpretation of the Convention. The advisory jurisdiction was introduced by Protocol No. 16 to the Convention, which entered into force in 2021, and aims to strengthen the dialogue between the Court and national courts, and to improve the understanding and application of the Convention at national level. Advisory opinions are not binding, but provide guidance to national courts on the interpretation of the Convention.

The Court may deliver advisory opinions on questions relating to the interpretation of the Convention and its additional protocols, provided that the question raised by the national court is relevant to a case pending before the national court. The Court may also deliver advisory opinions on questions relating to the interpretation of the general principles of the Convention, such as the principles of proportionality, subsidiarity and the margin of appreciation. The Court has delivered advisory opinions on questions such as the protection of private life in the digital environment, the proportionality of restrictions on freedom of expression and the interpretation of the principle of non-discrimination.

The advisory jurisdiction of the Court has particular importance in the context of the relationship between the European Convention on Human Rights and European Union law. The Court has delivered advisory opinions on questions such as the compatibility of Union legislation with the Convention, the interpretation of Article 6 of the Convention in the context of EU law and the protection of fundamental rights within the framework of police and judicial cooperation in criminal matters. These advisory opinions are particularly relevant for the accession of the European Union to the Convention, which remains a strategic priority for the protection of human rights in Europe.

### 18.3 Mediation and conciliation

The European Court of Human Rights offers a mediation and conciliation mechanism for cases submitted to it, with the aim of facilitating the friendly settlement of disputes between applicants and states party. Mediation is an alternative dispute resolution mechanism that allows the parties to reach an agreement without the need for the Court to deliver a judgment. The Court has established a conciliation procedure that is available for all cases meeting the admissibility requirements, and may be used at any stage of the proceedings.

The Court's conciliation procedure is based on the principles of confidentiality, voluntariness and equality of the parties. The Court appoints a mediator, who is generally one of the judges of the Court, and who has the responsibility of facilitating negotiations between the parties. The mediator acts impartially and neutrally, and has the obligation to maintain the confidentiality of the negotiations. If the parties reach an agreement, the agreement is incorporated into a judgment of the Court that sets out the measures that the state must adopt to comply with the agreement.

The Court's conciliation mechanism is an important complement to the Court's contentious jurisdiction, as it allows for a faster and more flexible resolution of cases. The Court has registered an increasing number of cases resolved by conciliation, which demonstrates the effectiveness of this mechanism. Conciliation is particularly useful for cases that raise questions of reparation, as it allows the parties to negotiate directly the reparation measures that the state must adopt.

## Chapter 19: Economic and Social Rights

### 19.1 Protection of property (Article 1 Protocol 1)

Article 1 of Protocol No. 1 to the European Convention on Human Rights establishes that every person is entitled to the peaceful enjoyment of his possessions, and that no one shall be deprived of his possessions except in the public interest and in the conditions provided for by law, for reasons of general interest and in the conditions provided for by the general principles of international law. The Court has established that Article 1 of Protocol No. 1 has three components: the general rule of peaceful enjoyment of possessions, the rule on the deprivation of possessions and the rule on the control of the use of possessions. The general rule of peaceful enjoyment protects holders of possessions against unjustified interference by public authorities. The rule on the deprivation of possessions regulates expropriations and other forms of deprivation of possessions for reasons of general interest. The rule on the control of the use of possessions regulates the control of the use of possessions, including urban planning regulation, environmental regulation and the regulation of intellectual property.

The Court has covered questions such as the expropriation of land for the construction of public infrastructure, the regulation of rents to protect tenants, the confiscation of property obtained through illicit means, liability for damage caused by industrial activities and the protection of intellectual property.
