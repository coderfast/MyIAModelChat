Feudalism is the socioeconomic and political system that dominated Western Europe during the Middle Ages, approximately from the 5th century to the 15th century, and that laid the foundations of medieval society, economy, and politics. This system was characterized by relationships of loyalty and service between lords and vassals, where the lord granted his vassal a fief in exchange for military loyalty, political counsel, and payments in kind, while the vassal administered the fief, derived his subsistence from the land, and provided military service to the lord when required. The feudal pyramid was organized in several levels: at the top was the monarch who was the nominal owner of all the territory of the kingdom; below the monarch were the great nobles who received large fiefs in exchange for loyalty and military service; below the great nobles were the lesser nobles who received smaller fiefs; and at the base of the pyramid were the peasants who worked the land of the fief in exchange for protection. The feudal system was not uniform or static, but varied significantly by region and historical period, and underwent significant evolution during the centuries of the Middle Ages.

Romanesque art is the first international artistic style of medieval Europe and developed between the 11th and 12th centuries throughout Western Europe, creating a common artistic language that reflected the faith, community, and spirituality of medieval society. Romanesque is characterized by its simple, massive, and symmetrical forms that evoke the solidity, permanence, and transcendence of the Christian faith, and that are expressed in architecture (churches, monasteries, hermitages), in sculpture (portals, capitals, cloisters), in mural painting (narrative paintings illustrating biblical scenes), and in goldsmithing (chalices, monstrances, reliquaries). Castles were one of the most emblematic architectural manifestations of the Middle Ages and played a fundamental role in the political, military, and social organization of the feudal world. A castle was not simply a fortified residence but a complex defensive system that included walls, towers, moats, barbicans, portcullises, and numerous devices designed to resist the siege of an enemy army. The first castles of the Middle Ages were built in the 9th century in response to Viking, Saracen, and Hungarian raids that ravaged Europe. These early castles were relatively simple structures built with wood and earth, known as motte-and-bailey castles, consisting of an artificial earthen mound crowned by a wooden tower surrounded by a moat. However, from the 11th century onward, castles began to be built in stone, which significantly increased their defensive capacity and durability.

The evolution of castle architecture was parallel to the evolution of siege techniques. As armies developed new siege weapons such as catapults, battering rams, siege towers, and mining tunnels, castle builders responded by improving defenses. Castle towers evolved from being circular to square and back to circular again, as it was discovered that circular towers better resisted catapult impacts and did not present blind spots for defense. Castle walls were made thicker and were equipped with battlements, arrow loops, and machicolations that allowed defenders to launch projectiles at attackers while remaining protected. Moats were enlarged and flooded to hinder tunnel excavation, and barbicans were added, which were outer walls protecting the main entrance of the castle. The internal layout of castles also grew increasingly complex, with the addition of inner courtyards, stables, warehouses, kitchens, chapels, audience halls, and quarters for the garrison and nobility.

Public health in medieval cities was a constant challenge.
