## Chapter 2: Evaporation

### From Water to Vapor

Evaporation is the process by which liquid water is transformed into water vapor, an invisible gas that rises into the atmosphere where it can condense, forming clouds, rain, snow, and other forms of precipitation. Evaporation is one of the most important processes of the water cycle because it is the main mechanism by which water is transported from the Earth's surface to the atmosphere and regulates the climate, hydrology, ecology, and life on the planet. Evaporation occurs when water molecules on the surface of liquid water gain enough kinetic energy to overcome the cohesion forces that keep them together and escape from the water surface, transforming into water vapor. The rate of evaporation depends on several factors, including the water temperature, which increases the evaporation rate since water molecules gain more kinetic energy at higher temperatures; the relative humidity of the air, which decreases the evaporation rate since humid air already contains a high concentration of water vapor and has less capacity to absorb more water vapor; wind speed, which increases the evaporation rate since wind carries water vapor molecules away from the water surface, facilitating evaporation; the surface area of the water, which increases the evaporation rate since a larger surface exposed to the air allows more water molecules to escape to the atmosphere; and atmospheric pressure, which decreases the evaporation rate since lower atmospheric pressure allows water molecules to escape more easily from the water surface. Evaporation occurs in all bodies of water, including oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, reservoirs, ponds, puddles, and other bodies of water, and also occurs on the soil surface, in plants through transpiration, and in animals through respiration and sweating.

### Transpiration

Transpiration is the process by which plants release water vapor through the stomata, small pores found on the surface of plant leaves, and is an important component of the water cycle because it transports water from plant roots to the atmosphere, contributing to the formation of clouds, rain, and other forms of precipitation. Transpiration occurs when water absorbed by plant roots is transported through the stem and leaves to the stomata, where it evaporates, transforming into water vapor that is released into the atmosphere. The rate of transpiration depends on several factors, including air temperature, which increases the transpiration rate since plants lose more water at higher temperatures; relative humidity of the air, which decreases the transpiration rate since humid air reduces the evaporation of water from leaves; wind speed, which increases the transpiration rate since wind carries water vapor away from the leaf surface, facilitating evaporation; sunlight, which increases the transpiration rate since sunlight opens the stomata and accelerates the evaporation of water from leaves; and the availability of water in the soil, which increases the transpiration rate since plants have more water available to transport and evaporate. Transpiration is a continuous process that transports approximately seventy thousand cubic kilometers of water to the atmosphere each year, which is equivalent to approximately one-third of the water transported by evaporation from the Earth's surface. Transpiration is not only a hydrological process but also a physiological process that regulates plant temperature, transports nutrients from roots to leaves, and facilitates photosynthesis by keeping the stomata open to allow gas exchange.

## Chapter 11: Glaciers and Ice Caps

### Formation and Dynamics of Glaciers

Glaciers are continental ice masses formed by the compaction and recrystallization of snow over thousands of years and cover approximately ten percent of the Earth's surface, storing around sixty-nine percent of the planet's fresh water in solid form. The formation of a glacier begins when fallen snow does not completely melt during summer and accumulates layer upon layer during multiple winters, first creating a snow layer called firn that has an approximate density of one hundred kilograms per cubic meter. As more snow accumulates, the lower layers are compressed under the weight of the upper layers, expelling interstitial air and transforming the snow into snow granules with a density of four hundred to eight hundred kilograms per cubic meter, and finally into glacial ice with a density of eight hundred to nine hundred kilograms per cubic meter. This compaction process is called snow metamorphism and can take between one hundred and one thousand years depending on the accumulation rate and local climate conditions. Once the ice thickness reaches approximately thirty meters, the pressure at the base of the glacier is sufficient to produce partial melting, creating a lubricated water layer that allows the glacier to slide over the underlying bedrock, a process known as basal flow. Glaciers are classified into several types according to their size, shape, and behavior: valley or alpine glaciers that flow downhill through mountain valleys; ice caps that cover continental areas greater than fifty thousand square kilometers, such as those in Greenland and Antarctica; piedmont glaciers that extend from the mouth of a glacial valley to surrounding plains; hanging glaciers that are found on steep slopes and flow into valley glaciers; and icebergs that break off...

### Glacial Movement and Its Geological Impact

Glacial movement is a fundamental process that shapes the Earth's landscape through the erosion, transportation, and deposition of rock materials, creating unique geological formations such as U-shaped valleys, arêtes, glacial cirques, moraines, drumlins, and fjords. Glaciers move by three main mechanisms: basal sliding, which occurs when a lubricated water film between the glacier and the bedrock allows the ice to slide under the influence of gravity; internal deformation, which occurs when the lower layers of ice are plastically deformed under the pressure of the upper layers, flowing like a highly compressible viscous fluid; and brittle fracture, which occurs on the glacier surface where the ice behaves as a brittle solid and fractures forming crevasses and seracs. The speed of glacial movement varies enormously depending on the type of glacier and local conditions: alpine valley glaciers typically move between ten and one hundred meters per year, while ice stream glaciers in Antarctica can move up to several hundred meters per year, and the Jakobshavn glacier in Greenland reaches speeds of up to fifteen meters per day during acceleration episodes. Glacial erosion occurs primarily through two processes: plucking, which occurs when meltwater at the base of the glacier freezes around rock fragments and plucks them from the bedrock; and abrasion, which occurs when rock fragments embedded in the base of the glacier scratch and polish the bedrock surface, creating glacial striations and rock polish. Materials eroded by glaciers are transported to the melting zone, where they are deposited forming moraines, which are accumulations of unsorted sediments ranging from clays to house-sized boulders.

### Glacial Retreat and Climate Change

Glacial retreat is one of the most visible and alarming indicators of global climate change, since most of the world's glaciers are losing mass at an accelerated rate due to rising global temperatures caused by greenhouse gas emissions. Since the beginning of the industrial revolution, the world's glaciers have lost approximately three hundred thousand cubic kilometers of ice, contributing to sea level rise of approximately nine millimeters, and the rate of loss has accelerated significantly since the 1990s. The Alpine glaciers of Europe, such as those in the Alps, the Pyrenees, and the Swiss Alps, have lost between twenty and fifty percent of their volume since 1980, and some smaller glaciers have disappeared completely, such as the Pizol glacier in Switzerland, which was declared dead in 2019. In the tropics, the situation is even more dramatic, as glaciers such as those on Mount Kilimanjaro, the tropical Andes, and the mountains of Papua New Guinea are disappearing at an alarming rate, with some estimates suggesting that most tropical glaciers will disappear before 2050. Glacial retreat has significant consequences for the water resources of millions of people who depend on glacial melt as a source of fresh water for human consumption, agriculture, and hydroelectric power generation, particularly in regions such as the Andes, the Himalayas, and Central Asia. Additionally, glacial retreat can cause natural disasters such as glacial lake outburst floods, which form when meltwater accumulates behind unstable moraines and can cause catastrophic floods if the moraine collapses, as occurred in the Quelccaya glacial lake disaster in Peru in 2013.

## Chapter 12: Ocean Currents

### Thermohaline Circulation

Thermohaline circulation is the global system of ocean currents driven by temperature and salinity differences in seawater, which acts as a huge conveyor belt that redistributes heat, nutrients, oxygen, and carbon dioxide throughout the planet, regulating global climate and sustaining marine ecosystems. This circulation system, also known as the global ocean conveyor belt, originates in the Nordic seas and Antarctica, where cold, salty water sinks to the ocean depths due to its high density, creating deep currents that circulate slowly through all the world's oceans over several hundred years. Warm, less dense surface water flows from the tropics toward the poles, where it cools, increases its salinity through the formation of sea ice that expels salt, and sinks, completing a cycle that takes approximately sixteen hundred years to complete one full trip around the planet. The Gulf Stream, for example, transports approximately thirty sverdrups of warm water from the Gulf of Mexico toward the North Atlantic, a volume of water equivalent to thirty million cubic meters per second, enough to fill all the world's rivers several times per second. This current warms the western coasts of Europe by several degrees Celsius, making cities like London and Bergen have significantly milder winters than cities at similar latitudes in Canada. Climate change threatens thermohaline circulation by melting the Greenland ice cap, injecting large amounts of fresh water into the North Atlantic and reducing the salinity and density of surface water, which could weaken or even destabilize the conveyor belt, with potentially catastrophic consequences for the European and global climate.

### Surface Currents and Prevailing Winds

Ocean surface currents are massive water movements that flow on the ocean surface, driven primarily by prevailing winds, and play a fundamental role in redistributing solar heat, regulating climate, and sustaining marine ecosystems. Prevailing winds, which blow relatively constantly in specific directions due to Earth's rotation and the distribution of solar heat, drag the ocean surface creating currents that can reach speeds of several meters per second and transport millions of cubic meters of water per second. In the northern hemisphere, the easterly trade winds create equatorial currents that flow westward, while the mid-latitude westerlies create mid-latitude currents that flow eastward, forming cyclonic ocean gyres in a clockwise direction. These ocean gyres, such as the North Atlantic Gyre, the North Pacific Gyre, and the southern hemisphere gyres, are enormous water vortices that circulate around high-pressure centers and transport warm water on their outer edges and cold water on their inner edges, creating temperature gradients that influence precipitation patterns and ocean biological productivity. Ocean currents also transport marine organisms, seeds, pollutants, and nutrients across enormous distances, connecting distant ecosystems and maintaining global marine biodiversity, as occurs with the Gulf Stream, which transports fish and crustacean larvae from the Caribbean to Europe.

### The El Nino Phenomenon and the Southern Oscillation

The El Nino phenomenon, formally known as El Nino-Southern Oscillation or ENSO, is a natural climate variation that occurs in the tropical Pacific Ocean and has profound effects on climate, precipitation, wind patterns, and ecosystems worldwide, temporarily altering normal oceanic and atmospheric circulation patterns. During an El Nino event, the trade winds that normally blow westward weaken or reverse, allowing warm water from the western Pacific to flow eastward, raising sea surface temperature off the west coast of South America by several degrees Celsius and suppressing the upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water that normally sustains productive fisheries such as the Peruvian anchovy. The warming of the tropical Pacific during a strong El Nino event, such as those of 1997 and 1998, can raise global average surface temperature by approximately zero point two degrees Celsius and cause severe droughts in Australia, Indonesia, India, and southern Africa, while causing intense rainfall and floods on the west coast of South America, Central America, and the southern United States. The opposite phase of ENSO, known as La Nina, is characterized by strengthening trade winds and cooling of the tropical Pacific, causing climate effects generally opposite to those of El Nino, such as droughts in South America and floods in Australia and Southeast Asia. ENSO is the most influential interannual climate variability pattern, and its effects extend through atmospheric teleconnections that alter wind, temperature, and precipitation patterns in regions distant from the tropical Pacific, affecting billions of people and ecosystems throughout the planet.

### The Role of Oceans in the Water Cycle

Oceans play a dominant role in the global water cycle since they contain approximately ninety-seven percent of Earth's water and are responsible for approximately eighty percent of global evaporation, transferring enormous amounts of water and energy from the sea surface to the atmosphere that drives precipitation patterns and climate throughout the planet. Oceanic evaporation is especially intense in tropical regions where sea surface temperature is high and winds are strong, creating bands of high evaporation that feed the cloud and rain systems of the tropics and drive the Hadley cell that transports water vapor and heat from the tropics to mid-latitudes. Oceans also absorb approximately twenty-five percent of carbon dioxide emitted by human activities, a process that is acidifying ocean waters and threatening organisms with calcium carbonate shells such as corals, mollusks, and calcareous plankton, but that is also reducing the ocean's ability to absorb CO2 in the future. The heat absorbed by the oceans, which have absorbed more than ninety percent of the excess heat from global warming, is causing thermal expansion of seawater, contributing to sea level rise of approximately one and a half millimeters per year, in addition to the contribution from melting glaciers and ice caps. Oceans are also the primary source of continental precipitation, since water vapor evaporated from the oceans is transported by winds to the continents, where it condenses and falls as rain or snow, recharging rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers that supply fresh water to human populations and terrestrial ecosystems.

## Chapter 13: Rivers and Fluvial Systems

### River Formation and Morphology

Rivers are natural fresh water currents that flow from their sources at higher elevations to their mouths in the sea, lakes, or other rivers, and constitute one of the most important components of the water cycle by transporting fresh water from continents to oceans, eroding and depositing sediments that shape the landscape, and sustaining aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems of extraordinary biodiversity. A river forms when precipitation water, meltwater, or springs accumulates in natural terrain channels and begins to flow downhill under the influence of gravity, eroding soil and rock to create an increasingly deep and stable channel. The morphology of a river is defined by several characteristic elements: the channel or main course through which water flows; the banks or shores that bound the channel; the floodplains that are periodically flooded; the meanders, which are sharp channel curves caused by differential erosion; the confluences where two or more rivers join; the bifurcations where a river divides into two or more channels; and the mouths where the river empties into the sea or another body of water. Rivers are classified into several orders according to the Strahler system, where the smallest streams without tributaries are first order, the confluence of two first-order streams creates a second-order river, and so on, with the Amazon River reaching an order of twelve as the highest-order river in the world. River lengths vary enormously, from streams of a few meters to the Amazon River at approximately six thousand four hundred kilometers, the Nile at six thousand six hundred and five kilometers, the Yangtze at six thousand three hundred kilometers, and the Mississippi at six thousand two hundred and seventy-five kilometers, although exact measurement depends on the source and mouth definition used.

### Fluvial Processes: Erosion, Transportation, and Deposition

Fluvial processes are the mechanisms by which rivers erode, transport, and deposit rock materials and sediments, shaping the Earth's landscape over millions of years and creating geological formations such as V-shaped valleys, alluvial plains, deltas, estuaries, and canyons. Fluvial erosion occurs through three main mechanisms: abrasion or corrosion, which occurs when sediment particles carried by water scrape and wear away the river bed and banks; hydrolysis, which occurs when water chemically dissolves rock minerals, particularly limestone; and plucking, which occurs when the force of water plucks rock fragments from the river bed. Rivers transport sediments in three ways: dissolved load, which consists of minerals dissolved in water such as calcium, sodium, potassium, and silica that cannot be seen with the naked eye; suspended load, which consists of fine clay and silt particles that remain suspended in water by turbulence, making the water appear cloudy; and bed load or alluvium, which consists of gravel, sand, and cobbles that move along the river bed by rolling, jumping, or sliding. A river's transport capacity depends on its velocity, which in turn depends on the slope, flow rate, and channel shape, so rivers with steep slopes and high flow rates can transport enormous boulders, while rivers with gentle slopes and low flow rates can only transport fine particles. When a river loses velocity, as occurs when it reaches a plain, joins another larger river, or reaches the sea, it deposits its sediment load creating formations such as alluvial plains, alluvial fans, river bars, river islands, deltas, and estuaries, which are some of the most fertile and densely populated terrains in the world.
