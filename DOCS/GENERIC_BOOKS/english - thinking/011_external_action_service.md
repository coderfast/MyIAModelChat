# The European External Action Service

## Chapter 1: Structure and Functioning of the EEAS

### 1.1 Origin and creation of the EEAS

The European External Action Service, known by its acronym EEAS, was formally created on January 1, 2011 as a consequence of the entry into force of the Treaty of Lisbon, which entered into force on December 1, 2009. This institution represented a fundamental innovation in the institutional architecture of the European Union, as it constituted the first truly supranational diplomatic service in European history. Its creation responded to the historically recognized need to provide the Union with a unified voice on the international stage, overcoming the fragmentation that characterized European foreign policy in the preceding decades.

The Treaty of Lisbon established the EEAS in Articles 27 and 27a of the Treaty on European Union, conferring upon it the responsibility for conducting the common foreign and security policy of the Union, as well as for coordinating the Union's external action. The High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy chairs the EEAS and is assisted by two Deputy Secretary-Generals, one for foreign policy matters and another for security and defense matters. This dual structure reflects the dual nature of the service, which encompasses both the civilian and military dimensions of European external action.

The creation of the EEAS entailed the merger of the General Secretariat of the Council and the Directorate-General for External Relations of the European Commission, two structures that had previously operated with partial independence. This integration overcame the institutional rivalries that had weakened the coherence of European foreign policy, creating a single administrative structure at the service of all Member States and European institutions. EEAS staff were recruited from the officials of both institutions, ensuring balanced representation of all political and geographical sensitivities.

Since its creation, the EEAS has undergone significant evolution in its capabilities and geographical reach. Initially, the service had approximately 1,600 officials distributed between the headquarters in Brussels and the delegations abroad. Over the years, this figure has grown to exceed 4,500 employees, reflecting the expansion of the Union's international commitments. The EEAS has established delegations in more than 140 countries and in international organizations, becoming one of the most extensive diplomatic networks in the world.

### 1.2 Internal organization of the EEAS

The internal structure of the EEAS is organized around a Secretary-General who coordinates the different geographical and thematic directorates. The Secretary-General is responsible for the administrative and financial management of the service, as well as for coordination among the various units. Below this are the geographical directorates, which cover the different regions of the world, and the thematic directorates, which deal with cross-cutting issues such as security, trade, or development.

Each geographical Directorate is responsible for designing and implementing Union policy in its corresponding region. The main geographical directorates include Europe and Central Asia, Africa, the Americas, Asia and the Pacific, and the Middle East and North Africa. Each of these directorates has a team of regional experts, which includes political analysts, development specialists, and security advisors. These teams work in close cooperation with Union delegations abroad to ensure the coherence of European foreign policy.

The thematic directorates of the EEAS address issues that transcend geographical boundaries, such as the non-proliferation of weapons of mass destruction, climate change, human rights, or development cooperation. These directorates work in close cooperation with the geographical directorates to integrate thematic dimensions into the Union's regional policy. For example, the Directorate for Security and Defense is responsible for the Union's civilian and military missions, while the Directorate for Legal Affairs advises the service on matters of international and European law.

The EEAS also has specialized units that provide cross-cutting support to the service. These units include the Directorate for Human Resources, the Directorate for Financial Affairs, the Directorate for Communication, and the Directorate for Legal Affairs. These units ensure the efficient functioning of the service and provide officials with the tools and resources necessary to carry out their duties. The Directorate for Human Resources, for example, manages the recruitment, training, and professional development of EEAS staff.

### 1.3 Relations with other EU institutions

The EEAS maintains close and complementary relations with the other institutions of the European Union, particularly with the European Council, the Council of the European Union, and the European Commission. The High Representative, who chairs the EEAS, participates in the meetings of the European Council and the Foreign Affairs Council, where he/she presents reports on the international situation and proposes courses of action. This dual function ensures coherence between the political decisions adopted by the Member States and their implementation by the EEAS.

The relationship between the EEAS and the European Commission is particularly close, given that the High Representative is also Vice-President of the Commission. This dual function enables the EEAS to benefit from the resources and experience of the Commission in areas such as trade, development aid, or energy policy. Moreover, the EEAS works closely with the Commissioners responsible for these policies to ensure the coherence of the Union's external action.

The European Parliament exercises a control function over the EEAS through the Committee on Foreign Affairs and the Subcommittee on Security and Defense. The High Representative periodically appears before these committees to report on the Union's foreign policy and to answer questions from Members of the European Parliament. This parliamentary oversight function contributes to the transparency and accountability of the EEAS to European citizens.

The EEAS also maintains relations with the national parliaments of the Member States, through the foreign affairs committees of the national parliaments. These relations enable the EEAS to benefit from the knowledge and experience of national parliamentarians on foreign policy matters, as well as to ensure the political support of the Member States for Union initiatives on the international stage. This inter-parliamentary cooperation is particularly important during moments of international crisis, when the cohesion of the Member States is essential for an effective response.

### 1.4 EEAS budget and resources

The EEAS budget is financed primarily through the general budget of the European Union, with additional contributions from the Member States for civilian and military missions. The annual EEAS budget has experienced steady growth since its creation, rising from approximately 100 million euros in 2011 to more than 500 million euros in 2023. This growth reflects the expansion of the Union's international commitments and the increasing demand for diplomatic services.

The human resources of the EEAS constitute its most valuable asset. The service has approximately 4,500 officials, distributed between the headquarters in Brussels and the delegations abroad. These officials are recruited through a competitive process managed by the European Personnel Selection Office (EPSO), which ensures equal opportunities and meritocracy in access to positions. EEAS staff enjoys great cultural and linguistic diversity, with representatives from all Member States and a wide variety of academic and professional backgrounds.

The EEAS also benefits from cooperation with other international actors, such as international organizations, national governments, and civil society. This cooperation enables the EEAS to multiply its resources and expand its geographical reach. For example, the EEAS works closely with the United Nations, the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE), and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) in peacekeeping and conflict prevention missions.

The financial management of the EEAS is governed by the principles of sound financial management, transparency, and accountability. The EEAS is subject to the audit of the European Court of Auditors and is required to present annual financial reports to the European Parliament and the Council. These oversight mechanisms ensure the efficient and responsible use of public resources and contribute to the credibility of the EEAS before European citizens.

## Chapter 2: EU Delegations Abroad

### 2.1 Functions and structure of delegations

The European Union delegations abroad are the Union's diplomatic representations to the governments of third countries and to international organizations. They were created following the entry into force of the Treaty of Lisbon, which transferred to the Union the responsibility for the external representation of the Member States in certain areas. Delegations play a fundamental role in the implementation of European foreign policy, acting as the principal link between the Union and its interlocutors abroad.

Each delegation is headed by a Head of Delegation, who acts as the personal representative of the High Representative in the country or organization to which they are accredited. Heads of Delegation are appointed by the Council of the European Union on the proposal of the High Representative and enjoy diplomatic status equivalent to that of the ambassadors of the Member States. Under the direction of the Head of Delegation, delegation staff work on a wide range of issues, from trade policy to development cooperation, including human rights and security.

Union delegations differ from national embassies in several fundamental aspects. First, they represent the Union as a whole and not a particular Member State. Second, their staff are recruited through a European and not a national process. Third, their funding comes from the Union budget and not from national budgets. These differences reflect the supranational nature of the EEAS and its vocation to serve the common European interest.

Union delegations have undergone a significant transformation since their creation. Initially, many delegations were simply the former representation offices of the European Commission, with limited staff and a restricted mandate. Over the years, these delegations have been transformed into full European embassies, with expanded staff and a mandate that encompasses all dimensions of the Union's foreign policy. This transformation has required significant investments in infrastructure, training, and human resources.

### 2.2 Delegations to Member States and third countries

Union delegations are divided into two main categories: delegations to Member States and delegations to third countries. Delegations to Member States, known as permanent representations, represent the Union to the national governments and coordinate the relations between the Union and the corresponding Member State. These delegations play an important role in gathering information on national positions on foreign policy and in promoting Union interests to national governments.

Permanent representations are composed of national diplomats designated by the governments of the Member States and by EEAS European officials. Each permanent representation is headed by a Permanent Representative, who acts as the ambassador of the Member State to the Union. However, permanent representations also include EEAS staff who work in close cooperation with national staff to ensure the coherence of European foreign policy.

Delegations to third countries, for their part, represent the Union to the governments of countries that are not members of the Union. These delegations are the true European embassies and play a central role in the implementation of the Union's foreign policy abroad. Delegations to third countries vary in size and scope depending on the strategic importance of the host country and the breadth of relations between the Union and that country.

Union delegations in third countries usually have specialized sections covering the different dimensions of European foreign policy. These sections include the political section, the development cooperation section, the trade section, the press and communication section, and the administrative and financial section. Each section is responsible for managing matters within its competence in close cooperation with the host country authorities and with European institutions.

### 2.3 Delegations to international organizations

Union delegations to international organizations represent the Union to major international organizations, such as the United Nations, the Organization of American States (OAS), the African Union, or the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN). These delegations play a fundamental role in promoting Union interests in the multilateral arena and in coordinating European foreign policy with that of other international actors.

Delegations to international organizations benefit from a special diplomatic status that allows them to participate in the meetings and decision-making processes of the corresponding organization. Heads of Delegation to international organizations usually hold the rank of ambassadors and enjoy great autonomy in the exercise of their functions. This autonomy is particularly important in the case of delegations to international organizations with significant decision-making capacity, such as the United Nations Security Council.

Union delegations to international organizations work in close cooperation with the national delegations of the Member States in the corresponding organization. This cooperation takes place through coordination mechanisms established by the Treaty of Lisbon, which provide for the participation of the Member States in formulating the Union's position to international organizations. Heads of Delegation to international organizations are assisted by national working groups that represent the positions of the Member States in the corresponding organization.

The activity of Union delegations to international organizations has grown significantly in recent years, reflecting the increasing importance of multilateralism in European foreign policy. Union delegations have played an important role in promoting multilateral solutions to global problems, such as climate change, nuclear proliferation, or international migration. This multilateral activity is particularly relevant at a time when the rules-based multilateral system faces unprecedented challenges.

### 2.4 Coordination with national embassies

The coordination between Union delegations and national embassies of the Member States is an essential element of the functioning of the EEAS. This coordination takes place through formal and informal mechanisms that allow the development of common positions and the implementation of joint actions. Formal mechanisms include the meetings of the Committee of Permanent Representatives (COREPER) and the specialized committees of the Council, while informal mechanisms include regular meetings between Heads of Delegation and national ambassadors in host countries.

The coordination between Union delegations and national embassies is particularly important in situations of international crisis, when the speed and coherence of the response are essential. In these cases, Union delegations act as coordination centers that enable the rapid mobilization of Member State resources and the adoption of common positions. This coordination capability is one of the main assets of the EEAS and enables it to play a leadership role in the international response to crises.

The cooperation between Union delegations and national embassies also extends to areas such as consular assistance, the protection of European citizens abroad, and the promotion of trade and investment. In these areas, Union delegations and national embassies work in close complementarity, leveraging the comparative advantages of each. Union delegations usually have a greater presence in regions where Member States have limited presence, while national embassies usually have additional resources for specific sectors.

The coordination between Union delegations and national embassies also poses challenges, particularly with regard to the management of differences of opinion among the Member States. In some cases, national positions may diverge significantly, making it difficult to adopt common positions. The EEAS has developed mechanisms to manage these divergences, such as the mediation of the High Representative and informal negotiation processes, but these mechanisms are not always sufficient to overcome the political differences between the Member States.

## Chapter 3: The Common Foreign and Security Policy

### 3.1 Concept and scope of the CFSP

The Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP) constitutes the fundamental framework for the definition and implementation of the foreign policy of the European Union. It was established by the Treaty of Maastricht in 1992 and has undergone significant evolution over the decades, particularly with the entry into force of the Treaties of Amsterdam, Nice, and Lisbon. The CFSP encompasses all dimensions of the Union's foreign policy, from diplomacy to defense, including development cooperation and humanitarian aid.

The scope of the CFSP is extraordinarily broad and encompasses issues as diverse as conflict prevention, crisis management, the non-proliferation of weapons of mass destruction, international terrorism, climate change, migration, or human rights. This breadth reflects the complexity of the international environment and the multitude of challenges facing the Union on the global stage. The CFSP is complemented by other Union policies, such as trade policy, development policy, or energy policy, which contribute to the achievement of the objectives of European foreign policy.

The CFSP is based on the principles of Member State sovereignty, unanimity in decision-making, and respect for international law. These principles reflect the intergovernmental nature of the CFSP, which differs from the Union's supranational policies. However, the Treaty of Lisbon has introduced important innovations that have strengthened the European character of the CFSP, such as the creation of the EEAS, the appointment of a High Representative, and the adoption of decisions by qualified majority in certain areas.

The CFSP draws on a wide range of instruments, including bilateral and multilateral diplomacy, economic sanctions, development aid, civilian and military missions, and security cooperation. These instruments are managed by the EEAS in close cooperation with the Member States and with the other Union institutions. The combination of these instruments enables the Union to play an active role on the international stage and to contribute to peace, security, and sustainable development on a global scale.

### 3.2 Principles and objectives of the CFSP

The CFSP is governed by a set of fundamental principles that guide the Union's external action. These principles include democracy, the rule of law, human rights, and fundamental freedoms, which constitute the pillars of European foreign policy. The Union promotes these principles in its relations with third countries and in its participation in international organizations, contributing to the strengthening of the rules-based international system.

The objectives of the CFSP are set out in Article 21 of the Treaty on European Union and include the defense of the values and interests of the Union, the consolidation and support of democracy, the rule of law, human rights, and the principles of international law, the preservation of peace, conflict prevention, and the strengthening of international security, the promotion of international cooperation and sustainable development, and the promotion of a rules-based multilateral order. These objectives reflect the Union's ambition to play a leadership role on the international stage and to contribute to the construction of a more just and secure world.

The promotion of democracy and human rights is one of the fundamental pillars of the CFSP. The Union has established a set of instruments to promote these values abroad, such as conditionality mechanisms, programs supporting civil society, and parliamentary diplomacy initiatives. The Union also actively participates in international human rights protection mechanisms, such as the United Nations Human Rights Council, and promotes the adoption of international instruments on human rights.

Conflict prevention and crisis management are other fundamental objectives of the CFSP. The Union has established a set of civilian and military capabilities that enable it to intervene rapidly in crisis situations, both in the area of prevention and in the management of the consequences of conflicts. These capabilities include the Union's civilian and military missions, rapid response teams, and coordination mechanisms with other international organizations, such as NATO and the United Nations.

### 3.3 CFSP instruments

The CFSP has a wide range of instruments that enable it to deploy its external action effectively. These instruments include political decisions, declarations, diplomatic démarches, economic sanctions, development aid, civilian and military missions, and security cooperation. The combination of these instruments enables the Union to adapt its response to the different situations and actors on the international stage.

Economic sanctions constitute one of the most frequently used instruments by the CFSP to promote its objectives. The Union has imposed sanctions on a large number of countries, groups, and individuals in recent years, in response to violations of international law, attacks on human rights, or threats to international peace and security. Union sanctions include trade restrictions, asset freezes, travel bans, and other restrictive measures. These sanctions are adopted by the Council of the European Union on the proposal of the High Representative and require unanimity of the Member States.

Development aid is another fundamental instrument of the CFSP. The Union and its Member States are the world's largest donors of development aid, with an annual contribution exceeding 100 billion euros. This aid is directed at combating poverty, promoting sustainable development, strengthening governance, and consolidating peace in developing countries. The Union's development aid is managed through the Directorate-General for International Partnerships of the European Commission, in close cooperation with the EEAS and the Member States.

The Union's civilian and military missions are instruments of external action that enable the Union to intervene directly in crisis situations. These missions are deployed under the authority of the Council of the European Union and are directed by the EEAS. Civilian missions include electoral assistance, security sector reform, and rule of law consolidation, while military missions include peacekeeping operations, counter-piracy operations, and training of local security forces.

### 3.4 Cooperation with international organizations

Cooperation with international organizations is an essential element of the CFSP. The Union maintains close relations with a large number of international organizations, such as the United Nations, NATO, the OSCE, the Council of Europe, the Organization of American States (OAS), and the African Union. These relations are based on the principles of complementarity, mutual respect, and the pursuit of common results.

Cooperation with the United Nations is particularly important for the CFSP. The Union is the largest financial contributor to the United Nations and actively participates in its decision-making mechanisms, particularly in the Security Council, where several Member States are permanent or non-permanent members. The Union also makes a significant contribution to United Nations peacekeeping operations, both in terms of personnel and financial resources.

Cooperation with NATO is another fundamental pillar of European foreign and security policy. Although the Union and NATO are distinct organizations with different mandates, they share a large number of common members and face similar security challenges. Cooperation between the Union and NATO encompasses areas such as collective defense, crisis management, counter-terrorism, and cybersecurity. However, this cooperation is also marked by tensions, particularly with regard to European strategic autonomy and the transatlantic relationship.

Cooperation with the OSCE is particularly relevant in the area of European security. The OSCE is the world's largest regional security organization, with 57 participating States spanning from Canada to Central Asia. The Union and the OSCE share a large number of common objectives, such as conflict prevention, the promotion of human rights, and the consolidation of democracy. Cooperation between the Union and the OSCE takes place through consultation and coordination mechanisms established by treaties and bilateral agreements.

## Chapter 4: Cooperation with Member States

### 4.1 Political coordination mechanisms

Political coordination between the EEAS and the Member States is fundamental to ensuring the coherence and effectiveness of European foreign policy. This coordination takes place through a wide range of institutional and informal mechanisms that allow the Member States to participate in the formulation, implementation, and evaluation of the Union's foreign policy. The most important mechanisms include the Foreign Affairs Council, the Committee of Permanent Representatives (COREPER), and the specialized committees of the Council.

The Foreign Affairs Council is the main decision-making body in matters of common foreign and security policy. Chaired by the High Representative, it brings together the foreign ministers of the Member States at least four times a year to define the general guidelines of the CFSP and to adopt the necessary decisions. The Foreign Affairs Council also coordinates the Union's action in matters of defense and security, including the development of military capabilities and the planning of operations.

The Committee of Permanent Representatives (COREPER) is the preparatory body of the Council of the European Union that deals with foreign and security affairs. Composed of the Permanent Representatives of the Member States to the Union, COREPER prepares the meetings of the Foreign Affairs Council and ensures the implementation of its decisions. COREPER also deals with the coordination between Union delegations and national embassies in host countries.

The specialized committees of the Council play an important role in the coordination of European foreign policy in specific areas. These committees include the Political and Security Committee (PSC), which advises the Foreign Affairs Council on foreign and security policy, and the Military Committee (MC), which deals with military and defense matters. These committees bring together experts from the Member States who work in close cooperation with the EEAS to ensure the coherence of European foreign policy.

### 4.2 Participation in the formulation of foreign policy

The Member States participate actively in the formulation of the Union's foreign policy through the institutional mechanisms established by the Treaties. This participation is exercised at different levels, from the participation of the Heads of State and Government in the European Council to the participation of national officials in the specialized committees of the Council. Member State participation is particularly important in the area of foreign and security policy, where decisions require unanimity.

Member State participation in the formulation of European foreign policy takes place through consultation and negotiation processes that allow the incorporation of national positions into the Union's common position. These processes include informal meetings of foreign ministers, meetings of national ambassadors in Brussels, and the exchange of information between national diplomatic services and the EEAS. Prior consultation is particularly important during moments of international crisis, when the speed and coherence of the response are essential.

The Member States also participate in the formulation of European foreign policy through their representatives in international organizations. In the United Nations Security Council, for example, the Member States that are permanent members (France and the United Kingdom) and Germany as a non-permanent member coordinate their positions with the EEAS to ensure the coherence of European foreign policy in the multilateral arena. This coordination is particularly important during moments of international crisis, when the decisions of the Security Council have a direct impact on international peace and security.

Member State participation in the formulation of European foreign policy is also exercised through the Council's decision-making processes. In the area of the CFSP, decisions are adopted by unanimity, which gives each Member State a veto right. This requirement of unanimity reflects the intergovernmental nature of the CFSP and the importance that the Member States attach to their sovereignty in matters of foreign policy. However, unanimity can also make it difficult to adopt quick and effective decisions, particularly in crisis situations.

### 4.3 Joint implementation of actions

The joint implementation of actions between the EEAS and the Member States is an essential element of European foreign policy. This implementation takes place through coordination mechanisms that enable the mobilization of Union and Member State resources for the achievement of common objectives. The most important mechanisms include the Union's civilian and military missions, development cooperation programs, and joint diplomatic initiatives.

The Union's civilian and military missions are instruments of external action that enable the joint implementation of actions between the Union and the Member States. These missions are deployed under the authority of the Council of the European Union and are directed by the EEAS. The Member States contribute to the missions with personnel, financial resources, and equipment, while the EEAS provides strategic direction and operational coordination. This cooperation enables the Union to deploy civilian and military capabilities that would be unattainable for each Member State individually.

Development cooperation is another area of joint implementation between the EEAS and the Member States. The Union and its Member States are the world's largest donors of development aid, and their cooperation is based on principles of complementarity, alignment, and efficiency. The EEAS coordinates the development strategies of the Union with those of the Member States to ensure the coherence and effectiveness of aid. This coordination takes place through mechanisms such as partnership agreements, national cooperation frameworks, and joint evaluation processes.

Joint diplomatic initiatives are another instrument of joint implementation between the EEAS and the Member States. These initiatives include joint statements, concerted diplomatic démarches, and solidarity actions in crisis situations. Joint diplomatic initiatives enable the Union and the Member States to multiply their influence on the international stage and to demonstrate their cohesion on foreign policy issues. These initiatives are particularly important during moments of international crisis, when the speed and coherence of the response are essential.

### 4.4 Evaluation and monitoring of cooperation

The evaluation and monitoring of cooperation between the EEAS and the Member States are fundamental to ensuring the effectiveness of European foreign policy. This evaluation takes place through institutional mechanisms that enable the measurement of the impact of joint actions, the identification of lessons learned, and the formulation of recommendations for improving future cooperation. The most important mechanisms include periodic evaluations, monitoring reports, and organizational learning processes.

Periodic evaluations of cooperation between the EEAS and the Member States are carried out by the EEAS in close cooperation with the Member States. These evaluations measure the degree of achievement of objectives, the efficiency in the use of resources, and the quality of coordination between the parties. Evaluations also identify success factors and difficulties encountered, as well as lessons learned for improving future cooperation. The results of evaluations are presented to the Foreign Affairs Council and the European Parliament for their information and follow-up.

Monitoring reports are management tools that enable the continuous monitoring of cooperation between the EEAS and the Member States. These reports include performance indicators that enable the measurement of progress in the implementation of joint actions. Monitoring reports are prepared by the EEAS and shared with the Member States for their information and contribution. These reports are particularly important during moments of international crisis, when the speed and effectiveness of the response are essential.

Organizational learning processes are instruments that enable the EEAS and the Member States to identify and share best practices in external cooperation. These processes include experience-sharing meetings, training seminars, and case studies. Organizational learning processes are particularly important for the continuous improvement of cooperation between the EEAS and the Member States, as well as for the adaptation of European foreign policy to changes in the international environment.

## Chapter 5: EU Civilian and Military Missions

### 5.1 Overview of missions

The European Union has deployed a significant number of civilian and military missions since the creation of the Common Security and Defence Policy in 2003. These missions represent one of the most visible and concrete instruments of the Union's external action and enable it to contribute directly to peace, security, and stability in different regions of the world. From the first military mission in the Republic of Macedonia (EUFOR Althea) to the most recent civilian missions, the Union has accumulated significant experience in international crisis management.

The Union's civilian missions focus on conflict prevention, rule of law consolidation, security sector reform, and electoral assistance. These missions are deployed under the authority of the Council of the European Union and are directed by the EEAS. Civilian missions have specialized civilian staff, which includes judges, police officers, security sector reform experts, and electoral observers. These missions are funded by the European Peace Facility and, previously, by the Instrument contributing to Stability and Peace.

The Union's military missions, for their part, focus on peacekeeping, counter-piracy, training of local security forces, and deterrence. These missions are deployed under the authority of the Council of the European Union and are directed by the EEAS in cooperation with the European Union Military Staff. Military missions have military personnel from the Member States and are funded by the European Peace Facility.

The combination of civilian and military missions enables the Union to address international crises in a comprehensive manner, encompassing both the civilian and military dimensions. This comprehensive approach is one of the Union's main strengths in crisis management and enables it to contribute effectively to the prevention and resolution of conflicts in different regions of the world. The Union has deployed more than 30 civilian and military missions since 2003, with an active presence in Africa, the Balkans, the Middle East, and Asia.

### 5.2 Civilian missions in detail

The European Union's civilian missions represent a fundamental instrument for conflict prevention and rule of law consolidation in regions affected by crisis. These missions are deployed under the authority of the Council of the European Union and are directed by the EEAS, which provides strategic direction and operational coordination. Civilian missions have specialized civilian staff, which includes judges, prosecutors, police officers, security sector reform experts, electoral observers, and governance specialists.

One of the Union's most successful civilian missions has been EULEX Kosovo, which was deployed in 2008 to assist the Kosovar authorities in consolidating the rule of law. EULEX had a staff of approximately 2,000 people, including international judges, prosecutors, and police officers, and played an important role in reforming Kosovo's judicial and police systems. Although the mission faced significant challenges, such as the lack of cooperation from Serbia and the complexity of the local political environment, it contributed substantially to the consolidation of the rule of law in Kosovo.

The EUPOL Afghanistan mission was another important civilian mission of the Union, deployed in 2007 to support security sector reform in Afghanistan. The mission had a staff of approximately 200 police officers and civilian experts and worked in close cooperation with the NATO mission in Afghanistan (ISAF). EUPOL Afghanistan contributed to the training of the Afghan police and to the reform of the criminal justice system, but faced significant challenges due to persistent insecurity and lack of resources. The mission was finally withdrawn in 2016, coinciding with the withdrawal of international forces from Afghanistan.

Other important civilian missions of the Union include EUSEC RD Congo, which supported security sector reform in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and EUCAP Sahel Mali, which provided training to security forces in the Sahel. These missions demonstrate the Union's versatility in crisis management and its ability to adapt its tools to the specific needs of each situation. The Union's civilian missions benefit from the experience accumulated in previous missions and from the organizational learning mechanisms established by the EEAS.

### 5.3 Military missions in detail

The European Union's military missions constitute a fundamental instrument for conflict prevention and crisis management in regions affected by insecurity. These missions are deployed under the authority of the Council of the European Union and are directed by the EEAS in cooperation with the European Union Military Staff. Military missions have military personnel from the Member States and are funded by the European Peace Facility.

The Union's most important military mission has been EUFOR Althea, deployed in Bosnia and Herzegovina since 2004 to replace the NATO force (SFOR). EUFOR Althea initially had approximately 7,000 soldiers and played a crucial role in consolidating peace and stability in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Over the years, the force has been progressively reduced as the security situation has improved, but it maintains a significant presence to ensure long-term stability.

Operation Atalanta, deployed since 2008 in the Horn of Africa, is the Union's largest naval mission. Its main objective is counter-piracy in the Indian Ocean and the protection of international maritime shipping. Atalanta has achieved significant success in reducing acts of piracy and has contributed to improving maritime security in the region. The mission has warships from the Member States, patrol aircraft, and special forces, and works closely with other international forces present in the region.

Other important military missions of the Union include EUTM Mali, which provides training to the armed forces of Mali, and EUNAVFOR MED, which combats human trafficking in the central Mediterranean. These missions demonstrate the Union's capacity to deploy military forces in complex and hostile environments, and its commitment to international peace and security. The Union's military missions benefit from cooperation with NATO and other international organizations, as well as from the coordination mechanisms established by the Treaties.

### 5.4 Financing and sustainability of missions

The financing of the European Union's civilian and military missions is a crucial aspect of their sustainability and effectiveness. These missions are financed through specific instruments established by the Council of the European Union, which enable the rapid mobilization of resources for crisis management. The most important financing instruments include the European Peace Facility (EPF), which replaced the Instrument contributing to Stability and Peace in 2021, and the European Defence Fund (EDF).

The European Peace Facility (EPF) is the main financing instrument for the Union's civilian and military missions. With a budget of more than 17 billion euros for the period 2021-2027, the EPF enables the financing of civilian and military operations, the training of security forces in third countries, and investment in defence capabilities. The EPF benefits from contributions from the Member States and the Union budget, and is managed by the EEAS in close cooperation with the Member States.

The European Defence Fund (EDF) is a financing instrument that enables the Member States to finance joint defence actions, such as research, development, and the acquisition of military capabilities. The EDF has a budget of more than 13 billion euros for the period 2021-2027 and is open to the participation of Member States that wish to cooperate in the area of defence. The EDF is particularly important for the development of military capabilities that enable the Union to deploy forces more quickly and effectively in crisis situations.

The sustainability of the Union's civilian and military missions depends on the availability of adequate financial resources, the active participation of the Member States, and the capacity of the EEAS to manage operations efficiently. The EEAS has developed mechanisms to improve the efficiency of mission management, such as the centralization of support functions, the standardization of procedures, and the improvement of coordination with the Member States. These efforts are particularly important in a context of limited financial resources and increasing demand for missions.

## Chapter 6: The EEAS and Citizens

### 6.1 Protection of European citizens abroad

The EEAS plays a fundamental role in the protection of European citizens abroad, particularly in situations of international crisis. This function is carried out in close cooperation with the embassies and consulates of the Member States, but the EEAS also has its own competences for the protection of European citizens. The protection of citizens abroad is one of the most visible functions of the EEAS and contributes to citizens' perception of the usefulness of the Union in their daily lives.

The EEAS has developed mechanisms for the protection of European citizens abroad, which include traveler registration systems, telephone assistance lines, and emergency evacuation mechanisms. These mechanisms enable the EEAS to locate and assist European citizens in crisis situations, such as natural disasters, armed conflicts, or acts of terrorism. ## Chapter 11: The EEAS and Security

### 11.1 Common Security and Defence Policy

The Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP) is the military and security component of the Common Foreign and Security Policy of the European Union. It was established by the Treaty of Amsterdam in 1997 and has undergone significant evolution over the decades, particularly with the entry into force of the Treaties of Nice and Lisbon. The CSDP provides the Union with the civilian and military capabilities necessary to act in the area of security and defense.

The EEAS plays a central role in the implementation of the CSDP, being responsible for the planning and direction of the Union's civilian and military missions. The High Representative, who chairs the EEAS, is also the Union's representative on foreign and security policy, and coordinates the action of the Member States in the area of defense. The EEAS works in close cooperation with the European Union Military Staff (EUMS) for the planning and direction of military operations.

The CSDP is complemented by Permanent Structured Cooperation (PESCO), which was established by the Treaty of Lisbon to enable a group of Member States to develop military capabilities and to commit to participating in military operations. The EEAS coordinates the implementation of PESCO and promotes Member State participation in defense cooperation initiatives. PESCO is an important instrument for the development of European military capabilities and for the consolidation of the Union's strategic autonomy.

The CSDP also includes the European Defence Agency (EDA), which aims to strengthen the Union's defence capacity, promote cooperation in defence research and development, and support the industrialization of military capabilities. The EEAS works in close cooperation with the EDA to identify capability needs and to promote cooperation among the Member States in the area of defence. The EDA is a key actor in the development of European strategic autonomy.

Cybersecurity and digital defence are areas of increasing importance for the foreign and security policy of the European Union. The EEAS also participates in multilateral forums such as the United Nations Group of Governmental Experts in the field of information security.

The EEAS also coordinates the Union's response to cyberattacks through the mechanisms of the Common Security and Defence Policy. The Union has adopted a set of instruments to prevent and respond to cyberattacks, such as the NIS2 Directive (Security of Network and Information Systems), the Union's Cybersecurity Strategy, and the Union's Cybersecurity Incident Response Mechanism (EU-CyCLONe). The EEAS coordinates the implementation of these instruments in the external dimension and promotes cooperation with international partners.

Digital defence is another area of growing importance for the EEAS. Digital defence includes the protection of critical infrastructure, the detection and response to cyberattacks, and the training of security forces in cybersecurity. The EEAS works in close cooperation with the Member States and with the EDA to strengthen the Union's digital defence capabilities and to promote cooperation in this area. Digital defence is an essential component of European strategic autonomy in the 21st century.

## Chapter 12: The EEAS and Development Cooperation

### 12.1 The Union's strategy for development

Development cooperation is one of the most important dimensions of the European Union's external action. The Union and its Member States are the world's largest donors of development aid, with an annual contribution exceeding 100 billion euros. The EEAS plays a fundamental role in coordinating the Union's development policy, in close cooperation with the European Commission and the Member States.

The Union's development strategy is based on the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and on the principles of alignment, complementarity, and efficiency. The strategy defines the priorities of the Union's development policy, which include combating poverty, promoting sustainable development, strengthening governance, and consolidating peace. The EEAS coordinates the implementation of the strategy and promotes the coherence of development policy with other Union policies.

The Union has established an institutional framework for its development policy that includes the European Development Fund (EDF), the Neighbourhood, Development and International Cooperation Instrument (NDICI-Global Europe), and the Overseas Countries and Territories (OCT) Association Instrument. These instruments provide the financial resources necessary for the implementation of the Union's development policy. The EEAS coordinates the management of these instruments and promotes their effective use.

The Union's development policy is also complemented by other policies that contribute to sustainable development, such as trade policy, energy policy, migration policy, and research policy. The EEAS promotes the coherence of these policies with the sustainable development objectives and works to ensure that all Union actions contribute to the development of partner countries. This comprehensive approach is one of the main strengths of the Union's development policy.

### 12.2 Geographical and thematic priorities

The Union's development policy is structured around geographical and thematic priorities that reflect the needs of partner countries and the interests of the Union. Geographical priorities focus on the countries and regions most in need of development aid, particularly in Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Caribbean. Thematic priorities focus on areas where the Union can provide significant added value, such as education, health, agriculture, energy, and the environment.

Africa is the main geographical priority of the Union's development policy. The African continent is home to the majority of the world's least developed countries and faces significant development challenges, such as poverty, inequality, political instability, and climate change. The Union has adopted a Comprehensive Strategy for Africa that defines the priorities of development cooperation on the continent. The EEAS coordinates the implementation of this strategy and promotes cooperation with African partners.

The thematic priorities of the Union's development policy include education, health, sustainable agriculture, renewable energy, and governance. Education is a fundamental priority because it is a driver of economic and social development. Health is another important priority, particularly in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, which has highlighted the importance of strong health systems. Sustainable agriculture is a priority because it is essential for food security and poverty reduction in rural areas.

Renewable energy is a priority because it is fundamental to the energy transition and the fight against climate change. Governance is a priority because it is a prerequisite for sustainable development and peace consolidation. The EEAS promotes the integration of these thematic priorities into the Union's development cooperation and works to ensure that development aid is effective and sustainable.

### 12.3 Aid effectiveness and accountability

Aid effectiveness and accountability are fundamental principles of the Union's development policy. The Union is committed to using its development aid resources efficiently, effectively, and transparently, and to being accountable to European citizens and partner countries for the results of its aid. The EEAS plays a fundamental role in promoting aid effectiveness and accountability.

Aid effectiveness is measured through outcome indicators that enable the evaluation of the impact of aid on the development of partner countries. The Union has adopted a results framework that defines the objectives of aid and the indicators for measuring its achievement. The EEAS coordinates the collection and analysis of data on aid results and promotes their use for decision-making on development policy.

Accountability is promoted through transparency and participation mechanisms. The Union publishes annual reports on its development aid, which include information on the resources allocated, the programs implemented, and the results achieved. The EEAS also promotes the participation of European citizens and partner countries in the evaluation and monitoring of aid. These accountability mechanisms contribute to the credibility and legitimacy of the Union's development policy.

The Union also promotes aid effectiveness through the harmonization of aid procedures with those of other donors. The Union has actively participated in international aid effectiveness processes, such as the Paris Declaration on Aid Effectiveness and the Accra Action Agenda. The EEAS coordinates the implementation of the Union's international commitments on aid effectiveness and promotes cooperation with other donors to improve the quality of aid.

### 12.4 South-South and triangular cooperation

South-South and triangular cooperation is an increasingly important instrument of the Union's development policy. South-South cooperation refers to cooperation among developing countries, while triangular cooperation involves a developing country, a traditional donor, and a multilateral organization. The EEAS promotes these forms of cooperation as complements to the Union's traditional bilateral aid.

South-South cooperation enables developing countries to share knowledge, experiences, and resources to address development challenges. The Union has established South-South cooperation programs that support cooperation initiatives among developing countries, particularly in Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The EEAS coordinates these programs and promotes partner country participation in South-South cooperation initiatives.

Triangular cooperation combines the resources and experience of multiple actors to address development challenges. The Union has established triangular cooperation programs that involve developing countries, traditional donors, and multilateral organizations. The EEAS coordinates these programs and promotes cooperation among the different actors. Triangular cooperation is particularly effective in areas such as agriculture, energy, and health, where the combination of resources and knowledge can generate significant results.

The EEAS also promotes the use of new technologies and innovative approaches in development cooperation. These innovations include the use of digital technology to improve aid efficiency, the use of innovative financing mechanisms such as social impact bonds, and the promotion of public-private partnerships for development. The EEAS works in close cooperation with international partners to promote these innovations and to adapt them to the needs of partner countries.

## Chapter 13: Conclusions and Future Perspectives

### 13.1 Assessment of the EEAS

The assessment of the EEAS since its creation in 2011 is mixed. On the one hand, the service has succeeded in consolidating a significant diplomatic presence throughout the world, with delegations in more than 140 countries and an increasingly extensive network of bilateral and multilateral relations. The EEAS has deployed more than 30 civilian and military missions, contributing to peace and security in different regions of the world. The EEAS has also effectively promoted European values of democracy, human rights, and the rule of law on the international stage.

On the other hand, the EEAS faces significant challenges that limit its effectiveness. The unanimity required in CFSP decision-making often makes it difficult to adopt common positions, particularly in crisis situations. Competition among Union institutions and between the EEAS and national diplomatic services sometimes weakens the coherence of European foreign policy. Furthermore, the lack of adequate financial and human resources limits the capacity of the EEAS to respond effectively to all the challenges it faces.

Despite these challenges, the EEAS has demonstrated its value as an instrument of Union external action. The service has contributed to strengthening the voice of the Union on the international stage and has improved the coordination of European foreign policy. The EEAS has also demonstrated its capacity to adapt to changes in the international environment and to respond to new threats, such as terrorism, cybersecurity, and climate change.

The assessment of the EEAS must also be evaluated in the broader context of the evolution of the European Union. The creation of the EEAS was an important step in the process of European integration in the area of foreign policy, but this process is far from complete. The Union continues to face significant challenges in foreign policy, such as the need to strengthen its strategic autonomy, improve its crisis management capacity, and promote a rules-based multilateral order.

### 13.2 Future strategic priorities

The future strategic priorities of the EEAS are shaped by the need to adapt to changes in the international environment and to strengthen its capacity to implement European foreign policy. These priorities include the strengthening of European strategic autonomy, the improvement of crisis management capacity, the promotion of a rules-based multilateral order, and the deepening of cooperation with international partners.

The strengthening of European strategic autonomy is the most important strategic priority for the EEAS. Strategic autonomy refers to the capacity of the Union to act independently on the international stage, without depending on other actors for the protection of its interests and values. The EEAS works in close cooperation with the Member States and with the European Commission to strengthen the Union's civilian and military capabilities, develop European defense industries, and promote security cooperation.

The improvement of crisis management capacity is another fundamental strategic priority. Crisis management requires the capacity to rapidly deploy civilian and military resources in emergency situations, as well as the capacity to coordinate the action of multiple actors. The EEAS has adopted measures to improve its crisis management capacity, such as the creation of rapid response teams and the improvement of coordination mechanisms with the Member States.

The promotion of a rules-based multilateral order is another crucial strategic priority. The rules-based multilateral order is fundamental to international peace, security, and prosperity, and the Union has a vital interest in its preservation. The EEAS works in close cooperation with international organizations, national governments, and civil society to promote respect for international law, the peaceful settlement of disputes, and international cooperation.

### 13.3 Long-term vision

The long-term vision of the EEAS is shaped by the aspiration to become a first-rate diplomatic actor on the international stage. To achieve this vision, the EEAS needs to overcome the internal and external challenges it faces and to seize the opportunities offered by the constantly changing international environment. These initiatives contribute to strengthening citizens' trust in the EEAS and to promoting public support for European foreign policy.

The legacy and impact of the EEAS is measured by its contribution to international peace, security, and prosperity, as well as by its role in promoting European values on the global stage. ## Chapter 15: The EEAS and Neighbourhood Policy

### 15.1 The EU's neighbourhood strategy

The European Union's Neighbourhood Policy is a strategic framework that defines the Union's relations with its close neighbours, both to the east and to the south. It was established in 2004 in response to the Union's enlargement, which brought the Union's borders closer to new neighbours. The Neighbourhood Policy aims to promote stability, security, and prosperity in neighbouring regions, preventing the emergence of new dividing lines in Europe. The EEAS plays a fundamental role in the implementation of the Neighbourhood Policy, coordinating the Union's external action in neighbouring regions and working in close cooperation with the Member States and with European institutions.

The Neighbourhood Policy is structured around two geographical dimensions: the Eastern Partnership, which encompasses the six eastern partner countries (Armenia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Georgia, Moldova, and Ukraine), and the Southern Dimension, which encompasses the countries of the Southern Mediterranean and the Middle East (Algeria, Egypt, Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Palestine, Syria, and Tunisia). Each of these dimensions has specific strategies adapted to the needs and characteristics of the partner countries. The EEAS coordinates the implementation of these strategies and promotes the deepening of relations between the Union and its neighbours.

The main instruments of the Neighbourhood Policy include the Association Agreements, the Action Plans, the Eastern Partnership, and the Union for the Mediterranean. The Association Agreements are legal frameworks that regulate the relations between the Union and the partner countries, including provisions on trade, political cooperation, and human dialogue. The Action Plans define the priorities of cooperation between the Union and each partner country, establishing concrete objectives and deadlines for their achievement. The EEAS coordinates the implementation of these instruments and promotes the deepening of relations with partner countries.

### 15.2 Eastern Partnership

The Eastern Partnership is the component of the Neighbourhood Policy that encompasses the six eastern partner countries: Armenia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Georgia, Moldova, and Ukraine. It was launched in 2009 as a joint initiative of the European Commission and the Council of the European Union to promote the political and economic integration of the eastern partner countries with the Union. The EEAS plays a fundamental role in the implementation of the Eastern Partnership, coordinating the Union's external action in the region and promoting the deepening of relations with partner countries.

The Eastern Partnership is based on the Association Agreements, which are legal frameworks that regulate the relations between the Union and each partner country. These agreements include provisions on free trade, political cooperation, human dialogue, and people-to-people mobility. The Association Agreements also include commitments on democratic reforms, the rule of law, and human rights, which are conditions for the deepening of the relationship. The EEAS coordinates the implementation of the Association Agreements and promotes compliance with commitments by partner countries.

The Eastern Partnership has faced significant challenges in recent years, particularly due to instability in the region. The conflict in Ukraine, the political situation in Belarus, and tensions in the Caucasus have tested the Union's capacity to maintain cohesion and effectiveness in its policy towards the region. The EEAS has worked to maintain Member State unity in its response to these crises, to promote the peaceful resolution of conflicts, and to support democratic reforms in partner countries. Despite these challenges, the Eastern Partnership has achieved significant advances in deepening relations between the Union and the eastern partner countries.

### 15.3 Southern Dimension of the Neighbourhood Policy

The Southern Dimension of the Neighbourhood Policy encompasses the countries of the Southern Mediterranean and the Middle East with which the Union maintains bilateral relations within the framework of the Neighbourhood Policy. These countries include Algeria, Egypt, Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Palestine, Syria, and Tunisia. The Southern Dimension aims to promote stability, security, and prosperity in the Southern Mediterranean, a region that faces significant challenges in governance, economic development, and human security. The EEAS coordinates the Union's external action in the region and promotes the deepening of relations with partner countries.

The Union for the Mediterranean is the main regional cooperation forum in the Southern Dimension. Launched in 2008, the Union for the Mediterranean brings together the 27 Member States of the Union and 15 partner countries of the Southern Mediterranean and the Middle East to promote regional cooperation in areas such as stability, prosperity, and human integration. The EEAS actively participates in the Union for the Mediterranean and promotes the implementation of concrete regional cooperation projects, such as the interconnection of energy infrastructure, the protection of the marine environment, and the promotion of youth and employment.

The Southern Dimension faces significant challenges, particularly political instability, armed conflicts, and migration flows. The conflict in Syria, the situation in Libya, the political crisis in Tunisia, and tensions in the Middle East are challenges that have a direct impact on the Union, through migration, terrorism, and insecurity. The EEAS has worked to address these challenges through a combination of diplomacy, development aid, and civilian and military missions. The Southern Dimension of the Neighbourhood Policy remains a priority for European foreign policy, and the EEAS continues to work to promote stability and prosperity in the region.

## Chapter 17: The EEAS and Human Rights

The promotion and protection of human rights are a fundamental pillar of the foreign policy of the European Union and a core responsibility of the EEAS. The EEAS promotes respect for these rights both in the internal sphere and in the Union's external relations.

The international human rights normative framework constitutes another fundamental reference for the action of the EEAS. The EEAS promotes the ratification and implementation of these treaties by third countries, and works to strengthen international human rights protection mechanisms.

The EEAS is also governed by the Union's internal principles and guidelines on human rights. These principles include the universality, indivisibility, interdependence, and interrelation of all human rights, as well as non-discrimination and gender equality. The EEAS integrates these principles into all dimensions of its foreign policy, from development cooperation to security, including trade and neighbourhood policy. The integration of human rights into foreign policy is a fundamental commitment of the Union and a condition for the effectiveness of its external action.

The EEAS has a wide range of instruments for promoting and protecting human rights in its relations with third countries. These instruments include political dialogue, conditionality, technical assistance, cooperation programs, and sanctions. The combination of these instruments enables the EEAS to adapt its response to the different situations and actors, and to promote human rights effectively on the international stage.

Political dialogue is one of the instruments most frequently used by the EEAS to promote human rights. The EEAS maintains bilateral human rights dialogues with a large number of countries, in which issues such as extrajudicial executions, torture, arbitrary detention, freedom of expression, and discrimination are discussed. These dialogues enable the EEAS to express its concerns, to gather information on the human rights situation in partner countries, and to promote concrete reforms. The EEAS also participates in multilateral human rights dialogues in international organizations such as the United Nations Human Rights Council.

Conditionality is another important instrument for the promotion of human rights. Conditionality consists of linking development aid, economic cooperation, and other benefits to respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms. The EEAS uses conditionality in its cooperation and partnership agreements with third countries, establishing clear conditions on human rights that partner countries must comply with to benefit from aid. Conditionality is an effective instrument for promoting human rights reforms, but it also presents challenges, such as the need to balance the promotion of human rights with other foreign policy objectives.

Technical assistance and cooperation programs are complementary instruments for the promotion of human rights. The EEAS provides technical assistance to partner countries to strengthen their human rights capacities, including the reform of judicial systems, the training of public officials, and the strengthening of civil society organizations. The EEAS's cooperation programs also include human rights components that aim to promote the culture of human rights and respect for fundamental freedoms in partner countries. The EEAS has established specific cooperation programs on human rights that encompass areas such as the training of judges and prosecutors, the strengthening of national human rights institutions, the protection of human rights defenders, and the promotion of gender equality. These programs are implemented in close cooperation with international organizations, national governments, and civil society, and are funded by Union instruments such as the NDICI-Global Europe. The EEAS regularly evaluates the impact of these programs and uses the results to improve their effectiveness and to adapt their approaches to the changing needs of partner countries.

### 17.3 International protection mechanisms

The EEAS actively participates in international human rights protection mechanisms, contributing to the strengthening of the international protection system. The United Nations Human Rights Council is the main international forum for the promotion and protection of human rights, and the Union actively participates in its work. The EEAS coordinates the Union's position in the Human Rights Council and promotes the adoption of resolutions on human rights issues of interest to the Union. The EEAS also works to promote the candidacy of European jurists to treaty bodies and to the investigative mechanisms of the Human Rights Council, thereby contributing to the quality and independence of international protection mechanisms. The Union uses its influence in the Human Rights Council to promote the adoption of resolutions on specific human rights situations, such as serious violations in China, Russia, Myanmar, and other countries. The EEAS coordinates the position of the Member States in these negotiations and works to build coalitions with other countries that share the same values and concerns. The Union's participation in the Human Rights Council is particularly important at a time when the international human rights protection system faces significant challenges, including attempts by some states to undermine the independence of protection mechanisms and to limit civil society space.

The EEAS also participates in the Universal Periodic Review mechanisms of the Human Rights Council, which allow for the periodic evaluation of the human rights situation in all Member States of the United Nations. The Union participates in these review processes, presenting reports on the human rights situation in its Member States and making recommendations for the improvement of protection. The EEAS coordinates the preparation of these reports and promotes the implementation of recommendations in the Member States. The EEAS has also established an internal mechanism to follow the implementation of Universal Periodic Review recommendations in the Member States, which includes the collection of information, the analysis of progress made, and the formulation of recommendations for improvement. The EEAS uses the results of this monitoring to inform its position in Human Rights Council negotiations and to promote compliance with the international human rights commitments of the Member States. The Union has also established a structured dialogue with the Member States on the implementation of Universal Periodic Review recommendations, which enables the exchange of experiences and best practices among Member States. The EEAS actively participates in this dialogue and promotes cooperation among the Member States to improve human rights protection within the Union.

The EEAS also works to strengthen regional human rights protection mechanisms, particularly in Africa, Latin America, and Asia. The EEAS supports the creation and strengthening of regional human rights courts, commissions, and other regional protection institutions. The EEAS also promotes cooperation between regional and international human rights protection mechanisms, contributing to the creation of a more coherent and effective global protection system. In Africa, the EEAS actively supports the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights, providing technical and financial assistance to strengthen its judicial capacity and its independence. In Latin America, the EEAS works with the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights to promote human rights protection in the region. In Asia, the EEAS supports efforts to establish regional human rights protection mechanisms, where the absence of a regional human rights court remains a significant gap in the protection system. The EEAS also promotes cooperation between regional human rights protection mechanisms through the United Nations, facilitating the exchange of experiences and best practices among the different regions. This inter-regional cooperation is particularly valuable for addressing common challenges, such as the protection of minority rights, gender equality, and freedom of expression.

The EEAS is a young but vital institution for the foreign policy of the European Union, and its evolution will continue to shape the role of the Union on the international stage. The digital transformation, neighbourhood policy, multilateral cooperation, and the promotion of human rights are essential dimensions of the EEAS's external action that contribute to strengthening the role of the Union as a responsible and reliable international actor. The EEAS will continue to play a central role in promoting the values and interests of the Union in an increasingly complex and challenging international environment.*
