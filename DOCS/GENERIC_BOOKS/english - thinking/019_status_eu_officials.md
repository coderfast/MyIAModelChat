# The Statute of EU Officials

## Chapter 1: Introduction to the Statute of Officials

### 1.1 The Origin and Evolution of the Statute

The Statute of EU Officials is the body of legal rules that regulates the employment relationship between EU officials and the institutions for which they work. The origin of this statute dates back to the early years of the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC), when the first rules on the staff of the Community institutions were established. These initial rules evolved with the creation of the other European Communities and with the expansion of the competences of the institutions.

The ECSC Staff Regulations, adopted in 1953, were the first rules to comprehensively regulate the employment relationship between officials and the Community. These Regulations established the fundamental principles of equal treatment, meritocracy and transparency in access to Community public employment. With the creation of the European Economic Community (EEC) and the European Atomic Energy Community (EAEC) in 1957, similar Staff Regulations were adopted for these new institutions.

The consolidation of the Statute of Officials came with the adoption of the Staff Regulations of the European Communities in 1962, which unified employment rules for all the Communities. These Regulations established a comprehensive system of rights and obligations of officials, which has been amended and updated on numerous occasions to adapt to changes in institutional needs and the evolution of EU Law. The most important reforms included the introduction of the category of temporary agents in 1968, the creation of EPSO in 2003 and the reform of the pension system in 2014.

Today, the Statute of Officials is composed of the Staff Regulations (EC, Euratom) No 258/2012 and the Implementing Rules, which detail the provisions of the main Regulation. This regulatory framework is completed by the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union, which has interpreted and developed the provisions of the Statute in numerous judgments. The Statute of Officials is a dynamic regulatory framework that evolves to adapt to the changing needs of institutions and their officials.

### 1.2 The Scope of Application of the Statute

The Statute of Officials applies to the officials of the institutions of the European Union, which include the European Commission, the European Parliament, the Council of the European Union, the Court of Justice of the European Union, the European Court of Auditors, the European Central Bank, the European Economic and Social Committee, the Committee of the Regions and the European Ombudsman. Each institution has its own human resources service that manages staff in accordance with the provisions of the Statute.

The Statute applies to permanent officials who have passed a selection process organized by EPSO and have been appointed to occupy a post in one of the institutions. It also applies, with the necessary adaptations, to temporary agents and other EU agents. However, the Statute does not apply to local staff recruited by EU representations in third countries, who are subject to the labour laws of the country in which they work.

The scope of application of the Statute includes all phases of the employment relationship, from recruitment to termination of the contract. This includes access to employment, conditions of employment, the rights and obligations of officials, career management, continuing training, performance evaluation, disciplinary sanctions and retirement. The Statute establishes a comprehensive regulatory framework governing all aspects of the employment relationship between officials and institutions.

The application of the Statute is subject to the specific provisions of each institution, which may establish additional rules in certain areas. However, these additional rules must be compatible with the Statute and may not restrict the rights of officials. The Court of Justice of the European Union is the competent body to interpret the Statute and resolve disputes that may arise from its application.

### 1.3 The Fundamental Principles of the Statute

The Statute of Officials is based on a set of fundamental principles that guide the regulation of the employment relationship. These principles include equal treatment, non-discrimination, meritocracy, transparency, proportionality and the general interest. These principles are the foundation upon which the entire regulatory framework of the Statute is built and must be taken into account in the interpretation and application of its provisions.

The principle of equal treatment implies that all officials must receive fair and equitable treatment, regardless of their sex, race, colour, ethnic or social origin, genetic characteristics, language, religion or convictions, political or other opinions, trade union affiliation, properties, birth, disability, age or sexual orientation. This principle applies to all phases of the employment relationship, from recruitment to termination of the contract.

The principle of meritocracy implies that the selection and promotion of officials are based exclusively on their abilities, knowledge and skills to perform the functions of each post. This principle ensures that the best candidates are selected to fill vacant posts and that officials are promoted on the basis of their merit and not on personal or other considerations.

The principle of transparency implies that selection processes, personnel decisions and employment conditions are public and accessible to all interested parties. This principle ensures that officials and candidates can access the information necessary to exercise their rights and to participate in selection processes on an equal footing. Transparency is a fundamental component of trust in institutions and in their ability to manage human resources effectively and fairly.

### 1.4 The Structure of the Statute

The Statute of Officials is organized in different titles that regulate the various aspects of the employment relationship. Title I contains the general provisions, including definitions, scope of application and fundamental principles. Title II regulates access to employment, including selection processes, conditions of access and candidate requirements.

Title III regulates the rights and obligations of officials, including remuneration, social benefits, occupational health and safety rights, holidays and leave. Title IV regulates career development, including promotions, mobility, continuing training and performance evaluation. Title V regulates disciplinary sanctions, including disciplinary procedures, sanctions and defence rights.

Title VI regulates the termination of the employment relationship, including retirement, separation from service and compensation. Title VII contains the final provisions, including transitional provisions, implementing provisions and financial provisions. This organic structure allows a comprehensive and systematic regulation of all aspects of the employment relationship.

The Statute is supplemented by the Implementing Rules, which detail the provisions of the main Regulation in specific areas. These Rules include the Staff Implementing Rules, which set out the rules for applying the Staff Regulations, and the Pension Implementing Rules, which establish the rules for managing the pension system. The Implementing Rules are adopted by the competent institutions and have the same legal force as the main Regulation.

### 1.5 The Relationship between the Statute and National Law

The Statute of Officials is an autonomous regulatory framework governed by European Union Law. This means that the Statute takes precedence over the national law of Member States in the areas it regulates. However, the Statute does not regulate all aspects of the employment relationship, and in areas not regulated by the Statute, national law may apply.

The relationship between the Statute and national law is complex and has been the subject of numerous judgments of the Court of Justice. In general, the Court of Justice has held that the Statute takes precedence over national law in the areas it regulates, but that national law may apply in areas not regulated by the Statute. This interpretation has allowed a harmonious coexistence between the Statute and national law, and has ensured that officials receive adequate protection in all areas of their employment relationship.

The application of national law in areas not regulated by the Statute raises complex issues, as national law varies significantly between Member States. To address these issues, the Court of Justice has established principles for determining which national law is applicable, such as the principle of the place of employment and the principle of the law most favourable to the official. These principles allow for a coherent application of national law in areas not regulated by the Statute.

The relationship between the Statute and national law is an area in constant evolution, as the Court of Justice continues to interpret and develop the principles governing this relationship. The judgments of the Court of Justice are a fundamental source of law in this area, and have a significant impact on the employment conditions of officials. Harmonization between the Statute and national law is a continuous challenge that requires collaboration between European institutions and Member States.

## Chapter 2: Access to European Public Employment

### 2.1 EPSO Selection Processes

The European Personnel Selection Office (EPSO) is the body responsible for organizing selection processes for official posts in the institutions of the European Union. It was created in 2003 with the objective of centralizing and harmonizing selection processes, ensuring equal opportunities and the quality of candidates. EPSO organizes open competitions to fill posts in different categories and areas of specialization.

EPSO selection processes are based on a system of written and oral tests that evaluate the competences of candidates. Written tests include verbal reasoning, numerical reasoning, abstract reasoning, attention to detail and specific post-related competences. Oral tests include structured interviews, group exercises and oral presentations. All candidates who pass the written tests move on to the oral test phase, where their communication skills, teamwork and leadership abilities are assessed.

The selection process begins with the publication of a competition notice on the EPSO portal. The notice includes information about the vacant post, access requirements, selection criteria and the process timeline. Interested candidates submit their applications through the EPSO portal within the established deadline, together with supporting documents for their education and professional experience.

EPSO evaluates applications and selects candidates who meet the requirements to participate in the selection tests. Selected candidates take written tests at examination centres located in different Member States. Candidates who pass the written tests are invited to take oral tests at European institution facilities in Brussels or Luxembourg. The selection process is rigorous and competitive, with thousands of candidates applying for a limited number of posts.

### 2.2 Employment Access Requirements

The requirements for access to European public employment include nationality, education, professional experience and language knowledge requirements. These requirements are set out in the Statute of Officials and in EPSO selection notices, and vary depending on the category and the post.

The nationality requirement implies that candidates must be nationals of a Member State of the European Union. This requirement is based on the principle that European institutions must reflect the diversity of Member States and that EU citizens have the right to access employment in the institutions. The nationality requirement extends to nationals of Member States who have acquired the nationality of another Member State.

The education requirement implies that candidates must have adequate education for the post. Education requirements vary depending on the category of the post. Category A posts require advanced university education, Category B posts require university education, Category C posts require post-secondary education and Category D posts require basic education. Education requirements are specified in EPSO selection notices.

The professional experience requirement implies that candidates must have relevant professional experience for the post. Experience requirements vary depending on the category of the post and the competences required. Senior posts require significant experience, while junior posts may not require prior experience. The experience requirement is assessed on the basis of the competences and skills acquired through professional experience.

The language knowledge requirement implies that candidates must demonstrate an advanced level of knowledge of at least two official EU languages, one of which must be English, French or German. This requirement ensures that officials can communicate effectively with their colleagues from other Member States and perform their functions in a multilingual environment. The level of language knowledge is assessed through specific tests measuring listening comprehension, reading comprehension, spoken expression and written expression skills.

### 2.3 Selection and Assessment Criteria

Selection criteria are the parameters used to evaluate candidates and determine their suitability for the post. These criteria are specified in EPSO selection notices and are used at all stages of the selection process. Selection criteria include generic competences, such as communication, teamwork and leadership competences, and specific competences, such as technical or legal competences.

The assessment of candidates is carried out through different tools, such as written tests, structured interviews, group exercises and oral presentations. These tools allow the competences of candidates to be assessed objectively and comparatively. Written tests assess candidates' cognitive and technical competences, while oral tests assess their communication, teamwork and leadership skills.

The assessment process is based on meritocracy and equal treatment. All candidates are assessed on the basis of the same criteria and using the same assessment tools. Assessment results are formally recorded and used to determine each candidate's score. Candidates with the highest scores are selected to fill the vacant posts.

The assessment of candidates is a rigorous and objective process that ensures that the best candidates are selected to fill the vacant posts. The assessment process is subject to judicial review, which means that candidates can challenge selection decisions before the Court of Justice of the European Union if they consider that violations of the Statute or the fundamental principles of equal treatment and meritocracy have occurred.

### 2.4 Contract Selection Processes

Contract selection processes are those in which an EU institution requests EPSO to organize a specific selection process to fill vacant posts in that institution. These processes are used when an institution's staffing needs cannot be met through general open competitions, or when specific competences are required that are not available in the candidate lists of open competitions.

In a contract process, the institution defines the post requirements and selection criteria, and EPSO organizes the selection process in accordance with these requirements. Contract processes may include written tests, oral tests and interviews, depending on the complexity of the post and the competences required. Candidates who pass the selection process are included in a reserve list of suitable candidates, from which the institution can select the officials who best fit its needs.

Contract processes are an important tool for human resource management in EU institutions, as they allow staffing needs to be met quickly and efficiently. However, these processes must respect the principles of equal opportunity and meritocracy, and must be transparent and objective. EPSO ensures that contract processes are carried out in accordance with these principles and provides advice to institutions in defining requirements and selection criteria.

The use of contract processes has increased in recent years, as European institutions face new staffing needs due to the expansion of EU competences and the evolution of political and technical challenges. These processes allow institutions to adapt quickly to these changes and ensure that they have the necessary staff to perform their functions effectively. Contract processes are a flexible tool that complements EPSO's open selection processes.

### 2.5 Appointment and Taking Up of Duties

Appointment is the formal act by which an EU institution appoints a selected candidate to occupy a post as an official. Appointment is made by the competent authority of the institution, normally the President or the Secretary General, and is published in the Official Journal of the European Union. Appointment is the final act of the selection process and marks the beginning of the employment relationship between the official and the institution.

Appointment is subject to the condition that the candidate meets all the requirements set out in the Statute and in the selection notice. If the candidate does not meet any of the requirements, the institution may refuse to make the appointment. The candidate may also refuse to accept the appointment if the employment conditions are not as expected. In both cases, the selection process is void and the institution must seek another candidate to fill the vacant post.

Taking up of duties is the act by which the official officially begins to perform their functions in the institution. Taking up of duties occurs after appointment and is subject to the provision of a security deposit and the signing of a taking-up-of-duties record. The security deposit is a financial guarantee covering any damage that the official may cause to the institution in the performance of their functions. The taking-up-of-duties record is an official document certifying that the official has taken up their post and has assumed their obligations.

After taking up duties, the official begins their probationary period, which lasts nine months. During the probationary period, the official is subject to a specific evaluation regime that allows the institution to assess their suitability for the post. If the evaluation is positive, the official is confirmed in their post and acquires the status of permanent official. If the evaluation is negative, the official's contract may be terminated without the need to justify the decision.

### 2.6 Certificate of Service and Personnel Records

The certificate of service is the official document certifying that an official has been appointed and has taken up their post. The certificate of service is issued by the competent authority of the institution and is incorporated into the official's personal file. The certificate of service contains information about the official's appointment, date of taking up duties, category and grade, and the post occupied.

The personnel registry is the body responsible for managing the official documentation of institution officials. The personnel registry is responsible for issuing certificates of service, managing changes of post and category, recording performance evaluations and managing retirement documentation. The personnel registry is also responsible for ensuring the accuracy and confidentiality of information contained in officials' personal files.

The personnel registry works closely with the human resources services and the competent authorities of the institution to ensure that officials' official documentation is properly managed. The personnel registry is also responsible for providing officials with information about their rights and obligations, and advising them on matters related to their employment.

The management of official documentation is a fundamental aspect of human resource administration, as it ensures transparency and information security. The personnel registry must ensure that official documentation is properly archived and protected against unauthorized access. The personnel registry must also ensure that officials can access their official documentation and request copies of it.

## Chapter 3: Employment Conditions

### 3.1 Remuneration of Officials

The remuneration of EU officials comprises a basic salary and various supplements determined on the basis of the official's category and grade, place of work and other specific circumstances. The basic salary is set annually by the competent authorities and is updated to maintain its purchasing power in line with the evolution of consumer prices in the countries where officials work.

The remuneration system for European officials is based on the principle that remuneration must be sufficient to guarantee their independence and their ability to perform their functions impartially. This principle translates into remuneration that is competitive with that of other qualified employment sectors, which allows institutions to attract and retain the best professionals. The remuneration of European officials is not subject to national taxes, but to a European tax that is paid to the EU itself.

The basic salary of officials is determined on the basis of the official's category and grade. Category A includes senior posts requiring advanced university education and significant professional experience. Category B includes intermediate posts requiring university education and relevant professional experience. Category C includes executive posts requiring post-secondary education or relevant professional experience. Category D includes auxiliary staff posts requiring basic education and experience in service tasks.

In addition to the basic salary, officials receive various supplements that are added to their total remuneration. These supplements include family allowances, which are allowances for dependent children; the residence allowance, which is a supplement for officials working outside their country of origin; and other specific service supplements. The total remuneration of officials is competitive and is designed to guarantee their independence and their ability to perform their functions impartially.

### 3.2 Social Benefits

The social benefits of EU officials include various benefits that supplement their remuneration and provide them with comprehensive protection. These benefits include medical care, retirement benefits, unemployment benefits and temporary incapacity benefits. The social benefits system is designed to ensure that officials and their families have adequate protection at all stages of their lives.

Medical care is a benefit covering illness and accidents for both the official and their dependants. Officials are covered by a health insurance that covers medical expenses arising from illness or accident. The health insurance also covers the medical expenses of the official's dependants, including children, spouse and other dependent relatives. Medical care is a fundamental benefit that ensures the health and well-being of officials and their families.

Retirement benefits provide a pension to officials who retire after meeting certain age and length of service requirements. The pension system for European officials is a pay-as-you-go system, in which the contributions of active officials fund the pensions of retired officials. The amount of the pension is determined on the basis of the official's remuneration and years of service.

Unemployment benefits provide compensation to officials who lose their employment involuntarily. This benefit is a safety net for officials who find themselves unemployed and need financial support while seeking new employment. Unemployment benefits are of limited duration and are subject to the official's active search for employment.

### 3.3 Holidays and Leave

The annual holidays of EU officials are a minimum of 24 days per year, which may be increased on the basis of the official's seniority. Annual holidays are a fundamental right that allows officials to rest and recover from the demands of work. The holiday period is determined by mutual agreement between the official and their hierarchy, taking into account the needs of the service.

In addition to annual holidays, officials are entitled to leave for various reasons, such as sick leave, marriage leave, parental leave and bereavement leave. These types of leave are regulated in the Statute of Officials and in the Implementing Rules, and their duration varies depending on the type of leave. Leave is a fundamental right that allows officials to attend to their personal and family needs.

Officials are also entitled to leave for professional reasons, such as leave to attend training courses, leave to participate in conventions and conferences, and leave to carry out research activities. These types of leave are an investment in the professional development of officials and contribute to improving the quality of the European public service. The management of holidays and leave is a shared responsibility between the official and the institution.

The institution is obliged to ensure that officials can enjoy their holidays and leave under the conditions established in the Statute. The official is obliged to request their holidays and leave with sufficient advance notice and to respect the rights of their colleagues in the management of rest periods. The balanced management of holidays and leave is fundamental to ensuring the well-being of officials and the efficiency of the public service.

### 3.4 Occupational Health and Safety

Occupational health and safety is a priority for the institutions of the European Union. These institutions are obliged to ensure the health and safety of their officials at work, and have implemented a comprehensive occupational risk prevention system. This system includes risk assessment, prevention planning, health and safety training, the provision of personal protective equipment and the implementation of emergency protocols.

Risk assessment is an ongoing process that allows identifying hazards present in the work environment and assessing the risks associated with those hazards. The results of the risk assessment are used to establish prevention measures that eliminate or reduce risks to an acceptable level. Officials actively participate in the risk assessment process, as they are the ones who best know the working conditions in which they perform their functions.

Health and safety training is a fundamental element of the occupational risk prevention system. Officials receive training on the risks associated with their post, applicable prevention measures and emergency procedures. This training is provided regularly and is adapted to the specific needs of each post. Officials are obliged to participate in the training and to apply the prevention measures in their daily work.

European institutions have occupational health services that provide medical and psychological assistance to officials. These services carry out periodic medical examinations, assess health and safety conditions at work and advise institutions on the implementation of prevention measures. Officials are entitled to receive medical assistance in the event of a workplace accident or occupational disease, and institutions are obliged to cover the costs associated with this assistance. Occupational health and safety is a fundamental right of officials and a responsibility of institutions.

### 3.5 Privileges and Immunities

The privileges and immunities of EU officials are legal guarantees that allow them to perform their functions independently and impartially. These privileges and immunities are set out in the Treaties and in the Protocol on the Privileges and Immunities of the European Union, and include jurisdictional immunity, tax exemption and the protection of official documents.

Jurisdictional immunity protects officials from legal action in relation to acts performed in the exercise of their official functions. This immunity is not a personal privilege, but a guarantee of the independent functioning of institutions. Jurisdictional immunity may be lifted by the competent authorities in certain circumstances, such as when the official has committed a crime outside the exercise of their functions.

Tax exemption protects officials from taxation by Member States on salaries and benefits received from European institutions. Officials are subject to a European tax paid to the EU itself, which exempts them from taxation by Member States. This tax exemption is a guarantee of the independence of officials and their ability to perform their functions impartially.

The protection of official documents ensures that documents and information to which officials have access in the performance of their functions are protected against unauthorized disclosure. This protection is fundamental to ensuring the confidentiality of information and the efficiency of institutions. Officials are obliged to maintain the confidentiality of information to which they have access and not to disclose it to third parties without authorization.

## Chapter 4: Career Development

### 4.1 Promotions by Seniority

Promotions by seniority are advancements in category or grade that officials may obtain on the basis of their performance and competences. These promotions are based on a performance evaluation system that identifies officials with the best performance who meet the education and experience requirements for access to higher-level posts.

The promotion process by seniority begins with the evaluation of the official's performance during a given period. Officials who receive a positive evaluation and meet the education and experience requirements may be promoted to a higher grade within their category. This promotion involves an increase in responsibilities and remuneration. The promotion process is transparent and based on objective criteria that allow comparison of different candidates.

Promotion criteria include the official's performance in their current post, their education and experience, and their competences for assuming greater responsibilities. Performance evaluation is carried out periodically, normally once a year, and is based on predefined objectives established by mutual agreement between the official and their supervisor. The results of the evaluation are used as the basis for promotion decisions.

Promotions by seniority are a fundamental element of the professional development of officials, as they allow them to advance in their career and assume new responsibilities. The promotion system by seniority is designed to reward merit and experience, and to ensure that officials with the best performance are promoted to higher-level posts. Promotions by seniority contribute to the motivation and commitment of officials, and improve the efficiency of the European public service.

### 4.2 Internal Mobility

Internal mobility is the mechanism that allows officials to transfer to other posts within the same institution or to other institutions of the European Union. Internal mobility is an important element of professional development, as it allows officials to acquire new experiences and knowledge and to broaden their career opportunities.

Internal mobility may be voluntary, when the official requests the transfer, or mandatory, when the institution decides to transfer the official for service reasons. In both cases, internal mobility is governed by the principles of transparency, equal opportunity and meritocracy. Vacant posts are published on internal employment portals and interested officials may submit their applications.

Internal mobility has important benefits for both officials and institutions. For officials, it allows the acquisition of new experiences and knowledge and the development of new competences. For institutions, it allows the transfer of knowledge and experience between different areas and departments, and promotes cohesion and collaboration between the different services. Internal mobility also contributes to the motivation and commitment of officials, as it offers them new opportunities for professional development.

The conditions of internal mobility vary depending on the type of mobility and the provisions of the Statute. Officials who are transferred for service reasons retain their remuneration and employment conditions, and may receive supplements for the costs associated with the transfer. Officials who voluntarily request a transfer must negotiate their new employment conditions with the receiving institution.

### 4.3 Continuing Training

Continuing training is a fundamental element for the professional development of EU officials. European institutions offer a wide range of training programmes covering different areas of knowledge and competences. These programmes include technical training courses, language courses, leadership and management courses, and courses on EU policies and regulations.

Training programmes are organized by the human resources services of institutions, in collaboration with specialized training centres. These programmes are delivered in different formats, such as face-to-face courses, online courses, seminars, conferences and exchange programmes. Officials may participate in these programmes on a voluntary or compulsory basis, depending on the needs of the institution and the requirements of the post.

Continuing training has important benefits for both officials and institutions. For officials, it improves their competences and knowledge, which increases their career opportunities. For institutions, it ensures qualified and up-to-date staff, which improves the efficiency and quality of the public service. Investment in continuing training is an investment in the human capital of institutions, which generates long-term benefits.

European institutions also offer professional development programmes that go beyond technical training. These programmes include mentoring, coaching, job rotation and special projects. These programmes allow officials to develop leadership, management and communication skills, and prepare them to assume greater responsibilities in the future. Professional development is an integral part of human resource management in European institutions.

### 4.4 Performance Evaluation

Performance evaluation is a fundamental process for human resource management in EU institutions. This process allows assessing the performance of officials and identifying their strengths and areas for improvement. The evaluation is carried out periodically, normally once a year, and is based on objective criteria that allow measuring the official's performance in relation to the expectations of the post.

The evaluation process begins with the definition of performance objectives for the evaluation period. These objectives are established by mutual agreement between the official and their supervisor, and must be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound (SMART criteria). Once the objectives have been established, the official works to achieve them during the evaluation period, receiving continuous feedback from their supervisor and colleagues.

At the end of the evaluation period, an evaluation meeting is held in which the supervisor and the official review the official's performance in relation to the established objectives. At this meeting, the official's strengths and areas for improvement are identified, and action plans are established for the next period. The evaluation is formally documented and incorporated into the official's personal file.

The results of the performance evaluation have important consequences for the official's career. Officials with positive evaluations have a greater chance of being promoted and accessing posts of greater responsibility. Officials with negative evaluations may receive additional training to improve their performance, or may be subject to disciplinary measures if their performance does not improve. Performance evaluation is therefore a key tool for ensuring the quality and efficiency of the European public service.

### 4.5 Internal Competitions and Staff Notices

Internal competitions are selection processes organized within an institution to fill mid-level or senior vacant posts. Unlike EPSO's open selection processes, internal competitions are open exclusively to serving officials of European institutions. These competitions allow officials to advance in their career and assume new responsibilities within their institution.

Staff notices are selection processes organized to fill vacant posts in specific categories. These notices may be open to all external candidates or restricted to serving officials. Staff notices are published on the employment portals of institutions and include information about the vacant posts, access requirements and selection criteria.

Internal competitions and staff notices are important tools for human resource management, as they allow staffing needs to be met transparently and on a meritocratic basis. These processes ensure that the best candidates are selected to fill the vacant posts, regardless of whether they are internal or external candidates. The combination of internal competitions and staff notices gives institutions the flexibility needed to adapt to their changing staffing needs.

Internal selection processes and staff notices are subject to the same fundamental principles of equal treatment, meritocracy and transparency as EPSO's open selection processes. Candidates are assessed on the basis of their competences and experience, and selection is based on objective and transparent criteria. Internal selection processes and staff notices contribute to the efficiency and quality of the European public service, as they ensure that the best candidates are selected to fill the vacant posts.

## Chapter 5: Obligations of Officials

### 5.1 The Duty of Loyalty

The duty of loyalty is a fundamental obligation of EU officials. This duty implies that officials must act at all times in the interest of the institution and the European Union, and must avoid any conduct that may harm the interests of the institution. The duty of loyalty includes the obligation to maintain the confidentiality of information to which they have access, the obligation not to accept gifts or benefits that may influence their impartiality, and the obligation to report any external activity that may give rise to a conflict of interest.

The duty of loyalty extends to all activities of the official, both inside and outside the workplace. Officials must act loyally and honestly in all their actions, and must avoid any conduct that may compromise the image or interests of the institution. This duty is especially important for officials who have access to confidential information or who participate in decision-making.

Breach of the duty of loyalty may lead to disciplinary sanctions, which may include a reprimand, suspension of employment and salary or separation from service. European institutions are obliged to ensure that all officials comply with this duty and to take the necessary measures in the event of non-compliance. The duty of loyalty is fundamental to ensuring the integrity and efficiency of European institutions.

Officials also have the obligation to be honest and transparent in their actions, and to treat their colleagues and the public with respect and consideration. Professionalism is a fundamental value that guides the behaviour of all EU officials. The duty of loyalty and professionalism are essential values that must be actively promoted in all European institutions.

### 5.2 The Duty of Impartiality

The duty of impartiality is another fundamental obligation of EU officials. This duty implies that officials must act objectively and impartially in the performance of their functions, without allowing themselves to be influenced by personal or other considerations. The duty of impartiality is especially important for officials who participate in decision-making, as they must ensure that their decisions are based exclusively on objective criteria and the general interest.

The duty of impartiality applies to all activities of the official, including participation in selection processes, evaluation of candidates, awarding of contracts and administrative decision-making. Officials must refrain from participating in activities in which they have a personal interest that may compromise their impartiality. This duty is fundamental to ensuring fairness and equity in the decisions of European institutions.

Breach of the duty of impartiality may lead to disciplinary sanctions, which may include a reprimand, suspension of employment and salary or separation from service. European institutions are obliged to ensure that all officials comply with this duty and to take the necessary measures in the event of non-compliance. The duty of impartiality is fundamental to ensuring the integrity and efficiency of European institutions.

Officials also have the obligation to act with objectivity and equity in all their actions, and to respect the rights of EU citizens. The duty of impartiality is a fundamental principle that must be taken into account in all decisions and actions of officials. Impartiality is an essential value that contributes to citizens' trust in European institutions.

### 5.3 The Duty of Reserve

The duty of reserve is an obligation of EU officials that requires them to maintain the confidentiality of information to which they have access in the performance of their functions. This duty is fundamental to ensuring information security and the efficiency of institutions. Officials must not disclose confidential information to third parties without authorization, and must take the necessary measures to protect information against unauthorized access.

The duty of reserve applies to all information to which officials have access in the performance of their functions, including documents, data, communications and other forms of information. Officials are obliged to treat this information confidentially and not to use it for purposes other than those of their post. This duty is especially important for officials who have access to sensitive or classified information.

Breach of the duty of reserve may lead to serious disciplinary sanctions, which may include separation from service. European institutions are obliged to ensure that all officials comply with this duty and to take the necessary measures in the event of non-compliance. The duty of reserve is fundamental to ensuring information security and the efficiency of European institutions.

Officials also have the obligation to protect information against unauthorized access and to take the necessary measures to ensure its security. This includes the use of secure passwords, the protection of confidential documents and the immediate notification of any security breach. The duty of reserve is a fundamental principle that must be taken into account in all actions of officials.

### 5.4 The Duty to Follow Instructions

The duty to follow instructions from hierarchical superiors is a fundamental obligation of EU officials. This duty implies that officials must obey the instructions they receive from their superiors in the performance of their functions, provided that these instructions are lawful and within the scope of their competences. The duty to follow instructions is fundamental to ensuring hierarchy and efficiency in the organization of institutions.

However, the duty to follow instructions is not absolute. Officials may refuse to follow instructions that are manifestly unlawful or that infringe fundamental rights. In these cases, the official must inform their superiors and, if necessary, the competent authorities. ## Chapter 11: The Disciplinary System

### 11.1 Principles of the Disciplinary System

The disciplinary system of EU institutions is the body of rules and procedures that regulate offences committed by officials in the exercise of their functions and establish the corresponding sanctions. The disciplinary system is based on fundamental principles such as the presumption of innocence, the right of defence, the proportionality of sanctions, the non-retroactivity of sanctioning rules and the two-tier system. These principles ensure that disciplinary proceedings are conducted in a fair, transparent and rights-respecting manner.

The principle of the presumption of innocence implies that every official accused of a disciplinary offence is considered innocent until proven otherwise through regular disciplinary proceedings. This principle protects officials against unfounded accusations and ensures that sanctions are only imposed when there is sufficient evidence of the commission of an offence. The right of defence ensures that accused officials have the opportunity to present their arguments, provide evidence and be assisted by a representative during the disciplinary proceedings.

The proportionality of sanctions implies that the sanctions imposed must be proportionate to the gravity of the offence committed. Sanctions range from verbal reprimands to dismissal, including written reprimands, suspensions of employment and salary and demotions. The selection of the appropriate sanction depends on factors such as the gravity of the offence, mitigating or aggravating circumstances, the official's disciplinary record and the impact of the offence on the public service.

### 11.2 Disciplinary Procedure

The disciplinary procedure is initiated when a competent authority becomes aware of a possible disciplinary offence by an official. The competent authority to initiate a disciplinary procedure varies depending on the institution and the gravity of the offence, but generally includes the director of human resources, the secretary general of the institution or the president of the institution. Once the procedure has been initiated, an investigating officer is appointed who is responsible for investigating the facts and preparing a report with their conclusions.

The investigating officer of the disciplinary procedure has the obligation to carry out an impartial and complete investigation of the facts, collecting all relevant evidence and hearing all parties involved. The accused official has the right to be informed of the charges against them, to access the evidence against them, to present their defence arguments and evidence and to be assisted by a representative throughout the procedure. The investigating officer must ensure that the procedure is conducted with all the procedural guarantees established in the Statute of Officials.

Once the investigation has been completed, the investigating officer prepares a report that is submitted to the competent authority for decision-making. The competent authority examines the report and decides whether to impose a disciplinary sanction and, if so, which is the most appropriate sanction in the circumstances of the case. The disciplinary decision must be reasoned and notified to the official, who has the right to challenge it before the Court of Justice of the European Union.

### 11.3 Disciplinary Sanctions

The disciplinary sanctions that may be imposed on officials vary depending on the gravity of the offence and the circumstances of the case. The mildest sanctions include a verbal reprimand and a written reprimand, which are applied for minor offences that do not justify more severe sanctions. These sanctions have a deterrent and educational effect, and are recorded in the official's file for a given period.

Intermediate sanctions include suspension of employment and salary, which may last from a few days to several months, and demotion, which involves the reduction of the official's hierarchical level. These sanctions are applied for more serious offences that justify a restriction of the official's rights. Suspension of employment and salary is a particularly effective sanction for offences involving gross negligence or repeated failure to comply with professional obligations.

The most severe sanction is dismissal, which involves the termination of the employment relationship between the official and the institution. Dismissal is only imposed in the most serious cases, such as the commission of crimes, serious violation of professional obligations, irreparable loss of confidence or disqualification from the exercise of public functions. Dismissal is a proportionate sanction only for offences of extreme gravity that make the official's continued presence in the institution incompatible.

## Chapter 12: Rights of Officials

### 12.1 Individual Rights

The individual rights of EU officials are a set of guarantees and powers that protect the interests of officials and allow them to perform their functions with independence and dignity. These rights include the right to fair remuneration, the right to adequate working conditions, the right to continuing training, the right to personal data protection, the right to equal treatment and the right to freedom of expression. These rights are enshrined in the Statute of Officials and are directly applicable in all EU institutions.

The right to fair remuneration implies that officials have the right to receive adequate compensation for their work, which includes a basic salary, social benefits and other allowances. The remuneration of officials is determined on the basis of their category, level of experience and the responsibilities of their post. The Statute of Officials establishes the rules for calculating remuneration and ensures that officials receive fair treatment in terms of financial compensation.

The right to adequate working conditions implies that institutions must ensure that officials work in a safe, healthy and productivity-enhancing environment. This includes the provision of adequate facilities, appropriate working tools, reasonable working hours and health and safety measures. Institutions must also ensure that officials have access to support services, such as psychological assistance and conflict mediation.

### 12.2 Collective Rights

The collective rights of officials are the rights exercised collectively through their representatives, including trade unions and works councils. These rights include the right to join trade unions, the right to collective bargaining, the right to strike (with limitations) and the right to participate in the management of the institution. Collective rights are fundamental to ensuring that officials have a voice in decisions affecting their employment conditions and working environment.

The right to join trade unions implies that officials have the right to join trade unions and participate in union activities. The trade unions of European institutions represent the interests of officials before the institution's authorities and participate in the negotiation of agreements on employment conditions, remuneration and other labour issues. Trade unions also provide advice and legal assistance to officials in the event of a dispute with the institution.

The right to collective bargaining implies that trade unions may negotiate agreements with the institution that regulate the employment conditions of officials. These agreements may cover topics such as remuneration, working hours, holidays, continuing training and other employment conditions. Collective bargaining is an important mechanism for ensuring that the employment conditions of officials are fair and equitable.

### 12.3 Protection against Reprisals

Protection against reprisals is a fundamental right of officials that ensures that they can perform their functions, express their opinions and report irregularities without fear of negative consequences. The Statute of Officials expressly prohibits any form of reprisal against officials who exercise their rights or report irregularities in institutions. This protection is especially important for ensuring the integrity and transparency of institutions.

Officials who are victims of reprisals may file a complaint with the institution's authorities or with the Court of Justice of the European Union. The institution's authorities have the obligation to investigate reprisal complaints and to take the necessary measures to protect the affected officials. The Court of Justice has established in its case law that protection against reprisals is a fundamental right that must be guaranteed effectively.

Protection against reprisals includes both protection against direct reprisals (such as dismissal, demotion or unjustified sanction) and against indirect reprisals (such as the assignment of less qualified tasks, professional isolation or refusal of promotion). Officials who are victims of reprisals have the right to compensation for the damage suffered and reinstatement in their previous post if they have been affected by a reprisal.

## Chapter 13: Mobility of Officials

### 13.1 Principles of Mobility

Mobility is a fundamental principle of the European civil service that ensures that officials have the opportunity to work in different institutions, in different functional areas and in different countries. Mobility aims to promote organizational flexibility, knowledge transfer, the professional development of officials and institutional cohesion. Mobility is manifested in different forms, including inter-institutional transfers, temporary assignments, job rotation and exchange programmes.

The principle of mobility applies to all officials of EU institutions, regardless of their category or functional area. Officials have the right to apply for vacant posts in other institutions through the internal competition system, and institutions have the obligation to facilitate the mobility of their officials. Mobility is actively promoted by institutions through specific policies, such as giving priority to internal candidates in internal competitions and providing logistical support for officials transferring to another institution.

The benefits of mobility include enriching the professional experience of officials, acquiring new competences, creating a network of professional contacts and promoting a common organizational culture across all institutions. Mobility also contributes to the efficiency of institutions by allowing the reassignment of human resources in line with changing public service needs.

### 13.2 Types of Mobility

Inter-institutional mobility is the most common form of mobility and consists of the temporary or permanent transfer of an official from one institution to another. Inter-institutional mobility is carried out through the internal competition system, which is regularly published on the EU employment portal. Officials interested in inter-institutional mobility must meet the requirements of the vacant post and pass the corresponding selection process.

Intra-institutional mobility is mobility that takes place within the same institution, between different directorates-general, services or units. Intra-institutional mobility allows officials to acquire experience in different functional areas without needing to change institution. European institutions actively promote intra-institutional mobility through rotation programmes and internal competitions that facilitate transfers between departments.

International mobility is a form of mobility that involves the temporary transfer of an official to an EU delegation abroad or to an international organization. International mobility is especially valuable for the professional development of officials, as it allows them to work in different cultural environments and acquire experience in the implementation of EU policies at international level. European institutions offer international mobility programmes that facilitate temporary transfers to posts abroad.

### 13.3 Conditions of Mobility

The conditions of official mobility are regulated by the Statute of Officials and the Implementing Rules, which establish the rights and obligations of officials in the event of mobility. Officials participating in a mobility programme have the right to retain their remuneration and social benefits during the mobility period, as well as to receive assistance for relocation and settling in in the new location.

The duration of mobility varies depending on the type of mobility and the needs of institutions. Temporary mobility usually lasts between six months and four years, while permanent mobility involves the permanent transfer of the official to the new institution. Officials participating in temporary mobility have the right to return to their original post at the end of the mobility period, subject to the conditions established in the Statute of Officials.

European institutions provide logistical support to officials participating in mobility programmes, including assistance with the removal of furniture and personal effects, assistance in finding housing, support for the relocation of family members and language courses for officials transferring to countries with a different language. This logistical support aims to facilitate the transition of officials to their new location and reduce the inconvenience associated with relocation.

## Chapter 14: Social Protection of Officials

### 14.1 Health Insurance

Health insurance is a fundamental benefit of the social protection system of EU officials, ensuring that officials and their families have access to quality health services. Health insurance covers a wide range of services, including medical, hospital, pharmaceutical, dental and ophthalmic care. Officials are covered by health insurance jointly financed by the institution and the official, which ensures the sustainability of the system and the co-responsibility of beneficiaries.

Health insurance benefits include coverage of outpatient medical expenses, hospitalization, prescribed medication, clinical tests, diagnostic imaging and other necessary medical services. Health insurance also covers maternity and child care, medical rehabilitation and psychological care. Officials may choose between different insurance schemes, which vary depending on their place of residence and specific needs.

EU officials' health insurance is complemented by other insurance, such as life insurance and accident insurance, which provide additional protection in the event of death or incapacity. These supplementary insurance provide compensation to the official's beneficiaries in the event of a claim, which ensures the financial protection of the official's family.

### 14.2 Maternity and Paternity Benefits

Maternity and paternity benefits are benefits intended to facilitate work-life balance for officials. Mothers are entitled to a maternity leave period of four months with full remuneration, which may be enjoyed in full or in part before and after the birth of the child. Fathers are entitled to a paternity leave period of ten days with full remuneration, which may be enjoyed in a continuous or fragmented period.

Maternity and paternity benefits also include the possibility of requesting additional leave periods without remuneration, which may be enjoyed until the child reaches a certain age. European institutions have adopted work-life balance policies that facilitate the combination of work and family life, including flexible working hours, teleworking and family support services. These policies aim to promote gender equality and reduce the gender gap in European public employment.

Maternity and paternity benefits are compatible with other social benefits, such as family benefits and childcare subsidies. European institutions offer financial assistance for childcare costs, which aim to reduce the financial burden of officials with young children and facilitate their return to work after the leave period.

### 14.3 Retirement Benefits

Retirement benefits are a fundamental part of the social protection system of EU officials, ensuring financial protection for officials and their families during retirement. The EU pension scheme is a defined-contribution system, in which both the institution and the official make contributions to finance retirement benefits. Retirement benefits include the retirement pension, the survivor's pension and the orphan's pension.

The retirement pension is calculated on the basis of years of service and the official's average remuneration during the last years of their career. The pension percentage varies depending on years of service, with a maximum percentage of 70% of average remuneration for officials who have completed thirty-five years of service. Pensions are updated annually to compensate for inflation and maintain the purchasing power of retirees.

The survivor's pension is a benefit granted to the surviving spouse of a deceased official, and is calculated on the basis of the pension that the official would have received if they had been retired at the time of their death. The orphan's pension is a benefit granted to the dependent children of a deceased official, and is calculated on the basis of the official's pension and the number of children. These benefits are fundamental to ensuring the financial protection of officials' families in bereavement situations.

## Chapter 15: Training of Officials

### 15.1 Initial Training

Initial training is a fundamental component of the process of integrating new officials into EU institutions. Initial training aims to provide new officials with the knowledge and skills necessary to perform their functions effectively, as well as to familiarize them with the institutional environment, the rules and procedures of the institution, and the values and principles that guide work in European institutions.

Initial training generally includes a combination of theoretical and practical training. Theoretical training covers areas such as the structure of the European Union, EU law, the institution's policies, codes of conduct and administrative procedures. Practical training includes familiarization with working tools, information systems and operational processes of the institution. New officials also receive language training if necessary for the performance of their functions.

The duration of initial training varies depending on the category of the official and the functional area, but generally lasts between two weeks and three months. New officials are assigned a tutor or mentor who provides support and guidance during the training period. The tutor or mentor is an experienced official who knows the institution well and can help the new official to adapt quickly to their new working environment.

### 15.2 Continuing Training

Continuing training is a right and an obligation of EU officials, who must maintain and update their knowledge and skills throughout their career. European institutions offer a wide range of continuing training opportunities, including training courses, seminars, workshops, mentoring programmes and internal mobility opportunities. Continuing training is financed by institutions and is free of charge for officials.

Training programmes cover different areas, including technical training, linguistic training, leadership training, cross-cutting skills training and training in specific functional areas. Technical training aims to improve officials' technical knowledge and skills in areas such as information technology, law, economics and project management. Linguistic training aims to improve officials' language competences in the official EU languages.

Leadership training is intended for officials holding or aspiring to hold positions of responsibility, and aims to develop their leadership, team management and decision-making capabilities. Cross-cutting skills training includes courses on effective communication, conflict resolution, time management and teamwork. Continuing training programmes are fundamental to ensuring that officials have the competences necessary to face the changing challenges of the European public service.

### 15.3 Training Evaluation

Training evaluation is a process that aims to measure the effectiveness of training programmes and their impact on officials' performance. Training evaluation is carried out at different levels, including participant satisfaction evaluation, learning assessment, application of knowledge evaluation and impact on institutional results evaluation.

Participant satisfaction evaluation is generally carried out at the end of each training programme, and consists of questionnaires that assess the quality of training, the usefulness of the content and the competence of the trainers. Learning assessment is carried out through tests or exercises that measure the knowledge and skills acquired by participants. Application evaluation is carried out several months after training, to verify whether participants have applied the knowledge acquired in their post.

Impact evaluation on institutional results is the most advanced level of evaluation, and aims to measure whether training has contributed to improving the effectiveness of institutional activities. This evaluation is complex and requires the collection of data on institutional performance indicators before and after training. Training evaluation is a continuous process that allows institutions to improve the quality of their training programmes and ensure that training investment produces tangible results.

## Chapter 16: Performance Evaluation

### 16.1 Objectives of Performance Evaluation

Performance evaluation is a process that aims to measure and improve the performance of officials in their posts. Performance evaluation provides information on officials' strengths and weaknesses, identifies areas for improvement and establishes professional development objectives. Performance evaluation also serves as a basis for decision-making on promotion, training, remuneration and other labour issues.

The objectives of performance evaluation include improving the effectiveness of institutional activities, motivating officials, identifying training needs and planning career development. Performance evaluation is a management tool that allows institutions to align officials' individual objectives with the institution's strategic objectives. Performance evaluation also contributes to the creation of an organizational culture that values excellence, responsibility and continuous improvement.

Performance evaluation is based on principles of objectivity, transparency, equity and development orientation. These principles ensure that the evaluation is conducted fairly and that the results are used for officials' development and not merely as a control instrument. Performance evaluation must be carried out by trained evaluators who know the official's work well and apply standardized evaluation criteria.

### 16.2 Performance Evaluation Process

The performance evaluation process begins with the definition of objectives at the start of each evaluation period, which generally coincides with the calendar year or the institution's fiscal year. Objectives are established by mutual agreement between the official and their supervisor, and must be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound (SMART criteria). Objectives include both performance objectives (results that the official must achieve) and behavioural objectives (competences that the official must demonstrate).

During the evaluation period, the supervisor provides the official with continuous feedback on their performance, identifying strengths and areas for improvement. This continuous feedback is fundamental for the official to be able to adjust their behaviour and improve their performance during the evaluation period. The supervisor must also record observations on the official's performance, which will be used as the basis for the final evaluation.

At the end of the evaluation period, an evaluation interview is held in which the supervisor and the official jointly review the achievement of the established objectives, the official's strengths and weaknesses and areas for improvement. The supervisor prepares an evaluation report reflecting the conclusions of the interview, which is submitted to human resources for recording. The official has the right to express their observations on the evaluation and to request a review if they consider that the evaluation is unfair.

### 16.3 Consequences of Performance Evaluation

The results of performance evaluation have significant consequences for the official's career. Officials with outstanding performance are eligible for promotion, assignment of additional responsibilities and participation in leadership development programmes. Officials with unsatisfactory performance are identified as candidates for improvement plans, which include specific improvement objectives, defined timelines and additional support.

Performance evaluation also influences officials' remuneration, as variable compensation may be linked to performance. Officials with better performance may receive bonuses or salary increases, while officials with unsatisfactory performance may see their opportunities for salary improvement limited. This link between performance evaluation and remuneration aims to motivate officials to continuously improve their performance.

Performance evaluation also influences training decisions, as it allows identifying the areas in which officials need to improve their competences. Training plans are established on the basis of evaluation results, and may include training courses, internal mobility opportunities, mentoring programmes and other professional development initiatives. Performance evaluation is therefore a fundamental tool for the development of officials' careers.

## Chapter 17: Labour Conflicts

### 17.1 Types of Labour Conflicts

Labour conflicts in EU institutions can be classified into several categories, including individual conflicts, collective conflicts, functional conflicts and conflicts of interest. Individual conflicts are those that arise between an official and the institution, and may be related to issues such as performance evaluation, promotion, remuneration or working conditions. Collective conflicts are those that arise between a group of officials and the institution, and are usually related to human resource policies that affect multiple officials.

Functional conflicts are those that arise as a result of misunderstandings, lack of communication or differences in working style between officials or between officials and supervisors. These conflicts can negatively affect the working environment and team productivity. Conflicts of interest are those that arise when an official has personal or financial interests that may conflict with their professional obligations. Conflicts of interest are especially delicate in European institutions, where impartiality and integrity are fundamental requirements.

The effective resolution of labour conflicts is essential for maintaining a productive and harmonious working environment in European institutions. Institutions have different conflict resolution mechanisms, including mediation, conciliation, negotiation and formal procedures. The choice of the appropriate resolution mechanism depends on the nature of the conflict, the seriousness of the parties involved and the parties' preferences.

### 17.2 Conflict Resolution Mechanisms

Mediation is a conflict resolution mechanism in which a neutral and impartial third party (the mediator) helps the parties to find a solution acceptable to all. Mediation is a voluntary and confidential process that allows the parties to explore different solution options without committing to accept a solution that does not satisfy them. Mediation is especially effective for functional conflicts and for individual conflicts that can be resolved through communication and negotiation.

Conciliation is a mechanism similar to mediation, but in which the neutral third party (the conciliator) has a more active role in proposing solutions. Conciliation is frequently used in collective conflicts, where the conciliator proposes solutions that may be accepted or rejected by the parties. Conciliation is a rapid and efficient process that can resolve conflicts without the need to resort to more formal procedures.

Formal conflict resolution procedures include administrative complaints, disciplinary proceedings and appeals before the Court of Justice of the European Union. These procedures are used when informal resolution mechanisms have not been effective or when the nature of the conflict requires a formal resolution. Formal procedures ensure that conflicts are resolved fairly and in accordance with the law.

### 17.3 Conflict Prevention

Conflict prevention is a proactive strategy that aims to reduce the likelihood of labour conflicts occurring in European institutions. Prevention strategies include improving communication, training in interpersonal skills, clearly defining roles and responsibilities, and creating a respectful and collaborative working environment. Conflict prevention is more effective and less costly than conflict resolution once conflicts have occurred.

Improving communication is one of the most effective conflict prevention strategies. Institutions must ensure that communication between supervisors and officials is clear, frequent and two-way. Effective communication allows potential problems to be identified before they become conflicts, and facilitates the resolution of misunderstandings. Institutions must also promote a culture of constructive feedback, in which officials feel comfortable expressing their opinions and concerns.

Training in interpersonal skills is another important conflict prevention strategy. Officials who have good communication, negotiation and conflict resolution skills are less likely to become involved in labour conflicts. Institutions offer training courses in these skills, which include effective communication techniques, negotiation strategies and conflict resolution methods. This training is especially valuable for supervisors, who are responsible for managing conflicts in their teams.

## Chapter 18: Sustainability of the Employment System

### 18.1 Challenges of the Employment System

European public employment faces a series of challenges that threaten its long-term sustainability. These challenges include the ageing of staff, budgetary pressure, digital transformation, globalization and the need to adapt to new forms of work. The ageing of staff is one of the most significant challenges, as a significant number of officials will retire in the coming years, which will generate recruitment needs to fill their posts.

Budgetary pressure is another important challenge, as European institutions must manage their resources efficiently to maintain the quality of the public service without excessively increasing costs. This implies the need to optimize work processes, use new technologies to improve efficiency and reconsider the organizational structure of institutions. Budgetary pressure also affects employment conditions, as institutions must balance the competitiveness of remuneration with the financial sustainability of the system.

Digital transformation is both a challenge and an opportunity for European public employment. The digitalization of work processes allows efficiency to be improved, costs to be reduced and better services to be provided to citizens. However, digital transformation also requires investment in training so that officials acquire the necessary digital competences, and may involve changes in employment structure, with the reduction of routine posts and the creation of new, more specialized professional profiles.

### 18.2 Sustainability Strategies

Sustainability strategies for European public employment include strategic human resource planning, investment in training and development, promotion of diversity and inclusion, modernization of selection processes and improvement of employment conditions. Strategic human resource planning allows institutions to anticipate staffing needs and take proactive measures to meet them, including identifying the posts that will become vacant as a result of retirements and planning selection processes.

Investment in training and development is fundamental to ensuring that officials have the competences necessary to face the challenges of the future. Institutions must offer continuous training programmes adapted to the changing needs of the working environment, including training in digital competences, leadership training and training in emerging functional areas. Investment in training benefits not only officials, but also contributes to the effectiveness and competitiveness of institutions.

The promotion of diversity and inclusion is another important sustainability strategy, as it ensures that institutions have staff that reflects the diversity of European citizens. Institutions must take measures to eliminate the barriers that prevent certain groups from accessing public employment, and must create an inclusive working environment in which all officials feel valued and respected. Diversity enriches institutions by bringing different perspectives, experiences and competences.

### 18.3 Future Vision

The future vision of European public employment implies the need to adapt to a changing environment and to innovate in human resource management practices. Institutions must be prepared to face new challenges, such as artificial intelligence, automation, climate change and the evolution of citizens' expectations. The capacity for adaptation and innovation will be fundamental to maintaining the relevance and effectiveness of the European public service.

The integration of artificial intelligence and automation into the work processes of European institutions is a trend that will accelerate in the coming years. These technologies will allow repetitive and routine tasks to be automated, freeing officials to focus on higher value-added tasks such as analysis, planning and decision-making. However, the integration of these technologies will require investment in training and adaptation of officials' competences.

The evolution of citizens' expectations regarding the European public service is another factor that will influence the future of the employment system. Citizens expect more efficient, transparent and accessible services, and institutions must adapt to meet these expectations. This will imply the need to invest in technology, improve staff training and create a citizen-centred organizational culture. The future of European public employment will depend on the capacity of institutions to adapt to changes and to provide citizens with a quality service.

## Chapter 19: Civil Liability of Officials

### 19.1 Principles of Civil Liability

The civil liability of EU officials is a legal principle that establishes the obligation of officials to repair the damage they cause to European institutions as a result of a fault committed in the exercise of their functions. This principle is enshrined in the Statute of Officials and is supplemented by the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union, which has interpreted and developed the provisions of the Statute in numerous judgments. Civil liability aims to ensure that European institutions can obtain reparation for damage suffered as a result of the conduct of their officials.

The principles of civil liability include the need to demonstrate a fault by the official, the existence of actual and real damage, and the causal relationship between the fault and the damage. These three elements are cumulative, which means that the absence of any of them prevents the establishment of the official's civil liability. The official's fault may be an act or an omission, and may be intentional (wilful) or negligent. The damage must be actual and real, not hypothetical or potential, and must be economically quantifiable.

The civil liability of officials is distinguished from disciplinary liability, which refers to the sanctions that may be imposed for breaches of codes of conduct. Civil liability focuses on the reparation of damage suffered by the institution, while disciplinary liability focuses on the sanction of the official for their conduct. Both liabilities may coexist, as the same fault may give rise to both a disciplinary sanction and a claim for damages.

### 19.2 Claims Procedure

The civil liability claims procedure begins when the institution identifies damage suffered as a result of the conduct of an official. The institution must determine whether the official's conduct constitutes a fault that has caused the damage, and whether there is a causal relationship between the conduct and the damage. If the institution considers that the conditions for claiming the official's civil liability are met, it initiates a claims procedure that may be administrative or judicial in nature.

The administrative claims procedure is carried out before the competent authority of the institution, which examines the evidence and decides whether to proceed with the claim for the official's civil liability. The official has the right to be informed of the claim, to access the evidence against them and to present their defence arguments. If the competent authority decides to claim civil liability, it may attempt an amicable settlement with the official, which consists of an agreement between the institution and the official on the amount of compensation.

If an amicable settlement is not possible, the institution may bring the case before the Court of Justice of the European Union to rule on the official's civil liability. The Court of Justice examines the evidence presented by both parties and decides whether the official is civilly liable and, if so, the amount of compensation that must be paid. The judgments of the Court of Justice are binding and must be enforced by the parties.

### 19.3 Quantification of Damage

The quantification of damage is a complex process that requires the assessment of all losses suffered by the institution as a result of the official's fault. Damage may be material or non-material, and must be assessed in an objective and reasonable manner. Material damage includes direct economic losses, such as expenses incurred to repair the damage or lost income, and indirect economic losses, such as lost profits. Non-material damage includes loss of reputation, moral damage and other non-economic damage that cannot be easily quantifiable.

The quantification of material damage is generally carried out by presenting supporting documents certifying the losses suffered, such as invoices, contracts, accounting reports and other relevant documents. The quantification of non-material damage is more complex, as there are no objective criteria for assessing non-economic damage. The Court of Justice has established in its case law that non-material damage may be quantified on the basis of the circumstances of the case, including the gravity of the fault, the repercussion of the fault and the impact on the institution's reputation.

The civil liability of the official may be joint, when several officials are jointly responsible for the same damage, or several, when each official is responsible only for the portion of the damage corresponding to them. Joint liability implies that the institution may claim the full amount of compensation from any of the liable officials, who may then claim from the other officials the portion corresponding to them. Joint liability is the general rule in cases where several officials contribute to causing the same damage.

## Chapter 20: Protection of Whistleblowers

### 20.1 The Legal Framework for Whistleblower Protection

The protection of whistleblowers is a fundamental principle that ensures that officials can report irregularities, fraud, corruption or any other illegal activity in EU institutions without fear of reprisals. The Statute of Officials and the European directive on whistleblower protection establish a comprehensive legal framework for the protection of officials who report irregularities in institutions. This legal framework aims to encourage the reporting of irregularities and combat corruption in European institutions.

The legal framework for whistleblower protection includes the definition of the conduct that may be reported, the available reporting channels, the procedural guarantees for whistleblowers and the sanctions against those responsible for reprisals. The conduct that may be reported includes fraud, corruption, waste of public funds, violations of codes of conduct, illegal activities and any other irregularity affecting EU interests. The reporting channels include the institution's internal channels, the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF) and the European Ombudsman.

Procedural guarantees for whistleblowers include the confidentiality of the whistleblower's identity, protection against reprisals, the right to be informed of the outcome of the investigation and the right to compensation in the event of reprisal. The confidentiality of the whistleblower's identity is fundamental to ensuring that officials feel comfortable reporting irregularities without fear of being identified and suffering reprisals. Protection against reprisals includes both protection against direct reprisals and against indirect reprisals.

### 20.2 Reporting Channels

Reporting channels are the mechanisms through which officials can report irregularities in EU institutions. Internal channels are the channels established by the institutions themselves to receive and manage reports from their officials. These channels include human resources services, integrity units, ethics committees and other specialized bodies. Internal channels are preferable because they allow rapid and effective management of reports, and because they ensure that the institution can take immediate measures to correct the reported irregularities.

The European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF) is an external reporting channel that receives and investigates reports of fraud, corruption and other illegal activities affecting the EU's financial interests. OLAF has the power to investigate cases in EU institutions, in Member States and in third countries, and may propose disciplinary, judicial or financial measures on the basis of the results of its investigations. OLAF ensures the confidentiality of whistleblowers' identities and protection against reprisals.

The European Ombudsman is another external reporting channel that receives complaints about maladministration in EU institutions. The Ombudsman investigates complaints and may make recommendations to institutions to correct the reported irregularities. The Ombudsman does not have the power to impose sanctions, but their recommendations carry significant political weight and are generally complied with by institutions.

### 20.3 Protection against Reprisals

Protection against reprisals is a fundamental right of whistleblowers that ensures that they can report irregularities without fear of negative consequences. Protection against reprisals includes both protection against direct reprisals (such as dismissal, demotion or unjustified sanction) and against indirect reprisals (such as professional isolation, the assignment of less qualified tasks or refusal of promotion). EU institutions have the obligation to investigate reprisal reports and to take the necessary measures to protect affected whistleblowers.

The European directive on whistleblower protection establishes a strict regulatory framework for protection against reprisals, which includes the prohibition of any form of reprisal, the right to compensation for damage suffered and the reversal of the burden of proof in the event of litigation. The reversal of the burden of proof implies that, if a whistleblower alleges having suffered a reprisal, it is for the institution to demonstrate that the measures taken are not reprisals but legitimate consequences of the whistleblower's conduct.

European institutions have adopted specific measures to ensure whistleblower protection, including the creation of specialized units for receiving and managing reports, the training of staff on whistleblower protection and the adoption of confidential procedures for investigating reports. These measures aim to create an environment in which officials feel safe reporting irregularities and in which reprisals are effectively sanctioned.

## Chapter 21: Change Management in Institutions

### 21.1 Change Processes in Institutions

EU institutions are subject to continuous change processes that respond to the evolution of citizens' needs, political changes and technological developments. Change management is the body of processes and tools that allow institutions to adapt to these changes effectively and while minimizing negative impacts on staff and on the quality of the public service. Change management is especially important in European institutions, which operate in a complex and multicultural environment where changes can affect multiple functional areas and staff of different nationalities.

Change processes in institutions can be of different types, including structural changes, technological changes, policy and procedure changes, and cultural changes. Structural changes include the reorganization of departments, the creation of new units and the abolition of others. Technological changes include the implementation of new information systems, the automation of processes and the digitalization of services. Policy and procedure changes include the updating of regulations, the introduction of new work procedures and the modification of human resource policies.

The effective management of change requires careful planning, transparent communication and active staff participation in the change process. Change planning includes identifying the objectives of the change, assessing the impact of the change on staff and activities, defining implementation strategies and establishing monitoring indicators. Change communication is fundamental to ensuring that staff understand the reasons for the change, its expected benefits and the support measures available.

### 21.2 Resistance to Change

Resistance to change is a natural phenomenon that occurs when officials perceive that the change threatens their interests, skills or working environment. Resistance to change may manifest itself in different ways, including open opposition, demotivation, reduced productivity and staff turnover. Resistance to change is especially common in European institutions, where officials may fear that changes will affect their employment conditions, competences or position in the organization.

The causes of resistance to change include lack of understanding of the reasons for the change, fear of the unknown, loss of control over the working environment, concern about job loss or loss of status, and the perception that the change will not benefit the official. Institutions must identify the specific causes of resistance to change in each situation and develop strategies to address them effectively.

Strategies for overcoming resistance to change include staff participation in decision-making, transparent and frequent communication, training to acquire the new competences required, emotional and professional support during the transition, and recognition of partial achievements. Staff participation is one of the most effective strategies, as officials who participate in decision-making about change are more likely to accept it and commit to its implementation.

### 21.3 The Role of Leaders in Change Management

The leaders of European institutions play a fundamental role in change management, as they are responsible for defining the vision of change, communicating the objectives of change, mobilizing staff and supervising the implementation of change measures. Leaders must demonstrate commitment to change, communicate the benefits of change effectively and provide the necessary support to staff during the transition.

Leadership in times of change requires specific skills, including the ability to communicate an inspiring vision, the ability to manage uncertainty, the ability to make rapid decisions and the ability to motivate staff. Leaders must be able to balance the need for speed in implementing change with the need to respect staff concerns and ensure a smooth transition.

Leaders are also responsible for creating an environment of trust in which officials feel comfortable expressing their concerns and suggesting improvements. Trust is a critical factor for the success of change processes, as officials who trust their leaders are more likely to accept change and commit to its implementation. Leaders must demonstrate integrity, transparency and consistency in their actions to build and maintain staff trust.

## Chapter 22: The Future of the Statute of Officials

### 22.1 Future Trends

The future of the Statute of EU Officials is marked by a series of trends that are transforming the way institutions manage their staff. These trends include digital transformation, automation, globalization, population ageing, climate change and the evolution of officials' expectations. Institutions need to adapt the Statute to these trends to ensure that it remains relevant and effective in a changing environment.

Digital transformation will be increasingly integrated into all aspects of public employment, from recruitment to performance management, through training and communication. The automation of repetitive tasks will allow officials to focus on higher value-added tasks, such as analysis, planning and decision-making. The globalization of the economy and politics will require officials with advanced intercultural and linguistic competences, who can work effectively in a multicultural and multilingual environment.

The ageing of the European population will have an impact on public employment, as a significant number of officials will retire in the coming years, which will generate recruitment needs to fill their posts. Climate change will require officials with competences in environmental sustainability, climate policies and natural resource management. The evolution of required competences will include new digital competences, analytical competences, leadership competences and adaptability to change competences.

### 22.2 Necessary Reforms

The necessary reforms of the Statute of Officials include adapting employment rules to new forms of work, modernizing selection processes, improving employment conditions and promoting diversity and inclusion. Adapting employment rules to new forms of work includes the regulation of teleworking, flexible working and project-based work, which are increasingly common forms of work in European institutions.

The modernization of selection processes includes the digitalization of tests, the introduction of new assessment techniques and the improvement of the candidate experience. Institutions must adopt more effective, efficient and accessible selection processes that allow candidates to be attracted and selected more quickly and effectively. The modernization of selection processes also includes the elimination of unnecessary barriers and the promotion of equal opportunities.

The improvement of employment conditions includes the review of remuneration, social benefits and career development opportunities. Institutions must ensure that their employment conditions are competitive to attract and retain the best professionals, and that they provide adequate protection for officials and their families. The improvement of employment conditions also includes the promotion of work-life balance and the creation of a healthy and satisfying working environment.

### 22.3 Long-Term Vision

The long-term vision of the Statute of Officials implies the need to create a flexible, adaptable and future-oriented regulatory framework that allows institutions to face the challenges of the 21st century. The Statute must be able to adapt to technological changes, the evolution of forms of work and the new expectations of officials, while maintaining the fundamental principles of equal treatment, meritocracy and transparency.

The Statute of the future must incorporate sustainability principles, including the promotion of work-life balance, the protection of the health and well-being of officials and the reduction of the carbon footprint of institutional activities. The Statute must also promote innovation and creativity, providing an environment in which officials can experiment with new ways of working and providing services to citizens.

The Statute of the future must be inclusive, ensuring that all persons, regardless of their origin, gender, disability or any other personal characteristic, have equal opportunities to access European public employment. Inclusiveness is not only an ethical principle, but also a competitive advantage that enriches institutions by bringing different perspectives, experiences and competences. The Statute of the future must therefore be a living instrument that evolves with the needs of institutions and their officials.
