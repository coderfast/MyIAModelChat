# History of the European Union

## Chapter 1: The origins of European integration

### The ECSC and the Schuman Plan

The European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) was established in 1951 through the Treaty of Paris, signed by six countries: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. This treaty represented an unprecedented milestone in European history, as for the first time sovereign nations decided to place strategic sectors of their economies under a common authority. The historical context was decisive: Europe was devastated after the Second World War, and the need for reconstruction was combined with the deep desire to prevent future conflicts between neighbors. The initiative came from the French Foreign Minister, Robert Schuman, who on May 9, 1950, presented a public declaration proposing an ambitious plan for European integration. This day is now celebrated as Europe Day, recognizing its importance as the starting point of the integration project.

Schuman's proposal did not arise in a vacuum, but was the result of deep reflections on the causes of the European wars of the 20th century. Jean Monnet, a French official considered the father of the European community, was the one who designed the detailed plan that Schuman presented to public opinion. The central idea was simple but revolutionary: by placing coal and steel production under a common authority, countries would become so economically interconnected that armed conflict would become materially impossible. This economic logic as the foundation of peace was the guiding principle of all subsequent European construction. The Treaty of Paris entered into force on July 23, 1952, establishing a supranational high authority with real powers to make binding decisions on the production and distribution of these resources.

The ECSC achieved immediate success in its economic mission, managing to increase coal and steel production in member countries and reducing prices through the creation of a common market for these products. However, its most significant impact was political: it demonstrated that supranational cooperation was viable and beneficial. The six member countries began to experience shared prosperity that contrasted with the postwar period. The institution had a high authority, a council of ministers, a parliamentary assembly, and a court of justice, laying the foundations of the institutional framework that would later be adopted by the future European communities. The ECSC experience was fundamental in convincing European leaders that integration should be extended to other economic sectors and, eventually, to the entire economy.

The success of the ECSC was due to several factors. First, the choice of coal and steel as pilot sectors was correct, as they were the foundation of heavy industry and war machinery, so their pooling had enormous symbolic and political value. Second, the high authority had real powers to make decisions, which allowed it to act effectively without depending on the will of national governments. Third, the economic benefits of integration were rapid and visible, which generated popular and political support for the project. The ECSC laid the institutional, legal, and political foundations for subsequent European construction, and its success was decisive for the decision to extend integration to other economic sectors.

### The Treaty of Rome and the EEC

On March 25, 1957, the same six countries that had signed the Treaty of Paris subscribed to the Treaties of Rome, which established the European Economic Community (EEC) and the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom). The Treaty of Rome was a much more ambitious document than that of Paris, as it was not limited to a single economic sector, but sought to create a complete common market among the Member States. The objective was to progressively eliminate obstacles to the trade of goods, services, capital, and people within the community, establishing a customs union with a common tariff vis-a-vis the outside. This treaty entered into force on January 1, 1958, inaugurating a new stage in European integration.

The Treaty of Rome established the fundamental principles that would guide European economic construction for decades. Article 2 defined the objective of promoting a harmonious development of economic activities, a continuous and balanced expansion, an increase in stability, a convergence of the policies of the Member States, and an improvement in the standard of living. To achieve these objectives, the treaty provided for the gradual elimination of customs duties between Member States, the adoption of a common tariff vis-a-vis third countries, the free movement of people, services, and capital, and the harmonization of economic policies. The treaty also established the principle of community loyalty, which obliged Member States not to adopt measures that could jeopardize the achievement of common objectives.

The creation of the EEC was received with enthusiasm by the economic sectors of the member countries, which saw in the common market an opportunity to expand their activities and increase their competitiveness. The elimination of trade barriers allowed European companies to access a much larger market, stimulating investment, innovation, and economic growth. However, economic integration was not without difficulties: member countries had different levels of economic development, and the common agricultural policy (CAP) had to be designed to protect European farmers while ensuring food supply. The CAP became one of the most important and costly policies of the EEC, and its reform has been a recurring theme in community history.

The Treaty of Rome also established a common tariff system that protected European agriculture through a dual pricing mechanism: a minimum price system that guaranteed the profitability of European farmers, and a system of import duties that protected the internal market from foreign competition. This policy was especially important for countries like France, Italy, and Belgium, which had large and politically influential agricultural sectors. The CAP was, from its origins, a solidarity policy, as it ensured that European consumers could access food at reasonable prices while ensuring the survival of agricultural holdings. Over the decades, the CAP has been reformed several times to adapt to new economic realities and to reduce its cost, but it remains one of the most important and symbolic policies of the European Union.

The process of economic integration advanced rapidly during the 1960s and 1970s. The customs union was completed in 1968, eliminating all customs duties between Member States and establishing the common tariff. The free movement of people was partially achieved through the elimination of internal border controls, although this process was not completed until the adoption of the Schengen area in the 1980s and 1990s. The common transport policy and common energy policy were also developed during this period, although with mixed results. The economic growth of member countries during these years was spectacular, with growth rates higher than those of other industrialized countries, confirming the benefits of economic integration.

### The first institutional steps

The institutional structure established by the Treaties of Rome was designed to ensure the effective functioning of the community while respecting the sovereignty of the Member States. The European Commission, headquartered in Brussels, was designated as the executive body responsible for proposing new legislation, implementing common policies, and representing the community externally. The Council of Ministers, composed of representatives of the governments of the Member States, was the main decision-making body, responsible for adopting the legislative proposals presented by the Commission. The European Parliament, initially called the European Assembly and composed of delegates designated by national parliaments, had limited consultative and supervisory functions. The Court of Justice was entrusted with ensuring the uniform interpretation and application of community law.

The decision-making process in the EEC was based on a careful balance between national interests and common objectives. The Commission, as a supranational body, had the monopoly of the legislative initiative, which allowed it to guide the political agenda of the community. However, the Commission's proposals had to be approved by the Council of Ministers, where national representatives defended the interests of their respective countries. This co-decision mechanism, although slow, ensured that all decisions were the result of consensus among the Member States. The Court of Justice played a crucial role in the consolidation of community law, establishing fundamental principles such as direct effect and the supremacy of community law over national law.

The Court of Justice played a crucial role in the consolidation of community law from the earliest years of the community. In 1963, in the Van Gend en Loos case, the Court established the principle of direct effect, which allows citizens to invoke community law before national courts. In 1964, in the Costa v. ENEL case, the Court confirmed the supremacy of community law over national law, even over the constitutional provisions of the Member States. These principles were fundamental in ensuring that community law was applied uniformly in all Member States, and laid the foundations for the development of the single market. The case law of the Court of Justice has been one of the pillars of European construction, as it has allowed European law to have real effects in the lives of citizens and companies.

## Chapter 2: The founders of the European Union

### Robert Schuman and the French vision

Robert Schuman (1886-1963) was a Franco-German statesman who played a decisive role in creating the foundations of European integration. Born in Luxembourg and raised in German territory, Schuman experienced firsthand the consequences of national rivalries in Europe. His political trajectory was marked by his commitment to Franco-German reconciliation and his vision of a common European future. As French Foreign Minister, Schuman presented on May 9, 1950, the declaration that bears his name, proposing the creation of the ECSC as the first step toward an integrated Europe. His proposal was bold: it turned France, the victorious power of the war, into the promoter of economic equality with Germany, the defeated power.

Schuman's vision was based on the conviction that peace in Europe could only be lasting if the economic and institutional conditions were created that made conflict impossible. Schuman was not a naive idealist, but a pragmatic politician who understood that integration had to be mutually beneficial to be sustainable. His ECSC proposal was an example of this philosophy: by placing coal and steel under a common authority, France gained access to German resources, while Germany gained access to the French market. Both countries benefited, and the resulting interdependence made war inconceivable. This logic of mutual benefit was the driving force behind all European construction.

Schuman was also a defender of the role of supranational institutions in European governance. Unlike other leaders of his time, who preferred intergovernmental cooperation, Schuman believed that common institutions had to have real powers to make binding decisions. This conviction was fundamental to the design of the ECSC institutions and, later, those of the EEC. The European Parliament, the Commission, and the Court of Justice owe much to Schuman's vision of the need for institutions that transcended national interests. Schuman's legacy is remembered every May 9, when Europe celebrates its Day, recognizing his decisive contribution to the construction of the European project.

Schuman had to overcome numerous resistances to carry out his project. In France, Gaullists and communists opposed integration for different reasons: the former feared that France would lose its national sovereignty, while the latter saw the ECSC as an instrument of American capitalism. In Germany, resistance came from industrial sectors that feared losing control over coal and steel production. In Great Britain, the Labour government of Clement Attlee rejected Schuman's proposal, preferring to maintain a close relationship with the United States through NATO. These resistances tested Schuman's ability to negotiate and build consensus, and his success was a testament to his political skill and the strength of his vision.

### Jean Monnet and the community method

Jean Monnet (1888-1979) is considered the father of the European community for his role as strategist and planner of European integration. Unlike Schuman, who was a career politician, Monnet was a businessman and international official who combined his practical experience with an innovative political vision. Monnet understood that European integration could not be achieved through grand political declarations, but required a gradual and pragmatic approach. His method, known as the community method, consisted of achieving concrete progress in specific areas of cooperation, creating facts on the ground that would demonstrate the benefits of integration. This step-by-step approach was fundamental to the success of the ECSC and the EEC.

Monnet's community method was based on several fundamental principles. First, integration should begin with specific economic sectors where cooperation was technical and less politically sensitive. Second, common institutions had to have real powers to make decisions, which required a partial transfer of sovereignty by Member States. Third, integration had to be progressive, so that each successful step created the conditions for the next. Fourth, the positive results of integration had to be visible to citizens, which would generate political support for moving toward a deeper union. These principles guided the creation of the ECSC and laid the foundations for the Treaty of Rome.

Monnet was also a master of negotiation and political persuasion. He knew how to find arguments that resonated with different audiences, adapting his discourse to both political leaders and public opinion. His ability to build consensus and overcome resistance was crucial to the signing of the Treaties of Rome in 1957. Monnet founded the Action Committee for the United States of Europe, an organization that promoted European integration through information campaigns and lobbying. Throughout the 1950s and 1960s, Monnet worked tirelessly to expand integration, defending the creation of a common foreign policy, a European defense, and a political union. Although not all his projects were realized, his influence on European construction was profound and lasting.

Monnet's influence extended beyond the ECSC and the EEC. He was an advisor to several European governments and maintained permanent contact with the political leaders of member countries. His ability to influence decision-making was based on his network of personal contacts, his deep knowledge of economic affairs, and his ability to present European integration as a pragmatic solution to postwar problems. Monnet was also a pioneer in using communication to promote integration, as he understood that popular support was essential for the success of the project. His work as founder and president of the Action Committee for the United States of Europe was fundamental in creating a pro-European movement throughout the community.

### Konrad Adenauer and Franco-German reconciliation

Konrad Adenauer (1876-1967) was the first Chancellor of the Federal Republic of Germany after the Second World War, and his role in reconciliation with France was fundamental to the success of European integration. Adenauer assumed office in 1949 with a clear objective: to rehabilitate Germany in the international community and overcome the isolation it had experienced during the Nazi period. His foreign policy was oriented toward the West, seeking to strengthen ties with France, Great Britain, and the United States. Reconciliation with France was the cornerstone of this strategy, and Adenauer understood that European economic integration was the best way to achieve it. His support for Schuman's ECSC proposal was decisive for its success.

Adenauer faced significant resistance both within Germany and abroad. In his own country, some political and economic sectors feared that integration would limit German sovereignty and harm their national interests. Abroad, suspicions about Germany's intentions remained alive, especially in France, where the memory of the occupation was recent. Adenauer worked with perseverance to overcome these resistances, using his personal charisma, his political experience, and his negotiating skills to build bridges of trust. His personal partnership with Charles de Gaulle, although not free of tensions, became a symbol of Franco-German reconciliation and European commitment.

Adenauer's legacy in European construction goes beyond reconciliation with France. Under his chancellorship, Germany was fully integrated into European institutions, becoming a responsible actor committed to common objectives. Adenauer was also a defender of the expansion of integration, supporting the inclusion of new member countries and the expansion of the competences of the European communities. His vision of a free, democratic, and prosperous Europe guided German foreign policy for decades and contributed significantly to the success of the European project. Franco-German reconciliation driven by Adenauer became the fundamental pillar on which all subsequent European construction was built.

### Alcide De Gasperi and Italy's European vocation

Alcide De Gasperi (1881-1954) was an Italian politician who played a fundamental role in creating the foundations of European integration. As Italy's first postwar prime minister, De Gasperi understood that the reconstruction of the country passed through cooperation with its European neighbors. Italy had suffered greatly during the war and sought to reintegrate into the international community. European integration offered Italy the opportunity to demonstrate that it was a democratic and responsible country, and to access reconstruction funds and broader markets. De Gasperi was one of the six signatories of the Treaty of Paris in 1951 and of the Treaty of Rome in 1957, and his commitment to integration was unwavering.

De Gasperi shared with Schuman and Monnet the conviction that peace in Europe could only be achieved through economic and political integration. His personal experience as a political prisoner under fascism had taught him the dangers of extreme nationalism and the importance of international cooperation. De Gasperi worked closely with Schuman and Monnet to design the institutions of the ECSC and the EEC, and defended the need for common institutions to have real powers. His influence was especially important in defining the common agricultural policy, which was of vital importance for Italy, a country with a significant agricultural sector.

De Gasperi's European commitment was also reflected in his support for the creation of a European defense. De Gasperi was one of the main defenders of the European Defense Community (EDC), a project that would have created a common European army. Although the EDC was rejected by the French Parliament in 1954, De Gasperi's project laid the foundations for the defense cooperation that developed in subsequent decades. De Gasperi died in 1954, but his European legacy endures in the institutions he helped to create and in Italy's European vocation, which remains one of the Member States most committed to integration.

### Paul-Henri Spaak and the expansion of integration

Paul-Henri Spaak (1899-1972) was a Belgian politician who played a fundamental role in the expansion of European integration. As Belgian Foreign Minister, Spaak was one of the main negotiators of the Treaties of Rome in 1957, and his role was decisive in overcoming differences among the six signatory countries. Spaak shared Schuman and Monnet's vision of the need to integrate European economies, but also understood that integration should be extended to new sectors and new countries. His experience as foreign minister of a small but trade-open country helped him understand the importance of the common market for economic prosperity.

Spaak had to overcome numerous difficulties during the negotiations of the Treaties of Rome. Member countries had different priorities: France wanted to protect its agriculture, Germany wanted to secure market access, Italy was seeking reconstruction funds, and the Netherlands and Belgium wanted to guarantee the free movement of goods. Spaak used his negotiating skills to find compromises that satisfied all parties, and his ability to build trust among negotiators was decisive for the success of the negotiations. The result was a treaty that balanced the interests of all member countries and laid the foundations for a complete common market.

After the Treaties of Rome, Spaak continued to defend the expansion of integration. He was one of the main defenders of the common foreign policy and defense cooperation. He also supported the expansion of the community to new countries, such as Great Britain, Ireland, and Denmark, although these enlargements occurred after his mandate. Spaak was also a defender of the European Parliament, which he considered essential for the democratization of integration. His influence on European construction extended beyond the Treaties of Rome, as he worked tirelessly to deepen integration and make Europe a global actor.

### Johan Willem Beyen and the economic union

Johan Willem Beyen (1897-1976) was a Dutch politician and diplomat who played a fundamental role in the design of European economic integration. As Dutch Foreign Minister, Beyen was one of the main negotiators of the Treaty of Rome in 1957, and his influence was decisive in defining the objectives and instruments of the common market. Beyen understood that economic integration should be the foundation of European construction, and worked to create a market that allowed the free movement of goods, services, capital, and people.

Beyen had an especially innovative vision of economic integration. Unlike other leaders, who focused on the elimination of customs tariffs, Beyen defended the need to eliminate all barriers to trade, including non-tariff barriers, such as differences in technical standards, homologation requirements, and administrative regulations. This vision was fundamental to the creation of the single market, which was completed in 1993 with the elimination of non-tariff barriers. Beyen also defended the need for a competition policy that guaranteed equal opportunities for all companies in the common market.

Beyen's legacy in European construction extends beyond the Treaties of Rome. He was a defender of monetary cooperation, anticipating the need for a single currency that would stabilize exchange rates and facilitate trade. He also defended the expansion of the community to new countries and the deepening of integration in areas such as research, education, and culture. Beyen was a visionary who understood that economic integration was not an end in itself, but a means to create a more prosperous, more democratic, and more united Europe. His influence on European construction is undeniable, and his legacy endures in the institutions and policies of the European Union.

## Chapter 3: The enlargements of the European Union

### The first enlargement (1973)

The first enlargement of the European Communities took place on January 1, 1973, when Great Britain, Ireland, and Denmark joined the EEC. This enlargement was the result of long negotiations and reflections both in the candidate countries and in the existing Member States. Great Britain, which had been rejected twice previously by France, was finally admitted following the change in policy of President Charles de Gaulle, who had opposed British inclusion fearing it would weaken integration. The admission of Great Britain, the largest economy in Western Europe at the time, significantly transformed the balance of power within the community and set an important precedent for future enlargements.

Ireland and Denmark also joined in 1973, for different reasons. Ireland, which was seeking to diversify its economy and reduce its dependence on Great Britain, saw community membership as an opportunity to modernize and grow economically. Denmark, for its part, sought to guarantee its access to the common market and strengthen its position in Europe. The inclusion of these three countries increased the number of EEC members to nine and significantly expanded its territory, population, and economic power. The 1973 enlargement demonstrated that European integration could grow and adapt to new realities, setting a precedent for future enlargements.

The accession process of Great Britain, Ireland, and Denmark was complex and prolonged. Each country had to negotiate specific accession conditions, adapt its national legislation to community law, and demonstrate that it met the economic and political criteria necessary to join the community. Great Britain, in particular, negotiated special conditions related to the common agricultural policy and budgetary contributions, which generated tensions with other Member States. These negotiations laid the foundations for future accession processes, establishing a bilateral negotiation model that has been maintained to the present day. The first enlargement also highlighted the challenges of integrating different economies and political systems within a common community framework.

The 1973 enlargement had significant consequences for community policies. Great Britain, with its pragmatic economic approach and free trade tradition, influenced the orientation of the EEC toward more market-oriented policies. Ireland and Denmark brought new perspectives on agriculture, fisheries, and regional development. The enlargement also generated tensions, as the new members had different priorities from the founding states. These tensions tested the community's ability to adapt to diversity and maintain cohesion among countries with different histories and political trajectories.

### The southern enlargements (1981 and 1986)

The next enlargement of the EEC took place in 1981, when Greece joined the community, followed in 1986 by Spain and Portugal. These enlargements had special political significance, as the three countries had recently overcome authoritarian regimes and were seeking to consolidate their young democracies through integration into European institutions. Greece, which had emerged from a military dictatorship in 1974, saw community membership as a guarantee of democratic stability and an engine of economic modernization. Spain and Portugal, which had left the Franco and Salazar regimes in 1975, sought the same: to use European integration as a political anchor and catalyst for economic reforms.

The accession process of the southern countries was long and demanding. Greece had to carry out significant economic reforms to reach the development levels of existing Member States, while Spain and Portugal had to adapt their judicial, administrative, and economic systems to community law. The European Commission closely supervised these adaptation processes, providing technical and financial assistance to facilitate the transition. The southern enlargements also had a significant impact on community policies, especially the common agricultural policy, as the three countries had important agricultural sectors that required support and protection.

The inclusion of Greece, Spain, and Portugal transformed the EEC from a Western European organization into a community that encompassed most of the Iberian Peninsula and southeastern Europe. This geographical expansion brought new challenges, such as the need to improve transport and communication infrastructure to connect peripheral regions with the center of the community. European regional policy was largely developed in response to these challenges, with structural funds aimed at reducing economic disparities between the regions of the community. The southern enlargements also reinforced the democratic character of the EEC, by including countries that had actively fought for freedom and democracy.

The southern enlargements also had a significant cultural impact. The inclusion of countries with rich cultural traditions, different histories, and their own languages enriched the diversity of the community. Spain, Portugal, and Greece contributed their gastronomic, artistic, and literary traditions to the common European heritage. Cultural diversity became an asset of the community, as it enriched intercultural dialogue and fostered mutual understanding among European peoples. The southern enlargements demonstrated that European integration was not only an economic and political project, but also a cultural and social one.

### The eastern enlargement (2004 and 2007)

The largest enlargement in the history of the European Union took place on May 1, 2004, when ten new countries joined the EU: Poland, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Slovakia, Slovenia, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Cyprus, and Malta. This enlargement, known as the eastern enlargement, was the result of an integration process that had begun with the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 and the collapse of communist regimes in Eastern Europe. The countries of Central and Eastern Europe had aspired to join the European Union since the early 1990s, and their inclusion represented the culmination of an unprecedented political and economic effort.

The accession process of the eastern countries was the most ambitious and complex ever undertaken by the European Union. Each candidate country had to demonstrate that it met the Copenhagen criteria, established in 1993, which included the existence of stable institutions guaranteeing democracy, the rule of law, human rights, and respect for minorities, as well as a functioning market economy and the capacity to assume the obligations of membership. The European Commission supervised the progress of each country through periodic reports, and the European Council took the final decisions on accession. This process lasted almost a decade and required deep reforms in all candidate countries.

The 2004 enlargement radically transformed the European Union, both geographically, demographically, and economically. The population of the EU increased by approximately 75 million people, and its territory expanded to the borders of Russia and Belarus. The economy of the community also changed, as the new members brought growing markets, skilled labor, and natural resources. However, the enlargement also generated significant challenges, such as the need to coordinate economic policies among countries with very different levels of development, and the management of migration flows from the new Member States to the old ones. Bulgaria and Romania subsequently joined in 2007, completing the eastern enlargement.

The eastern enlargement also had a significant political impact. The new Member States brought unique experiences of democratic transition and economic reforms, enriching the political debate in the EU. The countries of Central and Eastern Europe brought a different perspective on national sovereignty, security, and relations with Russia, which influenced the political priorities of the community. The eastern enlargement also reinforced the democratic character of the EU, by including countries that had actively fought for freedom and democracy during the Cold War.

### The most recent enlargement and future candidacies

The most recent enlargement of the European Union took place in 2013, when Croatia became the 28th Member State. Croatia was the first country from the former Yugoslavia to join the EU, and its accession had special significance as a symbol of reconciliation and stabilization in the Western Balkans. Croatia's accession process was long and demanding, requiring significant reforms in areas such as the judicial system, the fight against corruption, and the protection of minority rights. The European Commission closely supervised Croatia's progress, and the accession process was completed in seven years from the initial application.

Currently, several Western Balkan countries are in the process of accession to the European Union. Serbia, North Macedonia, Montenegro, Albania, and Bosnia and Herzegovina are official candidates, while Kosovo is a potential candidate. These countries face significant challenges on their path to membership, including the need to carry out democratic reforms, improve their economies, and resolve outstanding regional conflicts. The European Union has established a clear framework for the accession process, which includes the opening and closing of negotiation chapters, supervision of progress through periodic reports, and financial and technical assistance to facilitate reforms.

Turkey is another country with candidate status, although its accession process has been paralyzed since 2005 due to concerns about the rule of law, human rights, and the Cyprus issue. Ukraine, Moldova, and Georgia applied to join the EU in 2022, following the Russian invasion of Ukraine, and were recognized as candidates. Georgia has had candidate status since 2023. The future enlargement of the European Union will be determined by the ability of candidate countries to meet the accession criteria and by the political will of existing Member States. The history of enlargements demonstrates that the EU is a dynamic project, capable of growing and adapting to new realities, while maintaining its commitment to democracy, the rule of law, and economic prosperity.

## Chapter 4: The euro and the single currency

### The origins of the monetary project

The idea of a single European currency has its roots in the earliest years of economic integration. The 1957 Treaty of Rome already provided for the possibility of harmonizing the monetary policies of the Member States, although it did not establish a concrete timetable for this. During the 1960s and 1970s, monetary cooperation developed through the European Monetary System (EMS), which sought to maintain exchange rate stability among European currencies. The EMS, created in 1979, was a partial success: it reduced exchange rate volatility but was unable to prevent the monetary crises that affected several member countries during the 1980s and 1990s. These experiences demonstrated the need for a deeper level of monetary integration.

The euro project was formalized with the signing of the Maastricht Treaty in 1992, which established the timetable and criteria for the creation of the monetary union. The treaty defined the convergence criteria that Member States had to meet to join the euro area: low inflation, stable exchange rates, sound public finances (deficit below 3% of GDP and debt below 60% of GDP), and stable long-term interest rates. These criteria were designed to ensure that countries joining the euro area had economies sufficiently similar to function under a single monetary policy. The creation of the European Monetary Institute in 1994 was an important step toward the monetary union, as this body was responsible for preparing the transition to the euro.

The process of creating the euro was complex and full of obstacles. Not all Member States were convinced of the benefits of the single currency, and some, like Great Britain and Denmark, negotiated opt-out clauses that allowed them not to join the euro area. There were also technical and political resistances: national central banks feared losing their autonomy, and governments were concerned about the fiscal limits imposed by the stability pact. However, the political commitment of European leaders, especially those of France and Germany, was decisive in overcoming these resistances and completing the process of creating the euro.

The debate on the monetary union was intense during the 1980s and 1990s. Economists argued for and against the euro, with concerns about the loss of monetary sovereignty, the need for coordinated fiscal policies, and the impact on economic convergence. Politicians had to balance technical concerns with political objectives, and the final decision to create the euro area was the result of a political compromise that balanced integration aspirations with national realities. The Maastricht Treaty was the document that formalized this compromise, and its implementation required unprecedented cooperation among Member States.

### The introduction of the euro

The euro was officially introduced on January 1, 1999, as an accounting currency for the eleven countries that met the convergence criteria: Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Austria, Finland, Ireland, and Portugal. Greece joined in 2001, becoming the twelfth member of the euro area. The introduction of the euro was a historic moment, as it represented the culmination of decades of economic integration and the birth of one of the world's most important currencies. The euro quickly became the world's second reserve currency, after the US dollar, and its use extended to international financial markets.

The physical introduction of euro banknotes and coins took place on January 1, 2002, when eleven countries simultaneously changed their national currencies to the euro. This transition was one of the most complex logistical operations ever undertaken by governmental organizations, as it required the production, distribution, and circulation of billions of banknotes and coins within a very short period. Citizens had to adapt quickly to the new currency, changing their savings and learning to coexist with two monetary systems during a transition period. The operation was generally a success, although there were concerns about price increases during the currency change process.

The creation of the euro had profound consequences for the European economy. On the one hand, it eliminated transaction costs and the uncertainty associated with fluctuating exchange rates, facilitating trade and investment among euro area countries. On the other hand, it meant the transfer of monetary sovereignty of the Member States to the European Central Bank (ECB), which assumed responsibility for monetary policy for the entire euro area. This transfer of sovereignty generated debate about the democratization of monetary decision-making, as the ECB is an independent institution that does not directly account to citizens. Despite these criticisms, the euro has remained the core of European economic integration.

The introduction of the euro also had an impact on the cultural identity of European citizens. Euro banknotes and coins incorporate architectural motifs representing the different periods of European history, from ancient Greece to modern architecture, symbolizing unity in diversity. The euro became a tangible symbol of European integration, as citizens use it daily in their transactions. The adoption of the euro also facilitated price comparison among the different countries of the euro area, stimulating competition and benefiting consumers. The euro is, ultimately, much more than a currency: it is a symbol of integration and European identity.

### The euro crisis and its consequences

The euro area faced its greatest challenge during the global financial crisis of 2008 and the subsequent sovereign debt crisis. The crisis exposed structural weaknesses in the initial design of the euro: the lack of a complete fiscal union, the absence of solidarity mechanisms to deal with crises, and differences in competitiveness between northern and southern European countries. Greece was the most affected country, when it was discovered that its public deficit was much larger than declared, generating a confidence crisis that spread to other peripheral countries such as Portugal, Ireland, Spain, and Italy. The crisis endangered the survival of the euro and required unprecedented interventions by European institutions and national governments.

The response to the euro crisis included the creation of financial rescue mechanisms, such as the European Stability Mechanism (ESM), and the adoption of austerity policies in affected countries. The European Central Bank played a crucial role, announcing in 2012 that it would do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro, which calmed financial markets. However, the austerity policies generated strong criticism for their social impact, including rising unemployment, reductions in public services, and the deterioration of living conditions for millions of European citizens. The crisis also generated a debate on the need to reform the economic governance of the euro area, including the creation of a common budget and solidarity mechanisms.

The consequences of the euro crisis have been lasting and have transformed the European Union. New institutions have been created, such as the Single Resolution Mechanism and the Single Supervisory Mechanism, to improve the supervision and regulation of banks in the euro area. Fiscal rules have also been reinforced through the Stability and Growth Pact, although their application has been flexible in some cases. The crisis has also had a significant political impact, fueling Euroscepticism and populism in several Member States. Despite these challenges, the euro area has demonstrated its resilience and has continued to expand, with the inclusion of Croatia in 2023.

The euro crisis also highlighted the importance of European solidarity. Northern European countries, especially Germany, had to provide financial assistance to southern countries, generating political tensions on both sides. Citizens of northern countries criticized what they perceived as irresponsible use of public funds, while citizens of southern countries criticized the austerity policies imposed on them. These tensions tested the cohesion of the EU and the willingness of Member States to maintain solidarity in times of crisis. The euro crisis was a reminder that economic integration requires not only common rules, but also solidarity and mutual trust.

## Chapter 5: The great challenges of the European Union

### The refugee crisis and migration

The European Union has faced one of its most critical challenges with the refugee and migration crisis that began in 2015. Thousands of people fled conflicts in Syria, Afghanistan, Iraq, and other countries, seeking protection and a better life in Europe. The crisis tested the EU's fundamental values, such as solidarity and human rights, and exposed the weaknesses of the European asylum system, which is based on the principle of first country of entry. Southern European countries, such as Greece and Italy, which are the main entry points for refugees, criticized the lack of solidarity from other Member States, which refused to accept a significant number of refugees.

The European Union's response to the migration crisis included the creation of relocation mechanisms, the expansion of external border operations through Frontex, and the signing of agreements with countries of origin and transit to manage migration flows. However, these measures were insufficient to resolve the crisis, and the lack of consensus among Member States generated significant tensions. Some countries, such as Hungary and Poland, strongly opposed relocation mechanisms, while others, such as Germany, adopted more open policies. The crisis also fueled the rise of far-right parties in several European countries, which exploited citizens' fears about immigration and security.

The management of migration remains one of the most divisive issues in the European Union. Deep reforms of the European asylum system have been proposed, including a permanent solidarity mechanism and better management of external borders. The European Border and Coast Guard Agency (Frontex) has been reinforced to improve border surveillance and control, but its performance has been questioned by reports of human rights violations. The European Union has also established migration dialogues with African and North African countries to address the root causes of migration, such as poverty, conflicts, and climate change. The refugee crisis has highlighted the need for a more coordinated and solidarity-based approach to migration in the EU.

The refugee crisis also highlighted the importance of international cooperation in managing migration flows. The EU established agreements with Turkey, Libya, and other countries to manage migration flows, but these agreements were criticized by human rights organizations, which argued that they violated the rights of refugees. The EU has also worked with the UN and other international organizations to improve the protection of refugees and to address the root causes of migration. The refugee crisis is a reminder that migration is a global phenomenon that requires global and solidarity-based responses.

### Brexit and the departure of Great Britain

The referendum of June 23, 2016, in which 51.9% of British citizens voted in favor of leaving the European Union, was one of the most dramatic moments in the history of European integration. Brexit brought to an end 43 years of British membership in the European community and generated a complex and prolonged negotiation process between Great Britain and the EU. The negotiations focused on issues such as citizens' rights, the border between Ireland and Northern Ireland, Great Britain's financial obligations, and the future conditions of trade relations. The withdrawal agreement was signed in January 2020, and Great Britain formally left the EU on January 31, 2020, completing the transition period on December 31, 2020.

Brexit had significant consequences for both Great Britain and the European Union. For the EU, the departure of Great Britain represented the first time that a Member State left the community, and generated concerns about the cohesion and sustainability of the European project. For Great Britain, Brexit has had a significant economic impact, with a reduction in trade with the EU, difficulties for businesses, and disruptions in the labor market. The Irish border issue remains a sensitive topic, as the Northern Ireland protocol establishes customs controls in the Irish Sea, generating political tensions both in Northern Ireland and in the Republic of Ireland.

Brexit has also had a disciplining effect on the European Union, demonstrating that leaving the community has significant costs. Several countries that previously showed Eurosceptic tendencies have moderated their positions after seeing the consequences of Brexit. The EU has seized the opportunity to strengthen its internal cohesion and advance in areas that were stagnant, such as common defense and security cooperation. Brexit has also reinforced the importance of the euro as a reserve currency, as the pound sterling has lost some of its position vis-a-vis the euro and the dollar. Despite the difficulties, relations between the EU and Great Britain remain important, and both parties are working to maintain close cooperation in areas of mutual interest.

Brexit also had an impact on the perception of the EU worldwide. Great Britain's departure was seen by some as a sign of weakness of the European project, while others saw it as an opportunity to strengthen integration among the remaining Member States. The EU has seized the situation to advance in areas such as common defense, fiscal policy, and foreign policy, demonstrating that integration can advance even with fewer members. Brexit is a reminder that European integration is a voluntary project, and that Member States have the right to leave if they consider that membership no longer benefits them.

### The rise of populism and Euroscepticism

In recent years, the European Union has faced the growing challenge of populism and Euroscepticism in several of its Member States. Far-right and far-left populist parties have gained ground in national and European elections, exploiting citizens' concerns about immigration, economic security, and national sovereignty. These parties tend to criticize European integration, arguing that the EU is disconnected from citizens, undermines national sovereignty, and does not adequately protect the economic interests of member countries. The rise of populism has generated significant tensions within the EU, as several national governments have opposed common policies and blocked important decisions.

Euroscepticism manifests itself in different ways in the various EU countries. In some countries, such as Hungary and Poland, populist governments have questioned democratic values and the rule of law, generating conflicts with European institutions. In other countries, such as Italy and France, Eurosceptic parties have gained popular support by criticizing austerity policies and the lack of solidarity in the euro area. In countries like Spain and Portugal, Euroscepticism is less significant, but there are also concerns about the lack of democratization in EU decision-making. Euroscepticism has also been fueled by the perception that the EU has not been able to respond effectively to the economic and social challenges affecting citizens.

The European Union has responded to the challenge of populism by reinforcing its commitment to democratic values and the rule of law. Mechanisms such as Article 7 of the Treaty on European Union have been activated, which allows sanctions against Member States that violate the fundamental values of the community. The EU has also intensified its efforts to improve communication with citizens through information campaigns, citizen dialogues, and the promotion of democratic participation. Despite these efforts, populism remains a significant challenge, as it exploits the real frustrations of citizens about the lack of economic opportunities, inequality, and insecurity. The EU's ability to respond effectively to these problems will be decisive for its future.

Populism has also had an impact on the EU's external relations. Populist governments of some Member States have questioned the EU's foreign policy, opposing sanctions against Russia, rejecting cooperation with international organizations, and questioning trade agreements. These positions have weakened the EU's ability to act as a single global actor, and have generated tensions with other Member States that defend a more cohesive foreign policy. The challenge of populism is, ultimately, an existential challenge for the EU, as it calls into question the values and principles on which the European project was built.

## Chapter 6: The EU facing the future

### The European Green Deal

The European Union has adopted the European Green Deal as its roadmap for achieving climate neutrality by 2050. This ambitious plan covers all sectors of the economy, from energy and transport to agriculture and industry, and requires massive investments in clean technologies, energy efficiency, and environmental sustainability. The Green Deal is not only an environmental strategy, but also an industrial strategy that seeks to make the EU the world leader in the green economy. The European Commission has proposed reducing greenhouse gas emissions by at least 55% by 2030, compared to 1990 levels, as an intermediate step toward climate neutrality in 2050.

The implementation of the European Green Deal requires a profound transformation of the European economy. The energy sector will have to transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources, such as wind, solar, and hydroelectric power. Transport will have to be electrified, with a massive increase in electric vehicles and the development of recharging infrastructure. Agriculture will have to adopt more sustainable practices, reducing the use of pesticides and chemical fertilizers, and promoting organic farming. Industry will have to invest in carbon capture and storage technologies, and in more efficient production processes. These transformations will have a significant impact on employment, innovation, and the competitiveness of the European economy.

The European Green Deal also has an important social dimension, as the green transition cannot leave anyone behind. The EU has created the Just Transition Mechanism, which provides financial and technical support to the regions and communities most affected by the transition from carbon-intensive industries to more sustainable economies. This mechanism is especially important for regions dependent on coal mining, heavy industry, and high energy-intensive sectors. The European Commission has also proposed the creation of a European climate bank, which will finance green investment projects throughout the EU. The Green Deal is one of the most ambitious initiatives in the history of the European Union, and its success will be decisive for the future of the continent and the planet.

The European Green Deal also has implications for the EU's external relations. The EU has used its influence to promote the fight against climate change in international organizations, and has established border adjustment mechanisms to protect its industries from unfair competition from countries with less stringent environmental standards. The Green Deal has also generated economic opportunities for European companies, which can develop clean technologies and export them to other markets. The implementation of the Green Deal is an enormous challenge, but also an opportunity for the EU to position itself as a leader in the green economy and contribute to environmental protection worldwide.

### European strategic autonomy

In recent years, the European Union has sought to develop greater strategic autonomy, that is, the capacity to act independently in critical areas such as defense, security, technology, and supply chains. This search has been driven by several factors: uncertainty about the United States' commitment to European defense, growing rivalry between the United States and China, Russian aggression against Ukraine, and the vulnerability exposed by the COVID-19 pandemic in supply chains. Strategic autonomy does not imply autarky, but the capacity to make independent decisions and to protect European interests and values in an increasingly competitive and conflictive world.

In the area of defense, the EU has made significant progress in military cooperation through the Permanent Structured Cooperation (PESCO), the European Defence Fund, and the Planning and Conduct of Civilian and Military Operations. These initiatives seek to improve European defense capacity, reduce duplication of efforts, and increase the efficiency of military spending. However, European defense remains dependent on NATO and, in particular, on the United States, which provides the majority of military resources and nuclear coverage. The war in Ukraine has reinforced the importance of defense cooperation, but has also highlighted the limitations of European strategic autonomy.

In the technological area, the EU has adopted strategies to reduce its dependence on foreign technologies, especially in areas such as artificial intelligence, semiconductors, 5G, and cybersecurity. The Digital Decade and the European Chips Act are examples of initiatives aimed at strengthening European technological sovereignty. The EU has also developed mechanisms to protect its strategic industries from foreign investments that could compromise national security. Strategic autonomy is a complex and evolving concept, which requires a balance between openness to the world and the protection of European interests. Its development will be one of the main challenges of the European Union in the coming decades.

Strategic autonomy also has a digital dimension, as the EU seeks to reduce its dependence on American and Chinese big tech companies. The Digital Markets Act and the Digital Services Act are examples of initiatives aimed at regulating digital markets and protecting European citizens from the abuses of digital platforms. The EU has also developed strategies to promote innovation in areas such as artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and biotechnology, seeking to create a competitive and autonomous European technology industry. Technological autonomy is an enormous challenge, but it is essential for European sovereignty in an increasingly digitized world.

## Chapter 7: The EU and international organizations

### The EU in the United Nations

The European Union plays an important role in the United Nations system, although its representation is complex due to its institutional structure. The EU Member States are individually represented in the General Assembly and the Security Council, but the European Commission and the European External Action Service (EEAS) coordinate the EU's position in many areas of international policy. The EU and its Member States are jointly responsible for approximately 30% of contributions to the UN budget and represent a significant portion of peacekeeping operations. The EU has also been an active defender of multilateralism and international law, opposing unilateral actions that undermine the international system.

In the Security Council, two EU Member States (France and Great Britain, until Brexit) have permanent seats with veto power. After Brexit, France is the only EU Member State with a permanent seat, reinforcing its role as a representative of European interests on the council. The EU has sought to reform the Security Council to make it more representative and effective, supporting the inclusion of new permanent and non-permanent members. The EU has also actively participated in international negotiations on issues such as climate change, human rights, sustainable development, and nuclear non-proliferation. Its influence in the UN is based on its experience in regional integration and its capacity to build consensus among countries with different interests.

The EU has also used its influence in the UN to promote values such as democracy, human rights, and the rule of law. It has supported the creation of international justice institutions, such as the International Criminal Court, and has promoted the adoption of international conventions on human rights and the environment. The EU has been a significant donor of development aid through its international cooperation programs, and has used its political influence to promote democratic governance and stability in developing regions of the world. Its role in the UN reflects its ambition to be a global actor capable of contributing to the solution of world problems.

The EU's participation in the United Nations also has limitations, as decision-making in the UN requires consensus among Member States, which can hinder the adoption of common positions. The EU has tried to overcome these limitations through the coordination of its Member States' positions in the UN, but the diversity of views among member countries often hinders the adoption of common positions. The EU has also used its economic influence to promote its interests in the UN, as it is the largest development aid donor in the world and has significant economic weight in international negotiations. Despite these limitations, the EU remains an important actor in the United Nations, and its participation is essential for the effectiveness of the international system.

### The EU and NATO

The relationship between the European Union and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) is one of the most important alliances in the international security architecture. Most EU Member States are also NATO members, creating significant overlap in the areas of defense and security. NATO has been the cornerstone of collective defense in Western Europe since the Cold War, and its role has been reinforced following the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022. The EU, for its part, has sought to develop its own defense capacity to complement NATO, rather than compete with it. Cooperation between the EU and NATO has been strengthened in areas such as cybersecurity, counterterrorism, and crisis management.

The relationship between the EU and NATO has not been without tensions. Some Member States, such as France, have defended the need for European strategic autonomy, while others, such as the countries of Eastern Europe, prefer to maintain a close link with the United States through NATO. The Turkey question, which is a NATO member but not an EU member, has generated additional tensions, especially in relation to Greece and Cyprus. Brexit has also affected the dynamics between the EU and NATO, as Great Britain was one of the main military contributors to European defense. Despite these tensions, cooperation between the EU and NATO is essential for the security of the continent, and both organizations have worked to improve their coordination and avoid duplication.

The Russian invasion of Ukraine has reinforced the importance of cooperation between the EU and NATO. The EU has imposed unprecedented sanctions on Russia and has provided military aid to Ukraine, while NATO has reinforced its presence in Eastern Europe and increased its defense spending. The war has demonstrated that Europe's security depends on a combination of military and diplomatic capabilities, and that cooperation between the EU and NATO is essential to address threats. The EU has also sought to expand its role in security through the creation of the European Peace Facility, which provides military aid to partner countries, and through the development of its own defense capabilities. The future of the EU-NATO relationship will be decisive for the security and stability of Europe and the world.

The relationship between the EU and NATO also has a technological dimension, as both organizations seek to improve their capabilities in areas such as cybersecurity, artificial intelligence, and defense technologies. The EU has created the European Centre of Excellence for Cybersecurity and has funded research projects in defense technologies. NATO, for its part, has established centres of excellence in areas such as cybersecurity and missile defense. Technological cooperation between the EU and NATO is essential to maintain Europe's technological advantage against emerging threats. The EU-NATO relationship is complex and multifaceted, but its importance for Europe's security is undeniable.

### The EU and the G7, G20, and other organizations

The European Union actively participates in international economic cooperation forums, such as the G7, the G20, and the World Trade Organization (WTO). In the G7, the EU is represented by the President of the European Council and the President of the Commission, along with the leaders of the United States, Japan, Canada, Great Britain, France, Germany, and Italy. The EU contributes a significant share of global GDP and international trade, giving it significant influence in global economic decisions. In the G20, which includes the largest emerging economies, the EU is represented by its main institutional leaders and defends positions on trade, climate change, sustainable development, and international financial regulation.

The EU's relationship with the WTO is especially important, as the EU is the world's largest trading bloc. The European Commission negotiates on behalf of Member States in multilateral trade negotiations, and has been an active defender of free trade and a rules-based trading system. The EU has participated in WTO negotiation rounds, including the Doha Round, and has sought to promote the liberalization of services trade, the protection of intellectual property, and the reduction of non-tariff barriers. However, WTO negotiations have faced significant obstacles, and the EU has sought alternatives through bilateral and regional free trade agreements with other countries and blocs.

The EU also maintains important relationships with regional organizations such as the African Union, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), and the Community of Latin American and Caribbean States (CELAC). These relationships are based on political, economic, and technical cooperation, and seek to promote stability, development, and democratic values in different regions of the world. The EU has established strategic partnerships with several countries and blocs, and has used its influence to promote the peaceful resolution of conflicts, respect for international law, and multilateral cooperation. Its role in international organizations reflects its ambition to be a responsible global actor committed to the common welfare of humanity.

The EU's participation in international forums also has limitations, as the diversity of views among Member States often hinders the adoption of common positions. The EU has tried to overcome these limitations through the coordination of its Member States' positions, but the complexity of the European decision-making process can hinder the speed and effectiveness of external action. The EU has also used its economic influence to promote its interests in international forums, as it is the world's largest single market and has significant economic weight in trade negotiations. Despite these limitations, the EU remains an important actor in international forums, and its participation is essential for global governance.

## Chapter 8: The European cultural dimension

Europe possesses an extraordinarily rich and diverse cultural heritage, the result of thousands of years of history, civilization, and human creativity. The protection of European cultural heritage is a priority for the EU, which has adopted rules to safeguard cultural property, promote restoration, and prevent the illegal trafficking of objects of historical and artistic value. The Cultural Goods Directive establishes common rules for the export and trade of cultural goods, protecting the heritage of Member States. The EU has also funded restoration projects for historical monuments, such as the Notre-Dame Cathedral in Paris and the historic center of Rome. Cultural heritage is, ultimately, an asset that enriches citizens' lives and contributes to the economic development of the EU.

Multilingualism is promoted by the EU as an essential skill for mobility, employment, and social participation. Linguistic diversity is also promoted in the cultural sphere through literature, cinema, music, and other forms of artistic expression in different languages. ## Chapter 11: The fundamental treaties of the European Union

### The Treaty of Paris (1951)

The Treaty of Paris, signed on April 18, 1951, was the foundational document of modern European integration. This treaty established the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC), a supranational community that placed the strategic sectors of the heavy industry of six countries under a common authority: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. The treaty was the result of the proposal of the French Minister Robert Schuman, who on May 9, 1950, presented a plan to place coal and steel production under a common authority, with the objective of making a new Franco-German conflict materially impossible. The treaty entered into force on July 23, 1952, for a period of 50 years, and its success was decisive for the decision to extend integration to other economic sectors.

The Treaty of Paris established an innovative institutional structure that served as a model for future European communities. The treaty created a supranational high authority, composed of representatives appointed by the governments of the six Member States, which had the power to make binding decisions on the production and distribution of coal and steel. The treaty also created a council of ministers, a parliamentary assembly, and a court of justice, laying the foundations of the institutional framework that would be adopted by the EEC in 1957. The high authority was chaired by Jean Monnet, who played a decisive role in the success of the community, demonstrating that supranational integration was viable and beneficial for the Member States.

The success of the ECSC was due to several decisive factors. First, the choice of coal and steel as pilot sectors was correct, as they were the foundation of heavy industry and war machinery, so their pooling had enormous symbolic and political value. Second, the high authority had real powers to make decisions, which allowed it to act effectively without depending on the will of national governments. Third, the economic benefits of integration were rapid and visible, which generated popular and political support for the project. The ECSC laid the institutional, legal, and political foundations for subsequent European construction, and its success was decisive for the decision to extend integration to other economic sectors.

The Treaty of Paris also had a significant impact on international relations. The ECSC was received with enthusiasm by the United States, which saw European integration as a means to contain Soviet expansion and stabilize Western Europe. The ECSC was also seen as a model of supranational cooperation that could be replicated in other regions of the world. However, the ECSC faced resistance in some countries, such as Great Britain, which preferred to maintain its national sovereignty and not join the community. Despite these resistances, the ECSC was a success that laid the foundations for subsequent European construction.

### The Treaty of Rome (1957)

The Treaty of Rome, signed on March 25, 1957, was the document that established the European Economic Community (EEC) and the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom). The Treaty of Rome was a much more ambitious document than that of Paris, as it was not limited to a single economic sector, but sought to create a complete common market among the six Member States. The objective was to progressively eliminate obstacles to the trade of goods, services, capital, and people within the community, establishing a customs union with a common tariff vis-a-vis the outside. This treaty entered into force on January 1, 1958, inaugurating a new stage in European integration that radically transformed the economy and society of the continent.

The Treaty of Rome established the fundamental principles that guided European economic construction for decades. Article 2 defined the objective of promoting a harmonious development of economic activities, a continuous and balanced expansion, an increase in stability, a convergence of the policies of the Member States, and an improvement in the standard of living. To achieve these objectives, the treaty provided for the gradual elimination of customs duties between Member States, the adoption of a common tariff vis-a-vis third countries, the free movement of people, services, and capital, and the harmonization of economic policies. The treaty also established the principle of community loyalty, which obliged Member States not to adopt measures that could jeopardize the achievement of common objectives.

The Treaty of Rome also created the common agricultural policy (CAP), which was one of the most important and costly policies of the EEC. The CAP sought to guarantee Europe's food security, stabilize agricultural prices, ensure an equitable standard of living for the agricultural population, and guarantee the availability of supplies at reasonable prices. The policy was based on a minimum price system that guaranteed the profitability of European farmers, and a system of import duties that protected the internal market from foreign competition. The CAP was especially important for countries like France, Italy, and Belgium, which had large and politically influential agricultural sectors. Over the decades, the CAP has been reformed several times to adapt to new economic realities and to reduce its cost, but it remains one of the most important policies of the European Union.

The Treaty of Rome also established a common tariff system that protected European agriculture through a dual pricing mechanism. The common agricultural policy was designed to ensure that European farmers could compete with foreign imports, while maintaining stable prices for consumers. This policy was fundamental to the modernization of European agriculture, as it allowed investment in machinery, fertilizers, and more advanced cultivation techniques. The CAP also contributed to rural development in Europe, as the funds allocated to agriculture were used to improve rural infrastructure, agricultural training, and environmental protection. The Treaty of Rome laid the foundations for Europe's economic prosperity and for the construction of a common market that benefited millions of citizens.

### The Single European Act (1986)

The Single European Act (SEA), signed in 1986 and in force since 1987, was a treaty that reformed the Treaties of Rome with the objective of completing the internal market before 1992. The SEA was the result of a decade of reflections on the obstacles that prevented the full realization of the common market, such as non-tariff barriers, differences in technical standards, and administrative regulations. The treaty expanded the EEC's competences in areas such as research policy, environmental policy, and social policy, and reinforced the powers of the European Parliament, which moved from having purely consultative functions to having a co-decision role in some legislative areas.

The SEA established the objective of completing the internal market before 1992, through the elimination of fiscal, technical, and physical barriers to trade. To achieve this objective, the treaty provided for the adoption of measures to harmonize technical standards, the mutualization of public procurement procedures, and the mutual recognition of national standards. The SEA also established the principle that a product legally manufactured in one Member State could be sold freely in any other Member State, without having to adapt to the national standards of the destination country. This principle of mutual recognition was fundamental to the creation of the single market and the free movement of goods.

The SEA also had a significant impact on the EEC's foreign and defense policy. The treaty recognized the importance of cooperation in matters of foreign policy and established the mandate of the Common Foreign Policy (CFP), which sought to coordinate the positions of Member States on international issues. The SEA also established cooperation in matters of security policy, creating the European Political Cooperation (EPC) as a forum for cooperation among foreign ministers. These provisions laid the foundations for the Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP) that would be created by the Maastricht Treaty in 1992.

The Single European Act was a decisive step toward the creation of the single market, which was completed in 1993 with the elimination of non-tariff barriers. The SEA demonstrated that European integration could advance through concrete and pragmatic reforms, and that the positive results of integration could be rapid and visible. The treaty also reinforced the democratization of the EU, by granting new powers to the European Parliament, which moved to having a more important role in the legislative process. The SEA was a model of how cooperation between Member States and European institutions could produce significant reforms that improved citizens' lives.

### The Maastricht Treaty (1992)

The Maastricht Treaty, signed in 1992 and in force since 1993, was one of the most important treaties in the history of the European Union, as it transformed the European Economic Community into the European Union. The treaty expanded the EU's competences in areas such as foreign and security policy, justice and home affairs, culture, and education, and established the creation of a single currency, the euro. Maastricht also reinforced the democratization of the EU, by expanding the powers of the European Parliament and establishing the principle of subsidiarity, which states that the EU should only act when the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States.

The Maastricht Treaty established the convergence criteria that Member States had to meet to join the euro area: low inflation, stable exchange rates, sound public finances (deficit below 3% of GDP and debt below 60% of GDP), and stable long-term interest rates. These criteria were designed to ensure that countries joining the euro area had economies sufficiently similar to function under a single monetary policy. The treaty also created the European Monetary Institute, which was responsible for preparing the transition to the euro and supervising the monetary policy of candidate Member States.

Maastricht also established European citizenship, which grants citizens of Member States rights and obligations in the EU. European citizenship includes the right to reside and work in any Member State, the right to vote and stand for election in European and municipal elections in the country of residence, the right to consular protection from the embassy of any Member State in a third country, and the right to contact the European Parliament and the European Ombudsman. European citizenship is complementary to national citizenship and does not replace it, reflecting the character of the EU as an organization of sovereign states.

The Maastricht Treaty had a significant impact on European politics. The treaty was ratified by referendum in several countries, and in France and Denmark the results were narrow, reflecting citizens' concerns about the loss of national sovereignty. These concerns generated a debate about the democratization of the EU and the need to improve communication between European institutions and citizens. Maastricht was also criticized by some economists, who argued that the creation of the euro area without a complete fiscal union would generate economic governance problems. These criticisms proved prophetic during the euro crisis of 2010, but the treaty laid the foundations for the single currency and for the economic and monetary union.

### The Treaty of Amsterdam (1997)

The Treaty of Amsterdam, signed in 1997 and in force since 1999, reformed the Maastricht Treaty with the objective of strengthening European institutions and preparing the eastern enlargement. The treaty expanded the powers of the European Parliament, granting it a co-decision role in new legislative areas, and reinforced the role of the European Ombudsman. Amsterdam also incorporated the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU into European law, establishing that fundamental rights are general principles of law that European institutions and Member States must respect.

The Treaty of Amsterdam also reformed the common foreign and security policy, creating the office of High Representative for Common Foreign and Security Policy, which coordinates the EU's foreign policy and represents the institution externally. The treaty also reinforced cooperation in matters of justice and home affairs, establishing the Area of Freedom, Security, and Justice, which seeks to ensure that citizens can move freely in the EU without losing the protection of their rights. Amsterdam also introduced improvements in economic governance, establishing a clearer mandate for the European Central Bank and reinforcing the rules of the Stability and Growth Pact.

The Treaty of Amsterdam was the first treaty to significantly reform a previous treaty, establishing a precedent for the reform of European treaties. The reform process was complex and required prolonged negotiations among Member States, as each country sought to protect its interests. The result was a treaty that balanced the need to reform institutions with respect for the sovereignty of Member States. Amsterdam was also a treaty that took into account citizens' concerns, as it incorporated the Charter of Fundamental Rights and reinforced the democratization of the EU. The treaty laid the foundations for the subsequent development of the EU and its expansion to Eastern Europe.

### The Treaty of Nice (2001)

The Treaty of Nice, signed in 2001 and in force since 2003, was a treaty that reformed the Treaty of Amsterdam with the objective of preparing the European Union for the eastern enlargement. The treaty reorganized European institutions to ensure their effective functioning with a larger number of Member States, including the reduction of the number of MEPs, the reorganization of voting in the Council of the EU, and the expansion of areas of qualified majority voting. Nice also reinforced the Common Foreign and Security Policy, establishing a more coherent structure for decision-making in matters of foreign policy.

The Treaty of Nice had to face the challenge of adapting European institutions to a larger number of members. The eastern enlargement, which took place in 2004 with the inclusion of ten new Member States, required reorganizing institutions to ensure that all Member States had equitable representation. Nice established a system of weighted votes in the Council of the EU, which assigned a number of votes to each Member State based on its population. The treaty also reorganized the European Parliament, reducing the number of MEPs per country to compensate for the inclusion of new members.

The Treaty of Nice was also criticized by some, who argued that it did not go far enough in reforming European institutions. The French referendum, which took place in 2000, approved the treaty by a narrow margin, reflecting citizens' concerns about the sufficiency of the reforms. French President Jacques Chirac responded to these concerns by proposing the creation of a European Convention, which would examine the need for additional reforms. This convention led to the European Constitutional Treaty, which was rejected in referendums in France and the Netherlands in 2005, and was subsequently replaced by the Treaty of Lisbon in 2007.

### The Treaty of Lisbon (2007)

The Treaty of Lisbon, signed in 2007 and in force since 2009, was the treaty that replaced the European Constitutional Treaty, which was rejected in referendums in France and the Netherlands in 2005. Lisbon maintained the substantive content of the Constitutional Treaty, but eliminated its constitutional format and its symbolism, adopting the form of an amendment to the existing treaties. The treaty strengthened European institutions, including the creation of the position of permanent President of the European Council, the expansion of the powers of the European Parliament, and the creation of the European External Action Service.

The Treaty of Lisbon significantly expanded the powers of the European Parliament, granting it a co-decision role in new legislative areas, such as the common agricultural policy, transport policy, and justice and home affairs policy. Lisbon also reinforced the democratization of the EU, establishing that the European Council must take into account the results of European elections when proposing a candidate for the presidency of the European Commission. The treaty also introduced the principle of subsidiarity, which states that the EU should only act when the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States.

The Treaty of Lisbon also created the European External Action Service (EEAS), which is the EU's diplomatic service and provides technical and political assistance to the High Representative for Common Foreign and Security Policy. The EEAS is composed of officials from the Commission and national governments, and has representations around the world. The EEAS has improved the coordination of the EU's foreign policy and has provided the institution with a more effective diplomatic capacity. Lisbon also reinforced the Common Foreign and Security Policy, establishing the European Peace Facility, which provides military aid to partner countries.

The Treaty of Lisbon was a milestone in the history of European integration, as it significantly strengthened European institutions and prepared the European Union for enlargement and the new challenges of the 21st century. The treaty was ratified by all Member States, demonstrating European leaders' commitment to integration. Lisbon also laid the foundations for the development of new European policies, such as defense policy, digital policy, and climate policy, which are essential for the future of the EU. The Treaty of Lisbon is, ultimately, a treaty that has transformed the EU and prepared the institution for the challenges of the 21st century.

### The Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union

The Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) is one of the two fundamental treaties of the European Union, together with the Treaty on European Union (TEU). The TFEU was created by the Treaty of Lisbon in 2007, and is based on the Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe, which was rejected in a referendum in 2005. The TFEU regulates the functioning of the European Union and establishes the competences, procedures, and institutions of the EU. The treaty is composed of a preamble, 358 articles, and 36 protocols, and is the legal framework that regulates all EU activities.

The TFEU establishes the competences of the European Union, which are divided into three categories: exclusive competences, shared competences, and supporting competences. Exclusive competences are areas where the EU has the exclusive power to legislate, such as the customs union, the common commercial policy, and competition policy. Shared competences are areas where the EU and the Member States can legislate, but where European legislation prevails over national legislation, such as environmental policy, transport policy, and energy policy. Supporting competences are areas where the EU can complement the actions of the Member States, such as education, culture, and health protection.

The TFEU also establishes the EU's legislative procedures, including the ordinary legislative procedure, the cooperation procedure, and the consent procedure. The ordinary legislative procedure is the most widely used, and establishes that legislative proposals must be jointly approved by the European Parliament and the Council of the EU. The cooperation procedure is used in areas where the Council has a particular influence, such as fiscal policy and competition policy. The consent procedure is used in areas where the Parliament must give its consent, such as the approval of international agreements and the admission of new Member States. The TFEU is the legal framework that regulates the functioning of the EU, and its application is essential for the effectiveness of European integration.

## Chapter 13: Economic integration and the single market

### The creation of the common market

The creation of the common market was one of the fundamental objectives of the European Economic Community from its founding in 1957. The common market sought to eliminate barriers to the trade of goods, services, capital, and people among Member States, creating an integrated economic space where products, services, people, and capital could circulate freely. The elimination of customs tariffs among Member States was completed in 1968, with the creation of the customs union, which established a common tariff vis-a-vis third countries. This common tariff protected European industry from foreign competition while facilitating trade among Member States.

The common market was based on several fundamental principles. The first was the free movement of goods, which allowed products manufactured in one Member State to be sold freely in any other Member State, without having to adapt to the national standards of the destination country. The second principle was the free movement of services, which allowed service providers from one Member State to provide services in any other Member State. The third principle was the free movement of capital, which allowed investors to invest freely in any Member State. The fourth principle was the free movement of people, which allowed citizens of the Member States to reside and work freely in any other Member State.

The creation of the common market had a significant impact on the European economy. The elimination of trade barriers stimulated competition, which allowed prices to be reduced and the quality of products and services to be improved. The expansion of the market allowed European companies to grow and achieve economies of scale, improving their competitiveness worldwide. The free movement of people allowed workers to seek employment in other European countries, reducing unemployment and improving working conditions. The common market was, ultimately, the engine of Europe's economic growth during the 1960s and 1970s.

### Non-tariff barriers

Although customs tariffs were eliminated among Member States in 1968, many non-tariff barriers continued to hinder trade within the common market. Non-tariff barriers included differences in technical standards, homologation requirements, administrative regulations, and differences in quality standards. These barriers created additional costs for companies that wanted to sell their products in other Member States, as they had to adapt their products to the national standards of each country. Non-tariff barriers were one of the main obstacles to the full realization of the common market, and their elimination was one of the main objectives of the Single European Act of 1986.

The Single European Act established the objective of completing the internal market before 1992, through the elimination of non-tariff barriers. To achieve this objective, the treaty provided for the adoption of measures to harmonize technical standards, the mutualization of public procurement procedures, and the mutual recognition of national standards. The principle of mutual recognition established that a product legally manufactured in one Member State could be sold freely in any other Member State, without having to adapt to the national standards of the destination country. This principle was fundamental to the creation of the single market and the free movement of goods.

The elimination of non-tariff barriers was a complex and prolonged process, which required cooperation among national governments, European institutions, and economic sectors. The European Commission played an important role, as it proposed the necessary measures to harmonize technical standards and eliminate administrative barriers. National governments had to adapt their national legislation to European standards, which sometimes generated resistance from affected sectors. Despite these difficulties, the elimination of non-tariff barriers was completed in 1993, with the creation of the single market, which allowed the free movement of goods, services, capital, and people throughout the EU.

### The single market and services

The single market in services was one of the areas where integration was slowest and most difficult. The Services Directive, adopted in 2006, was an important step toward the liberalization of the services market, as it established common rules for the provision of cross-border services. The European Commission has proposed additional measures to complete the services market, including the elimination of cross-border barriers and the harmonization of quality standards.

The single market in services has also generated challenges in areas such as consumer protection, equal treatment, and labor rights. The free movement of services can generate unfair competition among companies from different countries, as companies from countries with less strict standards can offer services at lower prices. The European Commission has adopted measures to protect consumers and workers, including quality standards, labor protections, and dispute resolution mechanisms. The single market in services is an area in constant evolution, and the European Commission continues to work to complete liberalization and to ensure that the benefits of the single market are distributed equitably among citizens and companies.

## Chapter 14: Political integration and European democracy

### The evolution of the European Parliament

The European Parliament has undergone a significant evolution since its creation as the European Assembly in 1952. Initially, the assembly was composed of delegates designated by national parliaments, and had purely consultative functions. With the Single European Act of 1986, the Parliament acquired a co-decision role in some legislative areas, and with the Maastricht Treaty in 1992, its powers were significantly expanded. The Treaty of Lisbon in 2009 granted the Parliament a co-decision role in the majority of legislative areas, making it a co-legislative institution with the Council of the EU. This evolution reflects the recognition that the Parliament, as a body directly elected by citizens, must have an important role in all policy areas that affect European citizens.

The first direct election of the European Parliament took place in 1979, and since then European elections have been held every five years. European elections are the largest transnational elections in the world, in which more than 400 million eligible citizens from the 27 Member States participate. European elections are an important moment in the democratic life of the EU, as they allow citizens to express their political preferences and influence the orientation of European policy. However, participation in European elections has fluctuated significantly, from 62% in 1979 to 50.6% in 2019, when a significant increase occurred driven by concerns about climate change, immigration, and the future of the EU.

The European Parliament is organized in transnational political groups, which are groupings of MEPs from different countries who share political affinities. Political groups are fundamental to the functioning of Parliament, as they determine the distribution of committee positions, the election of the president, and legislative priorities. Currently there are seven political groups in Parliament, ranging from the extreme left to the extreme right. Political groups facilitate the search for consensus among MEPs from different countries, and their political diversity is an asset for the democratization of Parliament.

### The relationship between the Parliament and the European Commission

The relationship between the European Parliament and the European Commission is one of the most important in the EU's institutional system. This investiture power gives the Parliament significant influence over the composition and priorities of the Commission. The Parliament supervises the activity of the European Commission through various mechanisms, including parliamentary questions, motions for resolutions, and public hearings. MEPs can put written and oral questions to Commissioners, and the Commission is obliged to respond within a specified period. The Parliament can also adopt non-binding resolutions on current issues, and the Commission is obliged to respond to these resolutions. Public hearings allow MEPs to question Commissioners on their activities and policies, and are an important instrument of democratic control.

The relationship between the Parliament and the Commission is also based on cooperation in the legislative process, as the Commission presents legislative proposals that are examined by the Parliament. The Commission may withdraw or modify its proposals in response to Parliament's concerns, and the Parliament may propose amendments that the Commission may accept or reject. This working relationship is essential for the quality of European legislation, as it allows the technical expertise of the Commission to be combined with the democratic legitimacy of the Parliament.

### The democratization of European decision-making

The democratization of European decision-making has been a constant concern since the earliest years of integration. The Treaties of Rome established a decision-making system in which the European Commission, as a supranational body, had the monopoly of the legislative initiative, while the Council of Ministers, composed of representatives of national governments, was the main decision-making body. The European Parliament had limited functions, which generated criticism about the lack of democratization of integration. Over the decades, successive treaties have reinforced the powers of the European Parliament, making it a co-legislative institution with the Council of the EU.

The principle of subsidiarity, established by the Maastricht Treaty and reinforced by the Treaty of Lisbon, has also contributed to the democratization of decision-making. The principle of subsidiarity establishes that the EU should only act when the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, ensuring that decisions are taken at the level closest to citizens. National parliaments have a role in monitoring subsidiarity, as they can examine the European Commission's legislative proposals and issue reasoned opinions if they consider that a proposal violates the principle of subsidiarity.

The democratization of decision-making also requires transparency and accountability of European institutions. The European Commission has implemented various measures to improve transparency, including the publication of preparatory documents, the conduct of public consultations, and the creation of the transparency register. The European Parliament has also improved the transparency of its activities, publishing voting records and MEPs' interventions in debates. The democratization of decision-making is a continuous process that requires the commitment of all European institutions and citizens.

### European political culture

European political culture is a developing phenomenon that reflects the growing political integration of the continent. Although most European citizens primarily identify with their country, there is also a growing sense of European identity, especially among young people. Eurobarometer studies show that a significant proportion of European citizens feel "European citizens" in addition to being citizens of their country, and that many citizens support political integration in areas such as defense, foreign policy, and the economy. This sense of European identity is essential for the sustainability of the European project, as political integration requires the support of citizens.

European political culture has developed through various instruments. Mobility programs, such as Erasmus+, have allowed millions of young people to study, work, and live in other European countries, creating a generation of citizens who feel part of a broader community. European media, such as Euronews, have contributed to creating a European public space, where citizens can be informed about the issues that affect them. European political parties have also contributed to the creation of a European political culture, by presenting candidates in European elections and debating European policies.

European political culture also has limitations, as most European citizens continue to inform themselves and participate in politics at the national level. National media remain the main source of political information for most citizens, and national elections remain the most important for most voters. The European Commission has tried to improve communication with citizens through information campaigns, citizen dialogues, and the promotion of democratic participation. The creation of a European political culture is a challenge that requires a continuous effort by European institutions and citizens.

## Chapter 15: The European Union in the 21st century

### The challenges of the 21st century

The European Union faces numerous challenges in the 21st century that will test its capacity for adaptation and internal cohesion. Climate change is one of the most urgent challenges, as Europe is one of the regions most vulnerable to its effects, such as rising sea levels, droughts, and floods. The European Commission has adopted the European Green Deal as its roadmap for achieving climate neutrality by 2050, but its implementation will require massive investments and profound transformations in all sectors of the economy. The energy transition is another important challenge, as the EU must reduce its dependence on fossil fuels and increase the use of renewable energies.

Digital transformation is another important challenge for the EU. Artificial intelligence, automation, and digitization are transforming the economy and society, and the EU must ensure that this transformation benefits all citizens. The European Commission has adopted strategies to promote digital innovation, such as the Digital Decade and the European Chips Act, but the EU faces competition from the United States and China in the technological area. The protection of personal data and cybersecurity are other important challenges, as digitization has created new risks for citizens' privacy and security.

Migration and cultural diversity are other challenges that the EU must face. Immigration from other continents has created more diverse and cosmopolitan societies, but has also generated social and political tensions. The EU must find a balance between openness to immigration and the protection of citizens' security, and must promote the social inclusion of immigrants and their descendants. Cultural diversity is an asset for the EU, as it enriches society and fosters creativity, but it also requires policies that promote mutual understanding and social cohesion.

### The future of European integration

The future of European integration is a subject of debate among politicians, academics, and citizens. Some defend a deeper union, with greater political, economic, and defense integration, while others prefer to maintain an intergovernmental cooperation model in which Member States retain their sovereignty. The European Commission has proposed various visions for the future of the EU, including the creation of a social Europe, a green Europe, and a digital Europe. These visions reflect the need to adapt European integration to the new challenges of the 21st century and to ensure that the EU remains relevant to citizens.

The enlargement of the EU is another important topic for the future of integration. Several Western Balkan and Eastern European countries are in the process of accession, and their inclusion will require significant reforms both in the candidate countries and in the EU itself. Enlargement will also require the adaptation of European institutions to ensure their effective functioning with a larger number of members. Enlargement is a complex and prolonged process, but it is essential for Europe's stability and prosperity.

The EU's relationship with the world is also an important topic for the future of integration. The EU must define its role in an increasingly multipolar world, where the United States, China, and Russia compete for influence. The EU must develop greater strategic autonomy, especially in the areas of defense, security, and technology, to be able to act independently on the international stage. The EU must also maintain its commitments to multilateralism and international law, and must use its influence to promote peace, democracy, and human rights in the world.

### The European vision for the future

The European vision for the future is based on several fundamental principles. First, the protection of fundamental rights and democracy, which are the values on which the EU was built and which are essential for its legitimacy. Second, solidarity among Member States and among citizens, which is the principle that ensures that integration benefits everyone. Third, innovation and sustainable development, which are essential for economic prosperity and environmental protection. Fourth, openness to the world and international cooperation, which are essential for international peace and stability.

The European Commission has proposed various strategies to realize this vision, including the European Green Deal, the Digital Europe Strategy, and the European Agenda on Migration. These strategies seek to address the most urgent challenges facing the EU, such as climate change, digital transformation, and migration, and seek to ensure that European integration remains relevant to citizens. The implementation of these strategies will require cooperation among European institutions, national governments, and citizens, and will require significant investments and profound transformations in the economy and society.

The future of the European Union depends, ultimately, on the willingness of citizens and political leaders to maintain and deepen integration. The EU has demonstrated its capacity to adapt to historical changes, from the postwar period to globalization, and has demonstrated that cooperation among European peoples can produce tangible benefits for citizens. The future of the EU will be determined by the institution's capacity to respond to the challenges of the 21st century and to maintain citizens' trust and support. The history of European integration is a history of progress and hope, and its future will depend on the capacity of Europeans to build together a more prosperous, more just, and more sustainable future.

## Chapter 16: The European economy in the global context

### The economic role of the EU in the world

The European Union is one of the world's largest economies, with a GDP representing approximately 15% of global GDP. The EU is the world's largest trading bloc and the largest donor of development aid. The European economy is based on a social market economy model, which combines free enterprise with social protection and state regulation. This model has allowed Europe to achieve a high standard of living, with quality public services such as education, health care, and social protection. The European social model is an asset that distinguishes Europe from other economic models, such as the American or Chinese model.

The EU is the largest trading partner of many countries in the world, and its trade policy is an important instrument of its economic influence. The European Commission negotiates trade agreements on behalf of the EU, and has signed agreements with countries and regional blocs around the world, such as Canada, Japan, South Korea, and the Mercosur countries. These agreements seek to open new markets for European companies, promote free trade, and establish fair trade rules. The EU's trade policy also includes measures to protect European industries from unfair competition, such as anti-dumping duties and trade defense measures.

The EU also plays an important role in global economic governance. The EU is a member of the G7, the G20, and the World Trade Organization, and actively participates in international negotiations on economic issues. The EU has been an active defender of multilateralism and free trade, and has used its influence to promote fair trade rules and combat protectionism. The European economy is an engine of global economic growth, and its stability is essential for the world economy.
