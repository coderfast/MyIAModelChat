Energy is a fundamental physical quantity defined as the capacity of a system to perform work. Energy is neither created nor destroyed, only transformed from one form to another. This principle, known as the conservation of energy, is one of the most fundamental in physics and applies to all natural phenomena. Energy can be transferred from one system to another through work or heat. The concept of energy is so fundamental that there is no single and complete definition that encompasses all its manifestations; however, all forms of energy share the common property of being able to be transformed into other forms and measured in the same units.

The understanding of these transformations is essential for designing efficient energy systems and for solving engineering and science problems.

The concept of energy has evolved significantly throughout the history of science. The ancient Greeks used the word "energeia" to describe activity or force, but they did not have a quantitative concept of energy. In the 17th century, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz introduced the concept of "vis viva" (living force), which he defined as the product of mass times the square of velocity (mv^2). In the 18th century, European scientists debated the nature of heat and its relationship to mechanical work. Some, like Count Rumford and Humphry Davy, argued that heat was a form of motion, while others, like Antoine Lavoisier, believed it was a substance called "caloric." This debate was not resolved until the investigations of James Prescott Joule in the 1840s.

James Prescott Joule (1818-1889) was a British brewer and physicist who conducted precise experiments to determine the mechanical equivalence of heat. Using a friction apparatus consisting of weights that spun paddles in a container with water, Joule demonstrated that mechanical work could be transformed into heat in a predictable manner. His experiments showed that a fixed amount of mechanical work always produced the same amount of heat, establishing the equivalence between the units of work and heat. This discovery was fundamental to the development of thermodynamics and the modern concept of energy. The unit of energy, the joule, was named in his honor in 1889, and his work laid the foundations for understanding the energy transformations that govern the universe.

Hermann von Helmholtz (1821-1894), a German physician and physicist, published in 1847 his work "Über die Erhaltung der Kraft" (On the Conservation of Force), in which he formulated the principle of conservation of energy. Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion. It is defined as half the product of the object's mass times the square of its velocity: KE = 1/2 m v^2. Kinetic energy is always positive (or zero for an object at rest) and depends on the square of the velocity, which means that doubling the velocity quadruples the kinetic energy. Kinetic energy is a scalar quantity with units of joules (J). The kinetic energy of an object depends on the reference frame in which it is measured, since velocity is relative. For an object in uniform rectilinear motion, kinetic energy is constant if no external forces act on it. Kinetic energy is one of the two main forms of mechanical energy, along with potential energy, and its study is fundamental to understanding the motion of objects.

Kinetic energy can be transformed into other forms of energy and vice versa. When an object falls freely, its gravitational potential energy is converted into kinetic energy. When an object goes up a ramp, its kinetic energy is converted into potential energy. When an object decelerates due to friction, its kinetic energy is converted into heat. These transformations are reversible in principle, although in practice there are always losses in the form of irreversible heat. The principle of conservation of energy states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant, although it may be transformed between different forms. In an ideal mechanical system without friction, the sum of kinetic and potential energy is conserved: KE + PE = constant. This principle is a powerful tool for solving mechanics problems without needing to calculate forces and accelerations explicitly.

Thermal energy is governed by the laws of thermodynamics. The first law states that energy is conserved: heat added to a system is transformed into work or an increase in internal energy. Nuclear fission is the process of splitting a heavy atomic nucleus into two or more lighter nuclei, releasing an enormous amount of energy. The process was discovered in 1938 by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, and theoretically explained by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch. When a neutron impacts a uranium-235 nucleus, the nucleus splits into two fragments of similar mass, emitting 2-3 additional neutrons and an energy of approximately 200 MeV per fission. This energy comes from the mass difference between the original nucleus and the fission products, according to Einstein's equation E = mc^2. The total mass of the products is slightly less than the mass of the original nucleus and incident neutrons, and this mass difference is converted into kinetic energy of the fragments, energy of the neutrons, and gamma radiation. The chain reaction occurs when neutrons emitted by one fission cause other fissions, creating a self-sustaining process that can be controlled in a nuclear reactor or uncontrolled in an atomic bomb.

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that studies the relationships between heat, work, temperature, and energy. The first law of thermodynamics states that the total energy of an isolated system is conserved, and that the heat supplied to a system equals the sum of the system's internal energy and the work done by the system: Q = ΔU + W. Entropy is a measure of the disorder or energy dispersion of a system, and its increase imposes a direction on natural processes, such as the diffusion of heat from a hot object to a cold one. The third law states that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero.
