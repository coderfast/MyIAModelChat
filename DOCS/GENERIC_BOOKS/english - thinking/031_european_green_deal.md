# The European Green Deal

## Chapter 1: What is the European Green Deal

### 1.1 Origin and Historical Context of the Green Deal

The European Green Deal constitutes one of the most ambitious and transformative initiatives ever undertaken by the European Union in its history of more than six decades of existence as a project of political and economic integration. Officially presented on December 11, 2019 by the European Commission under the presidency of Ursula von der Leyen, this ambitious action plan was conceived as a comprehensive roadmap to make the European Union economy sustainable and achieve climate neutrality by 2050. The Green Deal is not simply another environmental program, but represents a new model of economic growth that seeks to decouple economic progress from the depletion of natural resources, while ensuring a just transition for all regions and citizens of the Union. The context in which this plan emerges is fundamental to understanding its importance: the effects of climate change are becoming increasingly evident across the European continent, with extreme weather phenomena such as unprecedented heatwaves, prolonged droughts affecting Mediterranean agriculture, devastating floods in regions such as Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands, and the constant rise in average annual temperatures severely affecting ecosystems, agriculture and cities across the continent. Furthermore, the European Union has been a world leader in environmental policy for decades, having pioneered the implementation of emissions trading systems, the regulation of hazardous chemicals and the promotion of renewable energy through various directives and regulations. The Green Deal therefore represents the logical culmination of decades of environmental work, but with an unprecedented ambition and scale that aims to fundamentally transform the way energy is produced, consumed and distributed across the continent. ### 1.5 The Green Deal and Post-COVID Recovery

The COVID-19 pandemic, which devastated Europe and the world from early 2020, initially raised concerns about a possible setback in the European Union's climate ambitions, as governments focused on managing the health crisis and protecting their economies. However, paradoxically, the pandemic became a catalyst for more ambitious climate action, as the European Union explicitly integrated Green Deal objectives into its economic recovery plans. Next Generation EU, the 806.9 billion euro economic recovery instrument adopted by the European Council in July 2020, was designed to help European economies recover from the pandemic while accelerating the ecological and digital transition. 37 percent of Next Generation EU funds were allocated to ecological transition measures, and 20 percent to digital transformation, representing an unprecedented integration of sustainability objectives into economic recovery plans. The Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF), which is the centerpiece of Next Generation EU, requires that national recovery and resilience plans include at least 37 percent of expenditure related to climate and at least 20 percent related to digital transformation. This climate conditionality ensured that the billions of euros allocated to economic recovery would actively contribute to the ecological transition rather than perpetuating unsustainable economic models. The national recovery and resilience plans of the Member States included investments in renewable energy, building energy efficiency, sustainable transport, waste management, biodiversity protection and climate change adaptation. ## Chapter 2: Climate Neutrality Objectives

### 2.1 Climate Neutrality as a Legally Binding Objective

Climate neutrality, understood as the situation in which net greenhouse gas emissions are equal to zero, has become the fundamental strategic objective of the European Union's climate policy for 2050. This ambitious objective implies that, by that date, any residual greenhouse gas emissions that cannot be eliminated will have to be offset through carbon capture and storage or through natural carbon sinks such as forests, wetlands and agricultural soils that absorb CO2 from the atmosphere. The decision to set this objective was adopted by the European Council in December 2019, at the same time as the presentation of the Green Deal, and was subsequently reinforced with the adoption of the European Climate Law in June 2021, which establishes climate neutrality as a legally binding objective and includes a periodic assessment mechanism to ensure that the European Union and its Member States are on track to achieve this objective. The European Climate Law establishes that the European Commission must periodically assess the sufficiency of the measures adopted to achieve climate objectives and, if necessary, propose new additional measures. To achieve climate neutrality, the European Union has established an emissions reduction pathway that includes intermediate targets: a 55 percent reduction by 2030, a 90 percent reduction by 2040 (according to the European Commission's recommendation of February 2024) and climate neutrality by 2050. The importance of this objective lies in the fact that climate change is considered the greatest threat facing humanity in the 21st century, with potentially catastrophic consequences for ecosystems, the economy and society as a whole. ### 2.2 Priority Sectors for Emissions Reduction

The reduction of greenhouse gas emissions in the European Union focuses on several priority sectors that are the main contributors to current emissions and require significant structural transformations. The energy sector is by far the largest emitter of greenhouse gases in the European Union, responsible for approximately 75 percent of total emissions when all energy activities are considered. The decarbonization of the energy sector is therefore an absolute priority of the Green Deal, and is sought to be achieved through the progressive replacement of fossil fuels with renewable energy sources such as wind, solar, hydroelectric, geothermal and sustainable biomass, as well as through improvements in energy efficiency across all sectors of the economy and the modernization of electricity grids to accommodate distributed and intermittent renewable energy generation. The transport sector is the second largest emitter of greenhouse gases in the European Union, representing approximately 25 percent of total emissions, and is one of the most difficult sectors to decarbonize due to its fragmentation, its dependence on fossil fuels and the long lifespan of vehicles. Emissions reduction in the transport sector is sought to be achieved through the electrification of road transport, with the objective that all new cars sold in the European Union be zero-emission by 2035; the promotion of alternative fuels such as green hydrogen, advanced biofuels and synthetic fuels for maritime, air and rail transport; and the improvement of vehicle energy efficiency and the promotion of public transport, active mobility and sustainable logistics. ### 2.3 The Emissions Trading System and its Reform

The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is the most important market-based instrument for reducing greenhouse gas emissions in the European Union and has been a central element of European climate policy since its creation in 2005. The EU ETS works by establishing a total emissions cap for covered installations, which includes power plants, industrial facilities and, since 2012, airlines operating within the European Union. This total cap is reduced annually to reflect emissions reduction targets, and installations must acquire emission permits both to cover their current emissions and to accumulate a stock of permits for the future. Installations that reduce their emissions below their allocation can sell their surplus permits to other installations that need additional permits, creating an economic incentive for emissions reduction. ### 2.4 The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) is one of the most innovative and ambitious tools of the European Green Deal, designed to prevent the European Union's climate policy from causing the relocation of carbon emissions to other countries with less stringent regulations, a phenomenon known as "carbon leakage." This mechanism, which was proposed by the European Commission in July 2021 and entered its transition phase in October 2023 with the obligation to report embedded emissions, establishes that importers of certain products from countries outside the European Union must acquire carbon certificates that reflect the carbon content of those products, thereby ensuring that the carbon price is similar for both products manufactured within the European Union and those imported from abroad. The products covered by CBAM initially include cement, iron and steel, fertilizers, energy, aluminium and lime, which are sectors particularly vulnerable to carbon leakage due to their high carbon intensity and exposure to international trade. The implementation of CBAM is carried out in two phases: a transition phase beginning in October 2023 and extending until the end of 2025, during which importers only have the obligation to report the embedded emissions in their products to allow data collection and verification; and a full implementation phase beginning in 2026, during which importers must acquire carbon certificates for each tonne of embedded emissions in their products. The price of CBAM carbon certificates will be determined based on the weekly average price of EU Emissions Trading System auctions, thereby ensuring consistency between both tools. ### 2.5 Climate Change Adaptation

The European Union Strategy for Adaptation to Climate Change, adopted in February 2021, establishes an ambitious vision for Europe to be climate-resilient by 2050, with well-integrated adaptive measures across all policy areas to ensure a carbon-neutral, resilient and prosperous society. The strategy identifies the sectors most vulnerable to climate change in Europe, including agriculture, forestry, transport, energy, health, tourism, ecosystems and coastal and inland urban areas, and establishes specific adaptation measures for each of these sectors. Climate adaptation includes measures such as building resilient infrastructure, developing drought and heat-resistant crops, creating urban green zones to mitigate the heat island effect, protecting coastlines against sea level rise, and creating early warning systems for extreme weather events. The Second Assessment Report on Climate Change Adaptation in Europe, published by the European Environment Agency in 2022, noted that Europe is warming faster than any other continent and that the effects of climate change are already evident across the continent, with a 50 percent increase in extreme weather events over the last 30 years. The report noted that climate adaptation in Europe has progressed but is not sufficient, and that more ambitious measures are needed to protect people, ecosystems and economies from the effects of climate change. ## Chapter 4: The Circular Economy

### 4.1 The Action Plan for the Circular Economy

The Action Plan for the Circular Economy, adopted by the European Commission in March 2020, constitutes a cornerstone of the European Green Deal and represents a fundamental paradigm shift in the way the European Union approaches resource and waste management. This plan establishes an ambitious vision to transform the European Union into a fully circular economy by 2050, in which the value of products, materials and resources is maintained in the economy for as long as possible, and in which waste generation is minimized. The circular economy differs from the traditional linear economy (extract, produce, use and dispose) in that it seeks to keep materials in use for as long as possible, extract maximum value from them while they are in use, and recover and regenerate products and materials at the end of their useful life. The plan is structured around four priority actions: making sustainable products the norm, empowering consumers and giving them rights, minimizing waste, and creating secondary markets for secondary raw materials. The first priority action focuses on product design, as it is estimated that more than 80 percent of a product's environmental impact is determined at the design stage. For this reason, the plan establishes measures to promote sustainable design, including requirements for durability, repairability, recyclability and recycled content, as well as restrictions on the use of hazardous substances that hinder recycling. The second priority action seeks to empower consumers by providing them with reliable and transparent information on product sustainability through instruments such as the Product Environmental Declaration, the eco-label and the digital product passport. ### 5.2 The Nature Restoration Law

The Nature Restoration Law, adopted in February 2024, is one of the most ambitious and fundamental legislative pieces of the European Green Deal in the area of biodiversity, as it establishes legally binding targets for the restoration of degraded ecosystems across the European Union for the first time in the history of European law. This law responds to the growing scientific evidence that the simple protection of natural areas is not sufficient to halt and reverse biodiversity loss, but that it is necessary to actively restore degraded ecosystems to recover their functioning and the services they provide to society. The law establishes that the European Union must restore at least 20 percent of terrestrial and marine areas by 2030, and all ecosystems requiring restoration by 2050. To achieve these objectives, the law establishes specific measures for different types of ecosystems. For terrestrial ecosystems, the law requires that Member States adopt restoration measures in at least 30 percent of their terrestrial and marine areas by 2030, including the restoration of wetlands, peatlands, forests, grasslands, agricultural areas and river ecosystems. For marine ecosystems, the law requires the restoration of seabeds, seagrass meadows and coral reefs, as well as the reduction of marine pollution and the protection of spawning areas for marine species. The Natura 2000 Network is the largest and most ambitious nature conservation project in the world, and constitutes the core of the European Union's biodiversity policy since its creation in 1992. The Natura 2000 Network is not a system of nature reserves closed to the public, but seeks to reconcile nature conservation with human activity, allowing sustainable uses of land and sea provided they are compatible with conservation objectives. Each site in the Natura 2000 Network is managed through a Management Guidance Document or a Management Plan that establishes the conservation measures necessary to maintain or improve the conservation status of habitats and species of community interest present at the site. ### 6.2 The Water Policy of the European Union

The water policy of the European Union is one of the most advanced and comprehensive areas of European environmental legislation, and has been internationally recognized as a model for integrated water management. The Water Framework Directive (Directive 2000/60/EC), adopted in 2000, is the cornerstone of this policy and establishes a comprehensive framework for the protection and sustainable management of all surface waters, groundwater, coastal and transitional waters in the European Union. This directive establishes the objective that all waters achieve good ecological status and good chemical status, defined based on biological, physico-chemical and pollutant indicators specific to each type of water body. ### 12.4 Sweden: Circular Economy and Waste Management
