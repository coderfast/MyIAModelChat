Formal greetings form the foundation of all social and professional interaction in the Spanish-speaking world. From the moment two people meet, the greeting sets the tone for the conversation that is about to begin. In formal settings, it is essential to use expressions that demonstrate respect and consideration toward the other person. The most common of all is "Buenos días" (Good morning), which is used from the early morning hours until approximately midday. This expression is not just a simple gesture of courtesy, but also reflects the culture of punctuality and formality that characterizes many Spanish-speaking countries. When we say "Buenos días," we convey a positive and receptive attitude that facilitates the beginning of any type of relationship, whether commercial, academic, or social. It is important to accompany this expression with a moderate smile and direct eye contact, but without being intimidating. In very formal contexts, such as business meetings or diplomatic encounters, the person's name is sometimes added after the greeting, for example: "Buenos días, señor García." This practice personalizes the greeting and demonstrates that we know and recognize the person we are greeting, which is especially appreciated in professional environments where personalized treatment makes the difference between a cold interaction and a warm one.

The formal greeting system in Spanish is organized primarily according to the time of day, creating a temporal structure that every speaker must master. "Buenos días" is used in the first part of the day, generally from dawn until noon. From that time until approximately seven in the afternoon, the appropriate greeting is "Buenas tardes." Finally, from that time until night, "Buenas noches" is used. This time-based distribution is not arbitrary, but reflects the traditional organization of the day in Hispanic cultures, where midday marks an important turning point in the daily routine. It is interesting to note that in some Latin American countries, especially in regions with warm climates, the schedules may vary slightly. For example, in some Caribbean countries, "Buenas tardes" may begin a little later because social activity extends beyond sunset. However, the three main expressions maintain their validity throughout the Spanish-speaking world. When we are not sure which greeting to use, "Buenas" works as an informal alternative that combines elements of the three expressions and can be used in less rigorous contexts. This flexibility demonstrates the richness of the Spanish language and its adaptability to different everyday situations.

In formal contexts, it is customary to accompany greetings with forms of address that indicate the position or profession of the person being greeted. The most common include "señor" (Mr.) and "señora" (Mrs./Ms.) for addressing adults whose name we do not know, as well as "don" and "doña" to show especially high respect, such as toward elderly people or those of recognized authority. In the professional field, academic titles such as "doctor" or "doctora" are used when we know the person holds that degree, and "profesor" or "profesora" in the educational context. In the business world, it is common to address people by their position: "Buenos días, director" (Good morning, director), "Buenas tardes, gerente" (Good afternoon, manager). This practice not only shows respect but also recognizes the organizational hierarchy and facilitates the identification of roles within a company or institution. In recent years, the use of Mr. and Mrs. followed by full surnames has grown, such as "señor Martínez" or "señora Rodríguez," especially in written communications and formal presentations. It is important to remember that the incorrect use of titles can generate awkward situations, so it is always preferable to ask or research before addressing someone formally, especially in international environments where customs may vary significantly.

Formal greetings in writing follow specific conventions that differ slightly from oral greetings. In a formal letter or email, the initial greeting is fundamental for establishing the tone of the communication. The most common options include "Estimado señor" (Dear Mr.) or "Estimada señora" (Dear Mrs./Ms.) when we know the person's gender, and "Estimado/a" when we want to be inclusive or do not have that information. If we know the person's full name, we can write "Estimado Sr. García" (Dear Mr. García) or "Estimada Dra. Martínez" (Dear Dr. Martínez). In institutional contexts, formulas such as "Muy señor mío" (Dear Sir) or "A quien corresponda" (To whom it may concern) may be found, although these latter ones are losing usage in favor of more modern and direct expressions. The closing of the message also requires a formal farewell that completes the greeting cycle: "Atentamente" (Sincerely), "Cordialmente" (Cordially), "Un saludo distinguido" (Kind regards), or "Le saluda atentamente" (Yours sincerely) are valid options that show courtesy and professionalism. In the digital world, formal greetings have adapted to emails and instant messaging communications in work environments, maintaining the essence of respect but with a slightly more relaxed tone. It is essential to adapt the formal greeting to the specific context, considering factors such as the prior relationship with the person, the degree of familiarity, and the cultural norms of the environment.

At official events such as inaugurations, conferences, ceremonies, or protocol events, formal greetings take on paramount importance. The host or moderator generally begins with a greeting that includes all the authorities present, starting with the highest-ranking one. For example: "Excelentísimo señor presidente, distinguidas autoridades, señores y señoras invitados, bienvenidos a este evento" (Mr. President, distinguished authorities, ladies and gentlemen, welcome to this event). This formula not only shows respect toward authority figures but also establishes a hierarchical order that reflects the power structure of the event. When introducing speakers or special guests, it is customary to use expressions such as "tenemos el honor de presentar al distinguido profesor" (we have the honor of presenting the distinguished professor) or "nos complace dar la bienvenida a" (we are pleased to welcome). Greetings at international events usually include references to the host country and the represented nations, creating an atmosphere of cordiality and cooperation. It is common for the opening speech to contain several levels of greeting: a general one to all attendees, a specific one to the authorities, and another to the speakers or participants. Careful preparation of these greetings demonstrates professionalism and attention to detail, qualities highly valued in the world of corporate and academic events. The tone should be solemn but not excessively rigid, conveying warmth without losing the formality required by the occasion.

Informal greetings among friends represent the most spontaneous and authentic expression of interpersonal communication in the Spanish-speaking world. Unlike formal greetings, which follow strict conventions, informal greetings are characterized by their variety, creativity, and emotional proximity. Expressions like "¡Hola!" (Hi!), "¿Qué tal?" (How's it going?), "¿Cómo estás?" (How are you?) or "¿Qué hay?" (What's up?) are the most commonly used in everyday life among friends, close family members, and trusted colleagues. These greetings are usually accompanied by physical gestures such as hugs, kisses on the cheek (in many countries), or relaxed handshakes. The choice of informal greeting depends on several factors, including the length of the friendship, the geographic region, and personal preferences. In Spain, for example, it is common to greet with two kisses on the cheeks, while in Argentina only one kiss is preferred, and in other Latin American countries hugs are more frequent than kisses. Informal greetings can also include questions about everyday life, such as "¿Qué más?" (What else?), "¿Cómo va todo?" (How's everything going?) or "¿Qué hay de nuevo?" (What's new?), which do not seek detailed answers but function as an extension of the greeting itself. This practice reflects the importance of personal connection in Hispanic cultures, where human relationships are valued above formality.

Spanish is rich in colloquial greetings that vary enormously from one region to another, creating a fascinating linguistic mosaic. In Spain, expressions like "¡Hola, tío!" or "¡Hola, tía!" (Hi, dude! / Hi, girl!) are very common among young friends, while "¡Hola, guapo!" or "¡Hola, guapa!" (What happened!), which are very informal ways of asking how someone is doing. In Argentina, "¿Qué onda, boludo?" (What's up, dude?) or "¡Hola, che!" (Hi, hey!) are typical expressions that reflect the local idiosyncrasy. In Colombia, "¡Quiubo!" (a contraction of "¿Qué hubo?") is extremely popular and is used in almost any informal context. These expressions not only serve to greet but also function as regional identity markers, showing where the speaker comes from and what social group they belong to. The use of diminutives is also frequent in informal greetings: "Hola, cariño" (Hi, sweetheart), "Hola, corazón" (Hi, darling), "Hola, mi vida" (Hi, my love) are affectionate expressions used between couples, family members, or very close friends. The richness of these expressions demonstrates that Spanish is not a monolithic language, but a vibrant collection of dialects and varieties that reflect the cultural diversity of the Spanish-speaking world.

Introducing yourself is one of the most fundamental social skills that we all must master to navigate society successfully. An effective personal introduction consists of several essential elements that, although they may seem simple, require practice and confidence to execute correctly. The most basic component is the name, which is usually preceded by a formula like "Me llamo..." (My name is...) or "Mi nombre es..." (My name is...). Next, it is customary to provide additional information that helps the interlocutor place us: profession, origin, reason for the meeting, or some relevant contextual detail. For example: "Me llamo María García, soy profesora de español y estoy aquí para la conferencia sobre educación bilingüe" (My name is María García, I am a Spanish teacher and I am here for the conference on bilingual education). This concise introduction includes name, occupation, and context, the three pillars of an effective introduction. The tone of voice is equally important: it should be clear, measured, and at an appropriate volume so that the person listening can understand each word without difficulty. Eye contact during the introduction conveys confidence and honesty, while a moderate smile shows friendliness and openness. In very informal situations, the introduction can be shorter: "Soy Juan" (I'm Juan) or "Hola, Juan" (Hi, Juan) is sufficient when the context does not require further explanation. However, in professional or academic environments, it is always better to provide enough information so that the other person can start a conversation with us.

Introducing two people who do not know each other is a social art that requires tact, relevant information, and good judgment to facilitate a meaningful connection between them. The fundamental principles of a good introduction include clarity, relevance, and warmth. Clarity means pronouncing both names in a way that each person can understand and remember them easily, especially if the names are uncommon or come from different linguistic traditions. Relevance means providing information that is useful to both parties and can serve as a starting point for conversation: profession, common interests, relationship with the host, or reason for the meeting. The physical positioning of the introduction matters too: ideally, both people should be at a comfortable distance, with open body angles that initially include the introducer and then orient toward each other. It is important to avoid introductions that are too long or detailed, which can be uncomfortable for participants, as well as introductions that are too brief and do not provide enough information to start a meaningful conversation.
