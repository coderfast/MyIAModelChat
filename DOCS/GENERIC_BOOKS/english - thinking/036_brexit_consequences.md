Brexit is the term used to describe the withdrawal of the United Kingdom from the European Union, a historic event that took place on January 31, 2020, after almost 47 years of membership in the organization. The term Brexit is a combination of the English words British and exit, and was first used by journalist Peter Wilding in 2012 to describe the possible departure of the United Kingdom from the European Union. The Brexit process officially began on June 23, 2016, when the United Kingdom held a referendum in which 51.9 percent of voters supported leaving the European Union, with a turnout of 72.2 percent. The referendum was called by Prime Minister David Cameron in response to growing domestic and international political pressure over the question of the United Kingdom's membership in the European Union, and was influenced by multiple factors, including concerns about immigration, national sovereignty, the economy, and European bureaucracy. The referendum result surprised many observers, as previous polls had shown a preference for maintaining membership in the Union, and it generated a political crisis in the United Kingdom that led to the resignation of David Cameron and the nomination of Theresa May as the new Prime Minister. The Brexit process was long and complex, lasting more than four years from the 2016 referendum to the effective withdrawal of the United Kingdom in January 2020, and included difficult negotiations on the terms of withdrawal, the withdrawal agreement, the Northern Ireland Protocol, and the future relationship between the United Kingdom and the European Union.

Another important cause was concern about national sovereignty, as many British citizens perceived that the European Union had accumulated too much power and had reduced the ability of the British government to make its own decisions in areas such as legislation, the economy, and foreign policy. The Brexit negotiation process was long, complex, and emotionally charged, lasting more than four years from the start of negotiations in 2017 to the end of the transition period in 2020. The process included multiple phases, including the notification of withdrawal, negotiations on the withdrawal agreement, negotiations on the future relationship, ratification of the withdrawal agreement, extensions of deadlines, and the end of the transition period. The first phase was the notification of withdrawal, which took place on March 29, 2017, when Prime Minister Theresa May triggered Article 50 of the Treaty on European Union, which establishes the withdrawal procedure for a Member State. The notification started a two-year deadline for negotiations on the terms of withdrawal, which were completed in November 2018 with the signing of the Withdrawal Agreement between the United Kingdom and the European Union. The Withdrawal Agreement included provisions on citizens' rights, the financial settlement, the Northern Ireland Protocol, and the transition period. The withdrawal agreement was repeatedly rejected by the British Parliament, which generated a political crisis in the United Kingdom and led to the resignation of Theresa May and the nomination of Boris Johnson as the new Prime Minister. Boris Johnson renegotiated the withdrawal agreement, modifying the Northern Ireland Protocol, and the new agreement was approved by the British Parliament in December 2019. The transition period began on February 1, 2020 and lasted until December 31, 2020, during which the United Kingdom remained in the single market and the Schengen Area, and the future relationship between the United Kingdom and the European Union was negotiated.

The Northern Ireland Protocol was the most delicate and controversial aspect of the Brexit withdrawal agreement, as it addressed the question of the border between Northern Ireland (which is part of the United Kingdom) and the Republic of Ireland (which is a Member State of the European Union). The border between Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland is a sensitive location due to the history of violence and conflict in Northern Ireland, known as The Troubles, which lasted from the late 1960s until the Good Friday Agreement of 1998. The Good Friday Agreement established a peace framework that put an end to political violence in Northern Ireland, and a fundamental component of the agreement was the elimination of physical border controls between Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. The withdrawal of the United Kingdom from the European Union posed a dilemma: if the United Kingdom left the single market and the customs union, customs and regulatory controls between Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland would have to be restored, which could threaten peace in the region. The Northern Ireland Protocol established that Northern Ireland would remain in the European Union customs union for goods purposes, which meant that goods entering Northern Ireland from the rest of the United Kingdom would be subject to customs controls. The protocol also established that goods moving from Northern Ireland to the Republic of Ireland and to the single market would not be subject to controls, as Northern Ireland would remain in the Union's customs union. The Northern Ireland Protocol was criticized by the British government and by unionists in Northern Ireland, who argued that it created a maritime border between Northern Ireland and the rest of the United Kingdom, which could threaten the territorial integrity of the United Kingdom. The European Union maintained that the protocol was necessary to protect peace in Northern Ireland and to guarantee the integrity of the single market, and rejected requests from the British government to renegotiate the protocol.
