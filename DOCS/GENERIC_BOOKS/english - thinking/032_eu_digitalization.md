The European Union has undertaken an unprecedented digital transformation with the goal of creating a Digital Single Market that enables European citizens and businesses to fully benefit from the opportunities offered by the digital economy. The Digital Single Market Strategy, presented by the European Commission in January 2025, is the ambitious plan that sets out the policy guidelines for creating an integrated, competitive, and open digital market across the entire European Union. This strategy is based on the need to eliminate barriers that fragment the European digital market, that prevent the free flow of data, services, and digital products between Member States, and that limit the competitiveness of European companies against their American and Asian competitors. The strategy identifies several priority areas of action, including connectivity, online trust and security, empowering people and businesses for the digital age, and promoting an open and democratic digital governance framework. To achieve these objectives, the European Commission has adopted numerous legislative initiatives in recent years, including the Digital Markets Act (DMA), the Digital Services Act (DSA), the Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act), the Data Act, the Data Governance Act, the Regulation on the Interoperability of Public Digital Services, and the eIDAS 2.0 Regulation on electronic identification and trust services. These legislative initiatives, collectively known as the "digital rules," establish a comprehensive regulatory framework for the digital economy that spans from competition and consumer protection to data protection, artificial intelligence, and electronic identification. The importance of the Digital Single Market lies in the fact that Europe has more than 450 million consumers and thousands of digital companies, but its potential is limited by the fragmentation of the market into 27 different national markets with different regulations, standards, and practices. The creation of a true Digital Single Market will enable European companies to scale their businesses across the entire European Union without having to adapt their products or services to the requirements of 27 different countries, and will enable European consumers to access a wider range of digital products and services at competitive prices.

The Digital Markets Act (DMA) is one of the most innovative and ambitious legislative initiatives of the European Union's digital regulatory package, adopted in July 2022 and applicable since May 2023. The DMA establishes a regulatory framework for the largest digital platforms known as "gatekeepers," which are platforms that act as critical intermediaries between business users and companies that depend on them to reach their customers, and that have a significant impact on the European digital market. The DMA identifies as gatekeepers platforms such as Google (Search Engine, Google Maps, Google Play, Google Shopping, YouTube, Chrome, Android), Apple (App Store, Safari, iOS), Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger, Marketplace), Amazon (Marketplace, AWS), Microsoft (Windows, LinkedIn, Teams), and ByteDance (TikTok), among others. These platforms are subject to specific behavioral obligations that seek to ensure fair competition and openness of the digital market, including the prohibition of anticompetitive practices such as self-preferencing, blocking interoperability, using third-party data to compete with them, and giving preference to their own services and products on their platforms. The DMA also establishes interoperability obligations for messaging services, requiring messaging platforms such as WhatsApp, iMessage, and Messenger to allow interoperability with other messaging platforms, enabling users to send messages between different platforms without having to change applications. The European Commission is the authority responsible for enforcing the DMA, and has significant powers to investigate possible infringements, impose provisional measures, and issue sanctions that can reach up to 10 percent of the gatekeeper's annual worldwide turnover, or up to 20 percent for repeated infringements.

The Digital Services Act (DSA) is another of the key legislative initiatives of the European Union's digital regulatory package, adopted in October 2022 and applicable since February 2024. The DSA establishes a modern and harmonized regulatory framework for digital services, ranging from providers of intermediary services (such as Internet access providers, web hosting services, and cloud storage services) to the largest online platforms (such as search engines, social networks, and online commerce platforms). The DSA establishes transparency, accountability, and consumer protection obligations for digital services, including the obligation to provide clear information about online advertising, content moderation, recommendation algorithms, and dispute resolution mechanisms. For the largest online platforms, known as "very large online platforms" (VLOPs), which are those with more than 45 million active monthly users in the European Union (approximately 10 percent of the European population), the DSA establishes significant additional obligations, including the obligation to assess and mitigate systemic risks associated with their services, such as the dissemination of illegal content, the protection of public health, the integrity of electoral processes, gender-based violence, the protection of fundamental rights, and the impact on minors. The DSA also establishes obligations for independent auditing of recommendation algorithms, requiring platforms to submit their algorithms to external audits to assess whether these algorithms generate negative effects on fundamental rights, public health, or the integrity of electoral processes. The European Commission has significant powers to enforce the DSA and can impose sanctions that can reach up to 6 percent of the platform's annual worldwide turnover.

The original eIDAS Regulation, adopted in 2014, established a framework for electronic identification and trust services in the European Union, but its implementation was uneven and failed to achieve the goal of full interoperability between national electronic identification systems. The eIDAS 2.0 Regulation addresses these limitations by establishing a renewed framework for electronic identification that includes the creation of a European Digital Identity Wallet (EUDI Wallet), a mobile application that will enable European citizens to store digital identity documents, identification cards, driving licenses, academic certificates, medical prescriptions, and other official documents, and use them across the entire European Union to authenticate themselves in online services, prove their identity, and share documents securely. The Regulation also establishes obligations for digital identity service providers, including requirements for security, data protection, and interoperability, and establishes a framework for the mutual recognition of electronic identification across the European Union.

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which came into force in May 2018, is the most important and comprehensive data protection standard in the world, and has made the European Union a global reference in matters of privacy and personal data protection. The GDPR establishes a harmonized legal framework for the protection of European citizens' personal data, which applies to all organizations, both public and private, that process personal data of residents in the European Union, regardless of where the organization is located. The GDPR is based on several fundamental principles: lawfulness, transparency, and fairness in data processing; purpose limitation, which establishes that data may only be collected for specific, explicit, and legitimate purposes; data minimization, which establishes that only data necessary for the intended purpose may be collected; data accuracy; storage limitation; data integrity and confidentiality; and proactive accountability, which requires organizations to demonstrate compliance with the GDPR. The GDPR establishes specific rights for European citizens, including the right of access to personal data, the right to rectification of inaccurate data, the right to erasure ("right to be forgotten"), the right to restriction of processing, the right to data portability, the right to object to processing, and the right not to be subject to individualized automated decisions, including profiling. The GDPR establishes significant penalties for non-compliance with the standard, with fines that can reach up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of the organization's annual worldwide turnover, which constitutes a powerful incentive for organizations to comply with the standard. The European Commission has published guidelines and interpretations to help organizations implement the GDPR effectively, and the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) coordinates the application of the GDPR across the European Union.

The Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act), adopted in March 2024 and applicable gradually from August 2025, is the world's first comprehensive regulatory framework for artificial intelligence, and establishes a risk-based approach to the regulation of AI systems that balances innovation with the protection of fundamental rights. The AI Act classifies AI systems into four categories based on their risk level: unacceptable risk, high risk, limited risk, and minimal risk, and establishes specific requirements for each category. Unacceptable risk AI systems, which include real-time mass surveillance, government social scoring, and subliminal manipulation of vulnerable people, are prohibited under the AI Act. High-risk AI systems, which include AI systems used in education, employment, credit, justice, migration management, essential public services, healthcare, and autonomous vehicles, are subject to strict requirements for conformity assessment, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, and continuous monitoring. Limited risk AI systems, which include chatbots, facial recognition systems, and deepfake generators, are subject to transparency requirements that require providers to inform users that they are interacting with an AI system or that content has been generated by AI. Minimal risk AI systems, which include spam filters, video games, and music recommendation systems, are not subject to specific requirements under the AI Act. The AI Act also establishes specific requirements for general-purpose AI models (GPAI), such as large language models (LLMs) like GPT-4, including the obligation to provide detailed technical documentation, the obligation to comply with copyright legislation, the obligation to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the training content used to develop the model, and additional transparency requirements for GPAI models that present systemic risk, such as the ability to generate content that could be confused with real content (deepfakes) or content that could be used to commit crimes.

The protection of fundamental rights in the digital age is a fundamental challenge for the European Union, as digital transformation poses new risks to human rights that require innovative regulatory responses. The Digital Services Act and the AI Act establish specific requirements for the protection of minors online, including the prohibition of advertising targeted at minors and the obligation to assess and mitigate risks to minors in AI systems.
