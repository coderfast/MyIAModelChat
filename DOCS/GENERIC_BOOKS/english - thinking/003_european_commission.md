# The European Commission

## Chapter 1: Composition and Election

### The Structure of the Commission

The European Commission is the executive institution of the European Union and plays a central role in the decision-making process. It is currently composed of 27 commissioners, one from each Member State, headed by a president. Each commissioner is responsible for a specific policy area, known as a portfolio, which may cover a single policy area or several related ones. The Commission acts as a whole as a college, which means that all important decisions are taken by consensus among all commissioners. This collegiate structure ensures the coherence of European policy and reflects the supranational character of the institution.

The commissioners are proposed by the European Council, by common agreement with the President of the Commission, and must be approved by the European Parliament. The selection process for commissioners is politically delicate, as national governments want to ensure that their representatives obtain influential portfolios, while the President of the Commission seeks a geographic, gender, and competence balance. The European Parliament holds hearings with candidates for commissioner to assess their suitability and their political programs, and may reject individual candidates or the entire college. This investiture power gives the Parliament significant influence over the composition of the Commission.

The European Commission is headquartered in Brussels, in the Berlaymont building, which is one of the most recognizable buildings in the European capital. The Commission has approximately 32,000 officials, organized into directorates-general specialized in different policy areas. Each commissioner has their own cabinet, composed of political and technical advisors, and a directorate-general responsible for the preparation and implementation of policies. The administrative structure of the Commission is complex, as it must deal with a wide range of policy areas and coordinate its actions with other European institutions and national governments. The effectiveness of the Commission depends, in large part, on the capacity of its staff to work in a coordinated and efficient manner.

### The Investiture Process

The investiture process of the European Commission is a crucial moment in the political life of the EU, as it determines the composition and priorities of the executive institution. The process begins with the election of the President of the Commission by the European Council, taking into account the results of the European elections and the need to present a candidate who has the support of the majority of the Parliament. The candidate for the presidency presents their political program to the Parliament, and the plenary debates and votes on their investiture. If the candidate is approved, they proceed to propose the commissioners, in consultation with national governments, and the entire college is subjected to an investiture vote in the Parliament.

The parliamentary hearings of the candidates for commissioner are an important element of the investiture process. Each candidate is examined by the competent parliamentary committee, which assesses their skills, their political program, and their ability to perform the functions of a commissioner. The hearings are public and are followed by the media and citizens. During the hearings, MEPs ask candidates about their political plans and their opinions on various topics. If a candidate is considered unsuitable by the parliamentary committee, the President of the Commission may propose a new candidate or reassign the portfolio. This hearing process ensures that the commissioners are competent individuals committed to the EU's objectives.

The investiture vote of the college of commissioners takes place in the plenary session of the European Parliament. MEPs vote yes or no on the entire college as a whole, which means that they cannot reject individual commissioners, only the entire college. This system has been criticized by some, who argue that the Parliament should be allowed to evaluate each commissioner individually, but others defend it as a guarantee of the college's coherence and solidarity. If the college is rejected, the President of the Commission must propose a new college, which can lead to a prolonged political crisis. Once approved, the college of commissioners takes office and begins to perform its functions.

## Chapter 2: The Role of the President

### The Election and Functions of the President

The President of the European Commission is one of the most important figures in the European Union, as they lead the Commission and represent the institution externally. The President is elected by the European Council, taking into account the results of the European elections, and must be approved by the European Parliament. The Treaty of Lisbon established that the European Council must take into account the results of the European elections when proposing a candidate, which has led European political parties to present candidates for the presidency of the Commission before the elections, in a system known as spitzenkandidaten. This system seeks to democratize the election of the President by linking it to electoral results.

The functions of the President of the Commission are broad and varied. First, the President defines the political guidelines of the Commission and ensures that the institution acts in a coherent and effective manner. Second, the President chairs the meetings of the college of commissioners and coordinates the work of the different commissioners. Third, the President represents the Commission in relations with the other European institutions, especially the European Parliament and the Council of the EU. Fourth, the President represents the EU externally, participating in international summits and maintaining relationships with world leaders. Fifth, the President can dismiss individual commissioners, which gives them significant control over the composition of the Commission.

The President of the Commission also plays an important role in the legislative process, as they can propose new policies and legislation, and can withdraw or modify existing proposals. The President also coordinates the Commission's relationship with the European Parliament, participating in plenary debates and answering MEPs' questions. The President plays a crucial role in budget negotiations, as they present the draft budget and negotiate with the Council and Parliament to reach an agreement. The power of the President of the Commission has grown significantly with successive treaties, and is currently one of the most influential political figures in Europe.

### The Presidents of the Commission Throughout History

The European Commission has had several presidents throughout its history, each with their own style and political priorities. Walter Hallstein (1958-1967) was the first President of the Commission, and his mandate was characterized by the development of the EEC's competencies and the consolidation of European institutions. Hallstein promoted the creation of the common market and the common agricultural policy, and laid the foundations for economic integration. His successor, Jean Rey (1967-1970), oversaw the merger of the executives of the three European communities, and Francois-Xavier Ortoli (1973-1977) faced the oil crisis and its economic consequences.

Roy Jenkins (1977-1981) was the first British President of the Commission, and his mandate focused on deepening the common market and monetary cooperation. Gaston Thorn (1981-1985) promoted the creation of the internal market, and Jacques Delors (1985-1995) is considered one of the most influential presidents in the history of the Commission. Delors promoted the creation of the single market, the signing of the Treaty of Maastricht, and the preparation of the single currency. His mandate was characterized by an ambitious vision of European integration and effective management of the Commission. Jacques Santer (1995-1999) faced a crisis of confidence when the Commission was accused of mismanagement, which forced the resignation of the entire college.

Romano Prodi (1999-2004) was the first president with real executive powers, as the Treaty of Amsterdam granted the president the ability to assign portfolios to commissioners. Prodi promoted enlargement to the East and the reform of European institutions. Jose Manuel Barroso (2004-2014) faced the global financial crisis and the euro crisis, and his mandate was characterized by the management of these crises and the adoption of austerity measures. Jean-Claude Juncker (2014-2019) promoted the creation of the European Stability Mechanism and the adoption of the European Green Plan. Ursula von der Leyen (2019-present) is the first woman to lead the Commission, and her mandate focuses on the green and digital transformation of Europe.

## Chapter 3: The Commissioner Portfolios

### The Distribution of Portfolios

The commissioner portfolios are the areas of responsibility assigned to each commissioner of the European Commission. The distribution of portfolios is a political decision of the President of the Commission, which seeks to balance expertise, geographic representation, and political priorities. There are currently 27 portfolios, covering areas as diverse as the economy, trade, agriculture, competition, the environment, energy, justice, foreign affairs, and security policy. The distribution of portfolios reflects the Commission's political priorities and the importance given to each policy area.

Some portfolios are traditionally more influential than others, as they cover significant areas of competence and substantial budgets. The portfolio of Economy and Monetary Affairs, for example, has considerable influence on the EU's economic policy, while the portfolio of Agriculture and Food Policy controls the EU's largest budget. The portfolio of Competition plays an important role in regulating companies and protecting the single market, and the portfolio of Foreign Affairs and Security Policy coordinates the EU's foreign policy. The portfolio of Environment and Oceans has gained importance in recent years, due to the Commission's focus on the green transformation.

The distribution of portfolios also reflects the geographic and gender diversity of the Commission. Each Member State is entitled to one commissioner, which ensures equitable representation. The President of the Commission also seeks to balance the number of male and female commissioners, and to assign portfolios that reflect the skills and experience of each commissioner. The distribution of portfolios is a complex process that requires negotiations with national governments and with other commissioners, and can be a source of tension if any country considers that its commissioner has not received a sufficiently influential portfolio.

### The Directorates-General

The directorates-general of the European Commission are the administrative units responsible for the preparation and implementation of European policies. Each commissioner portfolio is backed by one or more directorates-general, which provide technical assistance, research, and administrative support to the commissioners. The directorates-general are composed of officials from different nationalities and professional backgrounds, and their work is essential for the effectiveness of the Commission. There are currently 34 directorates-general, each specialized in a specific policy area.

The directorates-general have a dual function: on the one hand, they prepare the legislative proposals and policies that the commissioners present to the Council and Parliament; on the other hand, they supervise the implementation of policies approved by the European institutions. The directorates-general also coordinate their work with the other directorates-general and with the Commission's representations in the Member States. The effectiveness of the directorates-general is crucial for the quality of European legislation and for the effective implementation of policies. The Commission has undertaken various reforms to improve the efficiency of its directorates-general, including the simplification of administrative procedures and the promotion of innovation.

The directorates-general also play an important role in the Commission's relationship with the other European institutions and with national governments. They provide technical information to MEPs during the legislative process, collaborate with the Council of the EU in the implementation of policies, and work with national governments to ensure the correct transposition of European directives into national law. The directorates-general also coordinate cooperation with international organizations and with EU partner countries. Their work is fundamental to the functioning of the Commission and to the effectiveness of European policies.

## Chapter 4: The Legislative Power of the Commission

### The Monopoly on Legislative Initiative

The European Commission has the monopoly on legislative initiative in the European Union, which means that only it can propose new European laws. This monopoly is a central element of the EU's institutional system, as it ensures that legislation is prepared by a supranational institution with technical expertise and a global view of European interests. The monopoly on legislative initiative was established by the Treaties of Rome in 1957 and has been maintained by successive treaties, although with some exceptions. The Commission presents annually a work program that sets the legislative priorities for the following year, and the European Parliament and the Council of the EU can make requests to the Commission to present proposals on specific topics.

The Commission's monopoly on legislative initiative has been criticized by some, who argue that it gives too much power to an institution not directly elected by citizens. The European Parliament, in particular, has called for the right to propose legislation, but this proposal has encountered opposition from national governments, who fear it would weaken the Council's role in the legislative process. However, the Commission has shown itself to be receptive to the Parliament's and Council's requests, and often presents proposals in response to the concerns of these institutions. The monopoly on legislative initiative has also been questioned for its impact on the agility of the legislative process, as the Commission may take months or years to prepare a proposal.

Despite these criticisms, the Commission's monopoly on legislative initiative has significant advantages. The Commission has a highly qualified staff that can analyze the implications of legislative proposals and prepare technically sound texts. The Commission also has a global view of European interests, which allows it to propose legislation that balances the different interests of Member States and citizens. The monopoly on legislative initiative also ensures the coherence of European legislation, as the Commission coordinates proposals from different policy areas to avoid duplications and contradictions. In general, the monopoly on legislative initiative has proven to be an effective mechanism for preparing quality legislation in the EU.

### The Preparation of Legislative Proposals

The preparation of legislative proposals is one of the main functions of the European Commission. The preparation process begins with an analysis of the policy needs and the implications of a possible proposal. The Commission conducts consultations with stakeholders, including national governments, civil society organizations, businesses, and citizens, to obtain a wide range of opinions and information. These consultations are essential to ensure that proposals are realistic, effective, and acceptable to the different actors. The Commission also conducts an impact assessment to evaluate the economic, social, and environmental consequences of proposals, and to identify the best options for achieving the policy objectives.

Once the Commission has completed the consultations and impact assessments, it prepares the text of the legislative proposal. This process is complex and requires coordination between several directorates-general and commissioners' cabinets. The text of the proposal must be technically precise, legally sound, and politically acceptable. The Commission also prepares an explanatory memorandum that describes the objectives of the proposal, the options considered, and the reasons for the choice made. The proposal is examined by the college of commissioners, which must approve it before presenting it to the Parliament and the Council. This internal approval process ensures that the proposal has the unanimous support of all commissioners.

Once approved by the college, the proposal is formally presented to the European Parliament and the Council of the EU. The proposal is published in the Official Journal of the EU and made available to the public. The Parliament and the Council examine the proposal and may propose amendments, which the Commission may accept or reject. The Commission may also modify or withdraw its proposal in response to the Parliament's or Council's objections. This negotiation and compromise process is essential for the quality of European legislation, as it allows combining the Commission's technical expertise with the democratic legitimacy of the Parliament and the representation of national governments in the Council.

## Chapter 5: Executive Power

### The Implementation of Policies

The European Commission plays an important role in the implementation of European policies, as it is responsible for ensuring that the laws and policies approved by the Parliament and Council are applied effectively and coherently in all Member States. The Commission supervises the transposition of European directives into national law, verifies that Member States correctly apply European regulations, and can initiate infringement proceedings against countries that fail to meet their obligations. This implementation role is essential to ensure that European legislation has the same effect in all Member States and that the single market functions correctly.

The Commission has various instruments for implementing European policies. First, the Commission adopts implementing acts, which are specific measures for applying European regulations and directives. These acts are adopted through comitology, a procedure in which representatives of national governments (committees) advise the Commission on the adoption of implementing acts. Second, the Commission may adopt delegated acts, which are measures to complete or modify non-essential elements of legislation. These acts are adopted by the college of commissioners and may be objected to by the Parliament or Council. Third, the Commission manages European funding programs, such as Horizon Europe, Erasmus+, and structural funds, supervising their implementation and ensuring that they are used efficiently.

The Commission also plays an important role in representing the EU externally. The Commission negotiates trade agreements on behalf of the EU, manages relationships with partner countries, and coordinates development aid. The High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy, who is also Vice-President of the Commission, coordinates the common foreign and security policy and represents the EU in international relations. The Commission also participates in international organizations, such as the WTO, G7, and G20, and represents the EU in international negotiations on issues such as climate change, trade, and human rights.

### Implementing Powers and Comitology

Comitology is a decision-making system in which representatives of national governments advise the European Commission on the adoption of implementing acts. This system was established by the Treaties of Rome and has been modified several times to improve its transparency and democratic nature. Comitology is based on the delegation of implementing powers by the Parliament and Council to the Commission, which acts with the advice of committees composed of representatives of national governments. These committees are specialized in specific policy areas, such as agriculture, the environment, health, or food safety.

The comitology system has been criticized for its complexity and lack of transparency. The committees meet behind closed doors, and their deliberations and votes are not public, which raises concerns about the democratic nature of the process. The Commission has undertaken various reforms to improve the transparency of comitology, including the publication of committee mandates and the minutes of their meetings. However, comitology remains a topic of debate, as some argue that it gives too much power to national governments in the implementation of European policies, while others defend it as an effective mechanism to ensure Member States' participation in decision-making.

Comitology has also been criticized for its impact on the agility of the decision-making process, as committees may take months to reach an agreement. The Commission has attempted to streamline comitology by simplifying procedures and reducing the number of committees. However, comitology remains an essential element of the EU's governance system, as it ensures national governments' participation in the implementation of European policies. The Commission has proposed reforms to modernize comitology, including the introduction of electronic voting and the publication of voting results. These reforms aim to improve the efficiency and transparency of the system while maintaining the democratic character of decision-making.

## Chapter 6: The Commission and Democracy

### Democratic Legitimacy

The European Commission is a supranational institution, which means that it is not directly elected by citizens, but is appointed by national governments and approved by the European Parliament. This lack of direct election has generated a debate about the democratic legitimacy of the Commission, as some people argue that a not democratically elected institution should not have so much power in decision-making that affects millions of citizens. However, others argue that the Commission has indirect democratic legitimacy, as it is appointed by national governments, which are elected by citizens, and is approved by the European Parliament, which is directly elected. The Treaty of Lisbon strengthened the democratic legitimacy of the Commission by establishing that the European Council must take into account the results of the European elections when proposing a candidate for the presidency.

The European Commission has also implemented various measures to improve its democracy and proximity to citizens. The President of the Commission regularly participates in European Parliament debates and answers MEPs' questions. The Commission also organizes public consultations to obtain citizens' opinions on legislative proposals, and uses social media and the Commission's website to inform citizens about its activities. The Commission has also created the Transparency Register, which requires representatives of interests who interact with the Commission to register and make their lobbying activities public. These initiatives seek to improve the Commission's transparency and accountability, and strengthen citizens' trust in the institution.

The Commission also plays a role in promoting democracy within the EU and externally. The Commission supervises respect for democratic values in Member States, and can activate Article 7 of the Treaty on European Union if it considers that there is a clear risk of serious violation of EU values. The Commission also promotes democracy and human rights in the EU's external relations, through the inclusion of democratic clauses in trade and cooperation agreements, and through support for civil society organizations in developing countries. The promotion of democracy is a priority for the Commission, as democracy is a fundamental value of the EU and a condition for membership.

### Transparency and Accountability

The European Commission has implemented various measures to improve its transparency and accountability. The Transparency Register, created in 2011, requires representatives of interests who interact with the Commission to register and make their lobbying activities public. This register seeks to ensure that the Commission takes decisions based on reliable information and that stakeholders have equal access to decision-making. The Commission has also created the Transparency Portal, which provides access to public documents, activity reports, and financial data. These initiatives seek to improve citizens' trust in the Commission and ensure that the institution acts in a transparent and responsible manner.

The Commission is also accountable to the European Parliament, which has the power to dismiss the Commission through a motion of censure. Although this mechanism has never been used, its existence serves as a reminder that the Commission must answer to the citizens' representatives. The Parliament also evaluates the Commission's activity through annual reports, public hearings, and parliamentary questions. The Commission is obliged to answer Parliament's questions and provide information about its activities. This control relationship is essential to ensure the Commission's democracy and transparency.

The Commission has also implemented measures to improve internal accountability. The Commission's annual activity report describes the institution's main actions and results, and is examined by the Parliament and Council. The Commission has also created an internal audit body that supervises the management of the institution's financial and human resources. The Commission has also implemented a code of conduct for its commissioners and officials, which establishes behavioral standards and transparency obligations. These measures seek to ensure that the Commission acts in an ethical and responsible manner, and that its officials meet their professional obligations.

## Chapter 7: The Commission and the General Interest

### Defending the European General Interest

The European Commission's main mission is to defend the European general interest in the decision-making process. The European general interest is a concept that encompasses the values, objectives, and interests shared by all EU citizens, and that go beyond national or sectoral interests. The Commission must act independently of national governments and political pressures to ensure that its decisions are oriented towards the common good. This principle of independence is enshrined in the treaties, which establish that commissioners may not receive instructions from national governments and must act in the general interest of the EU.

The defense of the European general interest manifests itself in various areas. In the economic area, the Commission ensures that the single market functions correctly, combats anti-competitive practices, and promotes innovation and growth. In the social area, the Commission promotes social inclusion, equal opportunities, and the protection of workers' rights. In the environmental area, the Commission promotes sustainability and environmental protection. In the area of human rights, the Commission defends fundamental rights and human dignity. In all these areas, the Commission acts as the guardian of EU values and objectives, and as the promoter of the general interest of European citizens.

The Commission also plays a role in resolving conflicts of interest between Member States. When national governments have different interests, the Commission can act as a mediator and propose solutions that balance the different interests. This mediating role is essential for EU cohesion, as it ensures that decisions are taken in an equitable manner and that all Member States benefit from integration. The Commission also plays a role in protecting the rights of minorities and vulnerable groups, as it ensures that European policies do not discriminate and that they protect the most disadvantaged. Defending the European general interest is the raison d'etre of the Commission, and its capacity to perform this function is decisive for the success of European integration.

## Chapter 8: Commission Staff

### European Officials

The European Commission has approximately 32,000 officials, organized into directorates-general and administrative units. European officials are selected through a public competitive examination process, administered by the European Personnel Selection Office (EPSO), which ensures that candidates are selected based on merit and skills. European officials come from all Member States, and their linguistic and cultural diversity is an asset to the Commission. Officials perform various functions, from the preparation of legislative proposals to the management of funding programs, as well as external representation and technical assistance.

European officials are subject to a staff regulations that governs their rights and obligations. The staff regulations establish employment conditions, salaries, benefits, retirement rights, and codes of conduct. European officials enjoy professional immunity, which protects them from legal action for activities carried out in the exercise of their duties. This immunity is necessary to ensure officials' independence and their ability to perform their duties without external interference. European officials are also subject to a code of conduct that establishes ethical behavioral standards, including the obligation to act impartially, to refuse gifts, and to declare conflicts of interest.

The Commission has implemented various measures to improve the management of its staff, including the promotion of diversity, equal opportunities, and continuing education. The Commission has established gender objectives to ensure that women are equitably represented in positions of responsibility, and has implemented work flexibility programs to reconcile professional and family life. The Commission also offers continuing education to its officials to improve their skills and keep up with changes in policy areas. The quality of the Commission's staff is crucial for the effectiveness of the institution, and the Commission has demonstrated a commitment to attracting, developing, and retaining talent.

### Committees and Expert Groups

In addition to permanent officials, the European Commission relies on the advice of committees and expert groups. These committees are composed of representatives of national governments, independent experts, and civil society representatives, and provide technical and political advice to the Commission in specific areas. Committees are essential to ensure that the Commission's proposals are technically sound and politically acceptable. Some of the most important committees include the European Economic and Social Committee, which represents civil society, and the European Committee of the Regions, which represents regional and local authorities.

Expert groups are assemblies of specialists who provide the Commission with advice on technical and scientific matters. These groups are particularly important in areas such as health, food safety, the environment, and technology, where specialized knowledge is required. Expert groups are selected by the Commission based on their skills and independence, and their reports are public and available to the public. The Commission also organizes public consultations to obtain stakeholders' opinions on legislative proposals, and these consultations are an important instrument to ensure that proposals take into account citizens' and civil society concerns.

The European Commission also relies on the advice of the European External Action Service (EEAS), which is the EU's diplomatic service and provides advice on foreign and security policy. The EEAS is composed of officials from the Commission and national governments, and has representations around the world. The EEAS provides information and political analysis to the Commission, and helps it implement the EU's foreign policy. The network of EU representations in partner countries also provides information and advice to the Commission on local conditions and cooperation opportunities. The Commission's staff, both permanent and temporary, is an essential resource for the institution's effectiveness and for the implementation of European policies.

## Chapter 9: The History and Evolution of the European Commission

### The Origins of the Institution

The history of the European Commission dates back to the early years of European integration, when the founders of the ECSC sought to create institutions that could manage the common policies of Member States. The institution was created with a clear mandate to serve the interests of the European Union and its citizens, and has since played a fundamental role in the development of European integration. Over the decades, the institution has evolved to adapt to the political, economic, and social changes of the continent, and has expanded its competencies to respond to new challenges.

The early years of the institution were marked by the need to establish the procedures and working structures that would enable the institution to function effectively. The institution had to develop its own rules and procedures, and had to create an administration capable of managing the increasingly complex policies of the European Union. The institution also had to establish relationships with the other European institutions and with national governments, which required the creation of cooperation and coordination mechanisms.

Throughout its history, the institution has faced numerous challenges, including economic crises, political conflicts, and changes in the balance of power between European institutions. However, the institution has demonstrated a remarkable capacity for adaptation and has managed to maintain its relevance and effectiveness in a constantly changing environment. The history of the institution is a testament to the importance of European institutions for integration and for the continent's prosperity.

### Treaties and Institutional Evolution

European treaties have been fundamental in defining the competencies and functions of the institution. The Treaty of Rome of 1957 established the foundations of the institution, and successive treaties, such as the Single European Act, the Treaty of Maastricht, the Treaty of Amsterdam, the Treaty of Nice, and the Treaty of Lisbon, have expanded and modified its competencies. Each treaty has represented a significant advance in European integration, and has allowed the institution to play a broader and more effective role in European policy.

The Treaty of Lisbon of 2009 was particularly important for the institution, as it significantly expanded its competencies in areas such as foreign policy, defense, and judicial cooperation. The treaty also established new decision-making procedures, and strengthened the democracy of European institutions by expanding the powers of the European Parliament. The institution had to adapt to these changes and had to develop new strategies to deal with the new competencies and procedures.

The institutional evolution of the institution has also been influenced by changes in Europe's political and economic context. The enlargement of the European Union to Eastern European countries, the euro crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic have been some of the challenges that the institution has had to face, and the institution has demonstrated a remarkable capacity for adaptation in responding to these challenges. The evolution of the institution is a testament to the importance of flexibility and adaptability for the effective functioning of European institutions.

Throughout its history, the institution has been led by personalities who have left a significant mark on European integration.
