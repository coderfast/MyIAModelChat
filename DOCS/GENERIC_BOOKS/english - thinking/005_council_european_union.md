The ordinary legislative procedure is the most widely used procedure in the European Union, and establishes that legislative proposals must be jointly approved by the European Parliament and the Council of the EU. This procedure grants the Council a role equal to that of the Parliament in the decision-making process, which reflects the principle of co-decision. The procedure begins with the submission of a proposal by the European Commission, which is first examined by the European Parliament. The Parliament approves its negotiating position, which is communicated to the Council. The Council examines the proposal and, if necessary, proposes amendments. If the Council and the Parliament do not agree, a conciliation procedure is initiated, in which a conciliation committee composed of representatives of both institutions seeks a common text.

The voting system of the Council of the EU is based on the qualified majority, which is the most widely used mechanism for the adoption of decisions. The qualified majority is defined as the favorable vote of at least 55% of the Member States, representing at least 65% of the EU population. This system seeks to balance the representation of large and small Member States, ensuring that decisions have the support of a broad majority of citizens and states. The qualified majority system is the Council's main decision-making mechanism, as it allows decisions to be adopted without the need for consensus, which streamlines the legislative process.

The qualified majority system has been modified several times throughout the history of the EU.
