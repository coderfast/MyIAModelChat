Solid matter exists in four fundamental states: solid, liquid, gas, and plasma. The solid state is characterized by having a defined shape and volume, since the particles (atoms, molecules, or ions) are strongly bonded and can only vibrate around fixed positions in an ordered structure called a crystal lattice. The distance between particles in a solid is very small, on the order of angstroms (10^-10 m), and the bonding forces are strong enough to maintain the rigid structure. Solids can be crystalline, with an ordered periodic structure, or amorphous, with a disordered structure similar to that of liquids but with virtually no particle mobility. Crystals are classified according to their symmetry system: cubic, hexagonal, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, and trigonal. Each symmetry system defines the relationships between the unit cell parameters, which is the minimum repeating unit of the crystalline structure.

Crystals form when a material solidifies from a liquid or deposits from a gas in an ordered manner. The crystallization process involves nucleation (formation of small seed crystals) and growth (addition of particles to the crystal). The cooling rate determines the size of the crystals: slow cooling produces large crystals, while rapid cooling produces small crystals or even amorphous materials. Natural crystals, such as quartz, pyrite, and diamonds, form in geological processes that occur over millions of years. Synthetic crystals are manufactured in laboratories for industrial applications, such as silicon crystals for electronics, sapphire crystals for lasers, and neodymium-doped garnet crystals (Nd:YAG) for medical and industrial applications.

X-ray diffraction is the primary technique for determining the crystalline structure of a material. When X-rays hit a crystal, they reflect off the atomic planes of the lattice, creating a diffraction pattern that provides information about the arrangement of atoms. Bragg's equation (n lambda = 2d sin theta) relates the wavelength of the X-rays (lambda), the spacing between atomic planes (d), and the angle of incidence (theta). X-ray crystallography has been fundamental to discovering the structure of thousands of materials, from simple salts to complex proteins. The structure of DNA was determined by Rosalind Franklin, James Watson, and Francis Crick in 1953 using X-ray diffraction. Modern crystallographers use synchrotron light sources that produce high-intensity X-ray beams to resolve increasingly complex crystalline structures.

The mechanical properties of solids, such as hardness, elasticity, and strength, depend on the nature of the bonds between their particles. Ionic bonds, such as those in sodium chloride (common salt), form when a metal transfers electrons to a nonmetal, creating ions with opposite charges that attract each other electrostatically. Ionic solids are brittle because the repulsion between ions of the same sign when displaced by one crystal plane weakens the structure. They have high melting points (NaCl: 801°C) and are good electrical insulators in solid state, but conduct electricity when melted or dissolved in water. Covalent bonds, such as those in diamond and graphene, form when atoms share electrons. Covalent solids are extremely hard (diamond: 10 on the Mohs scale) and have very high melting points (diamond: 3550°C). Graphene, a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice, is the strongest material ever measured, with a tensile strength 200 times greater than that of steel.

Metallic bonds, such as those in copper, aluminum, and iron, form when the valence electrons of metals delocalize and form an "electron sea" that holds the ionic nuclei together. This structure allows electrons to move freely, which explains the high electrical and thermal conductivity of metals. Metals are ductile (can be drawn into wires) and malleable (can be hammered into sheets) because atomic planes can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bond. Van der Waals bonds, such as those in dry ice (solid CO2) and solid nitrogen, are weak electrostatic forces between polar or induced molecules. Solids with van der Waals bonds are soft and have low melting points (solid CO2: -56.6°C). Hydrogen bonds, interactions between hydrogen atoms and electronegative atoms (O, N, F), are important in ice, where they create an open crystalline structure that makes solid water less dense than liquid water.

The mechanical properties of solids determine their behavior when external forces are applied. Hardness is the resistance of a solid to being scratched, and is measured on the Mohs scale (1 to 10) or the Vickers scale. Diamond is the hardest natural material (10 on Mohs), followed by corundum (9, which includes rubies and sapphires) and topaz (8). Steel has a Young's modulus of approximately 200 GPa, while rubber has a modulus of only 0.01-0.1 GPa. Plasticity is the property opposite to elasticity: plastic materials deform permanently when their elastic limit is exceeded. Brittleness is the tendency to fracture without significant plastic deformation, and is common in ceramic materials and glasses.

Tensile strength is the maximum stress a material can withstand before breaking, and is measured in pascals (Pa) or newtons per square millimeter (N/mm^2 = MPa). Structural steel has a tensile strength of 400-550 MPa, titanium of 900-1200 MPa, and carbon fiber up to 7000 MPa. Tough materials, such as carbon steel, are important in applications where impacts are expected, such as bridge and vehicle construction. The liquid state is characterized by having a defined volume but no shape of its own, adapting to the shape of the container that holds it. Particles in a liquid are close together, like in a solid, but they are not fixed in defined positions and can slide over each other, allowing the liquid to flow. Intermolecular forces in a liquid are strong enough to keep particles together, but not strong enough to fix them in fixed positions. The structure of a liquid is disordered like that of an amorphous solid, but with greater particle mobility. The intermolecular distance in a liquid is similar to that of a solid, but the long-range order is much less. Liquids have short ranges of local order, where neighboring particles maintain an approximately regular arrangement, but this order is lost at greater distances.

Viscosity is the property that measures a liquid's resistance to flow, and depends on temperature and the nature of the liquid. Viscous liquids like honey, glycerin, and motor oil flow slowly because their molecules have strong intermolecular interactions that resist relative movement. Low-viscosity liquids like water, alcohol, and gasoline flow rapidly because their molecules have weaker interactions. Viscosity decreases with temperature because increased kinetic energy allows molecules to overcome attractive forces more easily. The Arrhenius equation describes this dependence: eta = A exp(Ea/RT), where Ea is the activation energy of flow. The viscosity of polymeric liquids is especially complex because it depends on molecular weight, temperature, and shear rate. Newtonian liquids have constant viscosity independent of shear rate, while non-Newtonian liquids (like blood, paint, and mayonnaise) change their viscosity with shear rate.

Surface tension is the force that acts on the surface of a liquid and tends to minimize its area. It is due to the fact that surface molecules have fewer neighbors than interior molecules, and therefore experience a net force toward the interior of the liquid. Surface tension is measured in newtons per meter (N/m) and varies by liquid: water has a surface tension of 0.0728 N/m at 20°C, mercury of 0.4865 N/m, and ethyl alcohol of 0.0223 N/m. Surface tension is responsible for the formation of spherical droplets (a sphere has the smallest area for a given volume), capillarity (the rise of liquids in narrow tubes), and the flotation of light objects on the water surface. Detergents reduce the surface tension of water, allowing it to wet surfaces better and clean more efficiently. Surfactants are substances that reduce surface tension and are used in multiple applications, from detergents to the production of emulsions and foams.

Surface phenomena are processes that occur at the interface between a liquid and another medium (air, another liquid, or a solid). Surface tension is the fundamental cause of most of these phenomena. Wetting is the ability of a liquid to maintain contact with a solid surface, and is described by the contact angle. Capillarity is the phenomenon by which a liquid rises or falls in a narrow tube (capillary) due to the combination of surface tension and adhesion. In a glass capillary, water rises because it adheres to the glass walls (adhesion > cohesion), creating a curved surface (meniscus) that generates a negative pressure that sucks the water upward. The gaseous state is characterized by having neither a defined shape nor volume, since particles move freely at high speed and occupy all available volume. The distance between particles in a gas is large compared to the size of the particles themselves, and intermolecular forces are negligible except during collisions. The pressure of a gas is the result of molecular collisions against the container walls, and is directly proportional to the density and temperature of the gas. The temperature of a gas is directly related to the average kinetic energy of its molecules: the higher the temperature, the greater the molecular speed. Brownian motion, discovered by Robert Brown in 1827, is the random motion of small particles suspended in a fluid, caused by collisions with molecules of the surrounding fluid. The behavior of gases is described by the ideal gas law: PV = nRT, where P is pressure, V is volume, n is the number of moles, R is the ideal gas constant (8.314 J/(mol K)), and T is the absolute temperature. This law is valid for gases at high temperatures and low pressures, where intermolecular interactions are negligible. At high pressures or low temperatures, real gases deviate from ideal behavior, and more complex equations of state such as the van der Waals equation are used: (P + a/V^2)(V - b) = nRT, where a and b are constants that depend on the gas and represent intermolecular interactions and the volume occupied by molecules, respectively. The van der Waals equation better describes the behavior of real gases, especially near the critical point where the gas and liquid become indistinguishable.

The laws of thermodynamics govern the behavior of gases. The first law states that the internal energy of a gas equals the heat supplied minus the work done by the gas: Delta U = Q - W. For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature. Important thermodynamic processes include the isothermal process (constant temperature), isobaric process (constant pressure), isochoric process (constant volume), and adiabatic process (no heat exchange). The Carnot cycle defines the maximum theoretical efficiency of any heat engine: eta = 1 - T_cold/T_hot. Entropy is a measure of the disorder of a system, and the second law states that the entropy of an isolated system always increases or remains constant. These laws are fundamental to thermodynamics, chemistry, and engineering, and govern everything from gas behavior in engines to the evolution of the universe.

Gases mix spontaneously and completely, occupying the total volume of the container that holds them. Dalton's law of partial pressures states that the total pressure of a gas mixture equals the sum of the partial pressures of each component gas: P_total = P_1 + P_2 + ... + P_n. The partial pressure of each gas is the pressure it would exert if it alone occupied the total volume. Amagat's law states that the total volume of a gas mixture equals the sum of the partial volumes of each gas. These laws are fundamental to understanding the behavior of gas mixtures in applications such as respiration (air is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, and CO2), combustion (fuel and air mixtures), and refrigeration systems (refrigerant mixtures).

Gas diffusion is the process by which molecules of one gas mix with those of another gas due to their random motion. Compressible fluids are those whose density varies significantly with pressure, such as gases. Unlike liquids, which are virtually incompressible for most applications, gases are easily compressed when pressure is applied. The compressibility of a gas is quantified by the bulk modulus, which is the ratio of the change in pressure to the relative change in volume. For an ideal gas, compressibility equals the absolute pressure. The speed of sound in a gas depends on temperature and molecular mass: v = sqrt(gamma R T / M), where gamma is the ratio of specific heats (1.4 for air), R is the ideal gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, and M is the molar mass. Compressible fluid dynamics is fundamental to the aerodynamics of supersonic aircraft, rockets, and gas turbines. The Mach number is the ratio of an object's speed to the speed of sound: Ma = v/c. Subsonic flows (Ma < 1) are those in which the fluid moves slower than sound, and compressibility effects are small. Transonic flows (Ma = 0.8-1.2) are those in which the fluid speed is close to the speed of sound, and compressibility effects are significant. Supersonic flows (Ma > 1) are those in which the fluid moves faster than sound, and shock waves form. Supersonic aircraft like the Concorde operated at Mach 2, and spacecraft experience hypersonic flows during atmospheric reentry. Plasma is the fourth state of matter, and is the most abundant state in the observable universe: it is estimated that more than 99% of the visible matter in the universe is in the plasma state. A plasma is an ionized gas in which electrons have separated from atomic nuclei, creating a mixture of positive ions and free electrons. Unlike gases, plasmas conduct electricity and respond strongly to magnetic and electric fields. Plasma forms when a gas is heated to temperatures high enough to ionize atoms, or when subjected to intense electric fields. Plasmas are classified as hot (temperatures above 10^6 K, as in stars and fusion reactors), cold (temperatures near ambient, as in fluorescent lamps), and space charge (where electrons and ions have different temperatures). Electron density in a plasma can vary from 10^6 electrons per cubic meter in the Earth's ionosphere to 10^32 electrons per cubic meter in the interior of stars.

The behavior of plasmas is much more complex than that of gases, due to electromagnetic interactions between charged particles. Plasmas exhibit unique phenomena such as plasma oscillations (electron density fluctuations), electromagnetic waves (that propagate through the plasma), and magnetohydrodynamic turbulence (chaotic plasma motion driven by magnetic fields). Plasmas are described by magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equations, which combine the Navier-Stokes equations of fluid dynamics with Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism. Plasma confinement is one of the great challenges of modern physics, since hot plasmas tend to escape magnetic or material confinements. Researchers use donut-shaped (tokamaks), spiral (stellators), and z-pinch magnetic fields to confine fusion plasmas at temperatures of millions of degrees.
