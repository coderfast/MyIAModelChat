The Treaty establishing the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC), signed on 18 April 1951 in Paris, constitutes the founding document of the European integration project. This treaty was the direct result of the destruction and suffering caused by the Second World War, which left Europe in ruins and demonstrated the dangers of exacerbated nationalism. In this context, European leaders sought formulas to guarantee peace and prosperity through economic cooperation and the integration of strategic industries that had been used as instruments of war. Coal and steel were the essential materials for the armaments industry, so their joint control between France and Germany, the two nations that had fought three wars in less than eighty years, constituted a peace guarantee of enormous symbolic and practical value.

The initiative came from the then French Foreign Minister, Robert Schuman, who on 9 May 1950 presented a historic declaration proposing the creation of a European coal and steel community. The Schuman Declaration, as it is known, established that France proposed to place the entirety of its French coal and steel production under a common European authority, in an organization open to other European countries. This proposal did not arise in a vacuum, but was the result of long negotiations and debates between the French, German, Italian, and the three Benelux governments (Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg). The Schuman Plan was elaborated in close collaboration with the French politician Jean Monnet, who is considered one of the founding fathers of Europe and who developed the functionalist method that inspired the ECSC.

The international context also decisively influenced the creation of the ECSC. The Cold War had begun, and Europe was in a position of economic and military weakness vis-a-vis the two superpowers, the United States and the Soviet Union. The United States, through the Marshall Plan, had provided economic aid for the reconstruction of Europe, but also pressured Europeans to cooperate with each other and create a strong economic bloc that could serve as a counterweight to Soviet communism. In this sense, the ECSC also responded to a strategic need: strengthening Western Europe as a bulwark against Soviet expansion. The creation of a coal and steel community not only sought Franco-German peace, but also the economic recovery of Europe and its affirmation as an international actor.

The six founding countries were France, West Germany (the Federal Republic of Germany), Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, known as "The Six." These countries shared a series of characteristics that facilitated the negotiation: they were parliamentary democracies, they had open market economies, and their coal and steel industries were interconnected by commercial and financial ties. The signing of the treaty took place in the Hall of Mirrors at the Quai d'Orsay Palace in Paris, a place with profound symbolic significance given that in the same hall the Treaty of Versailles had been signed in 1919, which had imposed harsh conditions on Germany after the First World War. The choice of this venue underscored the change of approach: from vengeance to reconciliation, from confrontation to cooperation.

The ECSC Treaty established a supranational organization endowed with its own institutions, specific competences, and a decision-making system that broke with the traditional model of intergovernmental cooperation. The main institutions of the ECSC were the High Authority, the Council of Ministers, the Parliamentary Assembly, and the Court of Justice. The High Authority, initially presided over by Jean Monnet, was the executive body of the community and had significant powers to take binding decisions in matters of coal and steel policy. This institution represented the first example of a supranational authority in Europe, with the capacity to adopt decisions without the need for unanimous approval of national governments.

The ECSC Council of Ministers was composed of representatives of the governments of the member states, and its main function was to coordinate the policy of the High Authority with the general policies of national governments. The Council did not have veto power over the decisions of the High Authority, but could request it to reconsider its decisions and could adopt opinions on matters of general policy. The Parliamentary Assembly, which would later become the European Parliament, was composed of representatives designated by national parliaments and had a supervisory and control function over the High Authority, although its powers were initially limited. The Court of Justice, for its part, guaranteed the uniform interpretation and application of the treaty, and had jurisdiction to resolve disputes between member states, the institutions of the community, and, in certain cases, private individuals.

The treaty had an initial duration of fifty years, which meant it would automatically expire on 23 July 2002, a clause that reflected the provisional and experimental nature of the project, but which also served as an incentive for member states to seek broader cooperation formulas before the treaty expired.

One of the most important innovations of the ECSC Treaty was the principle of supranationality, which established that the decisions of the community institutions were binding on member states, their governments, and their citizens. This principle broke with the traditional model of international relations, in which treaties only bound states and required the unanimous approval of governments to be modified. In the ECSC, on the contrary, decisions were adopted by majority, and member states were obliged to comply with them. This principle of supranationality laid the foundations for the entire subsequent European legal order, including European law with supremacy over national law and the direct effect of European norms.

The Treaty of Rome, signed on 25 March 1957, constitutes the second fundamental pillar of the European construction and represented an ambitious expansion of the project initiated with the ECSC. While the ECSC had demonstrated that economic integration was viable and beneficial, its scope was limited to two specific industrial sectors. This treaty not only expanded the scope of integration, but also deepened its supranational nature, endowing European institutions with broader powers and establishing a more complete legal framework for economic cooperation among Western European countries.
