The Economic and Monetary Union (EMU) represents one of the boldest and most complex achievements of the European integration process, involving the transfer of a significant portion of the economic and monetary sovereignty of the Member States to a supranational level. The historical roots of the EMU date back to the Treaties of Rome of 1957, which established the European Economic Community with the objective of creating a common market among its Member States. However, the idea of a European monetary union did not begin to take shape until the 1960s, when monetary imbalances and exchange rate crises highlighted the need for greater coordination of national monetary policies. The system of fixed exchange rates established by the Bretton Woods Agreements in 1944 provided a framework of international monetary stability that facilitated trade and investment between European countries, but the monetary tensions of the 1960s highlighted the limits of this system and the need for greater monetary integration among European countries.

The Werner Report, presented in 1970 by Luxembourg Prime Minister Pierre Werner, proposed the creation of a complete economic and monetary union in the European Economic Community by 1980. The Werner Report established the principles of a monetary union, including the irreversibility of exchange rates, the free movement of capital, the coordination of economic policies, and the creation of a European central bank. Although the Werner Report was not fully implemented due to the economic and monetary crises of the 1970s, it laid the conceptual foundations for the future monetary union and established the principles that would guide the process of European monetary integration in the following decades. The European Monetary System (EMS), created in 1979, was an important step towards the monetary union by establishing a mechanism of fixed but adjustable exchange rates between European currencies, providing monetary stability and facilitating intra-community trade and investment.

The Delors Report, presented in 1989 by European Commission President Jacques Delors, was the founding document of the current Economic and Monetary Union. The Delors Report proposed a monetary union in three phases that would lay the foundations for the creation of a single European currency. The first phase, which began in July 1990, involved the free movement of capital between the Member States and greater coordination of monetary policies. The second phase, which began with the signing of the Maastricht Treaty in 1992, included the creation of the European Monetary Institute, the precursor to the European Central Bank, and the establishment of convergence criteria that Member States had to meet to participate in the monetary union. The third phase, which began in January 1999 with the introduction of the euro as an accounting currency, involved the transfer of monetary policy competences to the European Central Bank and the replacement of national currencies by the euro.

The Maastricht Treaty, signed in 1992 and entered into force in 1993, established the legal framework of the Economic and Monetary Union and defined the convergence criteria that Member States had to meet to participate in the monetary union. The convergence criteria included price stability, with an inflation rate no more than 1.5 percentage points above that of the three best-performing Member States in terms of inflation; sound public finances, with a government deficit no more than 3 percent of GDP and public debt no more than 60 percent of GDP; exchange rate stability, with participation in the EMS for at least two years without tensions or devaluations; and interest rate convergence, with a long-term interest rate no more than 2 percentage points above that of the three best-performing Member States in terms of inflation. These convergence criteria were aimed at ensuring that the Member States participating in the monetary union had an economic and financial situation compatible with the stability of the euro area.

The European Central Bank (ECB) is the central institution of the Economic and Monetary Union and has the primary objective of maintaining price stability in the euro area. The ECB was created in 1998 as the successor to the European Monetary Institute and is headquartered in Frankfurt am Main, Germany. The European System of Central Banks (ESCB) comprises the ECB and the national central banks of the Member States of the European Union, regardless of whether they participate in the euro area or not. The Member States that do not participate in the euro area, such as Sweden, Denmark, Poland, and Hungary, maintain their national central banks as part of the ESCB but do not participate in the monetary policy decision-making of the ECB's Governing Council. The ESCB is responsible for the monetary policy of the euro area and for the provision of banking services to the financial institutions of the Member States.
