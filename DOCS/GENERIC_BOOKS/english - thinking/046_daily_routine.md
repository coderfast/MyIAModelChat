The morning represents the start of a new daily cycle, a moment when the body and mind transition from nighttime rest to daytime activity. Waking up is a gradual process that involves multiple physiological systems of the organism, from the central nervous system to the endocrine system, which regulate the transition between sleep and wakefulness. The quality of waking depends on multiple factors, including the duration and quality of nighttime sleep, bedtime, the bedroom environment (brightness, noise, temperature), and personal habits. Many people use electronic alarm clocks or smartphone alarms to ensure they wake up at the desired time, although the ideal is to wake up naturally without needing an alarm, which indicates a healthy sleep rhythm. The first moments after waking are crucial for mood and productivity throughout the day, as they set the emotional and cognitive tone for the hours that follow. Some people need a period of gradual adaptation, moving slowly and allowing the body to activate progressively, while others prefer to get up immediately and start activity right away. Exposure to natural light after waking is especially important, as sunlight sends signals to the brain that help regulate the circadian rhythm and maintain a healthy sleep-wake cycle. The morning routine should be designed to allow a gentle and gradual wake-up, avoiding stress and rushing that can negatively affect the day's performance.

Despite the importance of breakfast, many people make habitual mistakes that reduce its health benefits. However, skipping breakfast causes a drop in blood glucose levels that affects cognitive and physical performance, and can lead to overeating during the rest of the day. The active break, which consists of getting up, stretching muscles, and moving the body every thirty to sixty minutes, is fundamental to prevent the negative effects of occupational sedentarism. Regular physical exercise, both during and outside working hours, contributes to maintaining musculoskeletal health, reducing stress, and improving cognitive performance. Companies have the responsibility to provide an ergonomic work environment and promote the health of their workers through ergonomics training, provision of adequate furniture, and implementation of workplace wellness programs.

The night is the period of the day dedicated to rest and recovery of the body, and sleep is its main manifestation. Sleep is a fundamental physiological process for health, as during sleep the body performs essential functions such as tissue repair, memory consolidation, hormonal regulation, and elimination of brain toxins. The sleep cycle is composed of different phases that repeat in cycles of approximately ninety minutes: light sleep (phases 1 and 2), deep sleep (phase 3), and REM sleep (rapid eye movement). Each phase has a specific function: deep sleep is essential for physical recovery and growth hormone release, while REM sleep is fundamental for emotional processing, creativity, and memory consolidation. The recommended sleep duration varies by age: children need between ten and fourteen hours, teenagers between eight and ten hours, adults between seven and nine hours, and elderly people between six and eight hours. Circadian rhythms, regulated by the body's internal biological clock, determine periods of greater drowsiness and alertness throughout the day. The secretion of melatonin, the hormone that regulates the sleep-wake cycle, increases with darkness and decreases with light, which explains why we feel drowsy at dusk and more alert during the day. Healthy sleep habits include going to bed and waking up at the same time every day, creating an optimal sleep environment (darkness, silence, cool temperature), and avoiding stimulants such as caffeine and electronic screens before bed.

The recommended sleep duration varies by age: children from four to twelve months need between twelve and fifteen hours, children from one to two years between eleven and fourteen hours, preschoolers from three to five years between ten and thirteen hours, school-age children from six to thirteen years between nine and eleven hours, teenagers from fourteen to seventeen years between eight and ten hours, and adults from eighteen to sixty-four years between seven and nine hours. The benefits of hobbies for mental health include stress reduction and relaxation (as immersing ourselves in an enjoyable activity reduces cortisol levels and activates the parasympathetic nervous system), mood improvement (the production of dopamine associated with pleasure and achievement), cognitive stimulation (many hobbies challenge the mind and foster problem-solving), identity construction (hobbies define part of who we are and how we express ourselves), social connection (many hobbies are practiced in the company of others, facilitating the creation of bonds), and the sense of competence and mastery (progressive improvement in a hobby provides confidence and satisfaction). Incorporating a hobby into the daily routine requires identifying an activity we are passionate about, dedicating a regular period of time to it (even if brief), protecting that time from interruptions and obligations, and allowing ourselves to enjoy the process without the pressure of performance or comparison with others. For people who do not have a defined hobby, exploring new activities (workshops, courses, events, experiences) is the best way to discover what type of activity provides us with pleasure and satisfaction.

Volunteering is the selfless dedication of time and effort to a cause or entity that works for the benefit of the community, and is a form of meaningful leisure that combines social connection, learning, expression of personal values, skill development, and contribution to collective well-being. Benefits of volunteering for mental health include reduced depression and loneliness (as it provides social connection, purpose, and belonging), improved self-esteem and self-efficacy (by contributing positively to others' lives), expanded social network (by meeting people with similar values and interests), learning new skills and acquiring professional experience, and the satisfaction of contributing to a cause we care about. Volunteering options are very diverse and adapt to different interests, skills, and availabilities: social volunteering (accompanying older adults, supporting at-risk families, attending to homeless people and refugees), educational volunteering (tutoring, literacy, language teaching, school support), environmental volunteering (cleaning natural spaces, animal care, urban gardens, environmental education), cultural volunteering (museums, libraries, cultural events, heritage), health volunteering (accompanying in hospitals, blood donation, patient support), and emergency and civil protection volunteering. Incorporating volunteering into the daily or weekly routine requires identifying a cause that motivates us, seeking organizations that need volunteers in our area, and dedicating a regular period of time (from a few hours a month to several days a week) to volunteering activity. Family volunteering, which involves all household members in community contribution activities, is a particularly valuable way to transmit values of solidarity, empathy, and responsibility.
