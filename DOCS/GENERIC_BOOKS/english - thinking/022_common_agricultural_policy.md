# The Common Agricultural Policy of the European Union

## General Introduction

The Common Agricultural Policy, known by its acronym CAP, is one of the oldest, most complex, and most prominent policies of the European Union. Since its establishment in the Treaty of Rome in 1957 until the most recent reforms of the 2023-2027 period, the CAP has undergone a continuous transformation that reflects the changing economic, social, environmental, and political challenges facing European agriculture. The CAP represents approximately one-third of the total European Union budget, making it the EU policy with the largest financial allocation and a fundamental instrument for ensuring food security, maintaining agricultural activity across the entire European territory, and promoting sustainable rural development. Over more than six decades, the CAP has been the subject of numerous reforms that have profoundly transformed its design, instruments, and objectives, moving from a system based on guaranteed prices and production subsidies to a model of direct payments and rural development measures.

Agriculture has always held a privileged place in the European construction, not only for its economic importance but also for its symbolic and cultural value. The founding fathers of Europe considered food security an indispensable condition for peace and stability, and believed that agriculture needed special protection to survive in an increasingly competitive global market. The CAP was designed to achieve three fundamental objectives that remain relevant today: increase agricultural productivity to ensure a fair standard of living for the agricultural population, stabilize agricultural markets to ensure food security, and ensure the availability of food at reasonable prices for consumers. These objectives have been gradually adapted and expanded over the decades to include environmental protection, sustainable rural development, food quality, and the competitiveness of the agricultural sector.

The history of the CAP is a story of successive reforms that have profoundly transformed its design and functioning. The first decades of the CAP were characterized by an approach based on guaranteed prices and production subsidies, which led to agricultural surpluses known as mountains of butter and lakes of milk. These surpluses generated enormous costs for the European budget and distorted international agricultural markets. Starting in the 1990s, the CAP underwent deep reforms that gradually transitioned from an approach based on guaranteed prices to one based on direct payments to farmers and rural development measures. These reforms sought to reconcile agricultural production objectives with environmental protection, financial sustainability, and trade openness.

In this book, we will analyze the CAP in depth and its evolution over the decades. We will examine the history of the CAP from its origins to the most recent reforms, analyze its fundamental objectives and principles, study the agricultural subsidy system and its impact on production and markets, examine the common agricultural market and its functioning, study the CAP reforms and their consequences, examine the relationship between the CAP and rural development, analyze the environmental dimension of the CAP, and evaluate the challenges facing European agricultural policy in the future.

## Chapter 1: History of the CAP

### The Origins and Creation of the CAP (1957-1962)

The Common Agricultural Policy was established in the Treaty of Rome in 1957 as one of the common policies of the European Economic Community. Articles 38 to 47 of the Treaty of Rome established the objectives and instruments of the CAP, which would become the cornerstone of European integration during the first decades of its existence. The objectives of the CAP, as set out in the treaty, included increasing agricultural productivity, ensuring a fair standard of living for the agricultural population, stabilizing markets, ensuring food availability, and guaranteeing reasonable prices for consumers. These objectives reflected the concerns of the time, marked by postwar food scarcity and the need to rebuild European agriculture after the devastation of World War II.

The historical context in which the CAP emerged was marked by several particularly relevant circumstances. First, European agriculture had been devastated by World War II, and it was necessary to rebuild the agricultural sector to ensure food security for a population that still remembered the famines and food shortages during the years of conflict. Second, European agriculture was characterized by significantly lower productivity compared to American agriculture, and it was necessary to modernize the sector to close that gap and ensure European competitiveness in international markets. Third, European agriculture was fragmented into millions of small family farms that needed protection from the competition of large international producers with much greater economies of scale. Fourth, agriculture had enormous political weight in all countries of the European Economic Community, since a significant portion of the population still lived in rural areas and depended directly or indirectly on agriculture for their livelihood and way of life.

The CAP was launched in 1962, after complex negotiations among the six founding countries. The main architects of the CAP were the Commissioner for Agriculture, the French Sicco Mansholt, and the agriculture ministers of the Member States, who negotiated for months to reach an agreement satisfactory to all parties. The CAP was based on an intervention price system that guaranteed European farmers a minimum price for their products, regardless of market conditions. This guaranteed price system was complemented by export aids that allowed European products to compete in international markets, and by tariff protections that prevented the import of cheaper products from outside the Community. The guaranteed price system was the engine of European agricultural growth during the first decades of the CAP, as it provided farmers with income security that enabled them to invest in modernizing their farms and progressively increase their productivity.

The first common market organization schemes were quickly established for the main agricultural products. In 1962, common schemes were created for wheat, oilseed crops, rice, fruits and vegetables, beef, sheep and goat meat, eggs, poultry, wine, and dairy products. Each common scheme established intervention prices, quality standards, import and export procedures, and specific financial aid for that product. These common schemes sought to ensure that European farmers could sell their products at stable and predictable prices, regardless of international market fluctuations. The initial success of these schemes was remarkable: European agricultural production grew rapidly, prices stabilized, and farmers' incomes improved significantly.

The financing of the CAP was established through the European Guidance and Guarantee Fund (EAGGF), created in 1962 as the financial instrument of agricultural policy. The EAGGF was divided into two sections: the Guarantee Section, which financed market interventions and export aids, and the Guidance Section, which financed structural development measures and farm modernization aids. The financing of the CAP was fed by the Community's own resources, giving the policy a financial autonomy that did not depend on voluntary contributions from Member States. This financing system was strengthened over the decades and remains the basis of the European agricultural budget today, although with significant modifications in its design and distribution.

### The Growth Period and Surpluses (1962-1985)

The CAP's guaranteed price system had impressive initial success in terms of increasing agricultural productivity and European farmers' incomes. Agricultural production in the Community grew rapidly during the 1960s and 1970s, driven by guaranteed prices, farm modernization, mechanization of production processes, introduction of high-yield varieties, and increasing use of fertilizers and phytosanitary products. European farmers responded to the incentives of guaranteed prices by continuously increasing their production, leading to unprecedented agricultural surpluses in European history. These surpluses manifested in forms that have become famous in European economic history: mountains of butter, lakes of milk, walls of oranges, and oceans of wine.

Agricultural surpluses generated enormous costs for the European budget, as the Community was obliged to buy products that were not sold on the market at the guaranteed prices set by the policy. Storage of surpluses required increasingly large and expensive storage facilities, and many of the stored products ended up deteriorating or being donated through the United Nations World Food Programme. The costs of agricultural surpluses consumed a growing proportion of the European budget, creating significant tensions with national budgets and limiting resources available for other European policies, such as regional policy, social policy, and research and development policy. These budgetary tensions were one of the main drivers of the CAP reforms that began in the 1980s.

Guaranteed prices also significantly distorted international agricultural markets, as European prices were systematically above world prices, incentivizing European production above what was needed and discouraging imports. Export aids allowed European products to be sold below their actual production cost in international markets, severely harming producers in developing countries who could not compete with European subsidies. These distortions in international trade created significant tensions with the United States, which considered that the CAP distorted world agricultural trade and harmed American exporters. International trade negotiations, within the GATT and later the WTO, were largely driven by the desire to reduce distortions caused by the CAP and similar agricultural policies adopted by other developed countries.

The CAP also generated significant environmental problems that began to be recognized from the 1970s onward. High guaranteed prices incentivized farmers to continuously intensify their production, leading to excessive use of chemical fertilizers and phytosanitary products, contamination of groundwater and surface water by nitrates and pesticides, soil degradation through erosion and compaction, and dramatic loss of biodiversity in the European rural landscape. Agricultural intensification also led to the disappearance of traditional landscapes that had been an integral part of the European cultural heritage for centuries, such as hedgerows, permanent meadows, crop mosaics, and pastoral exploitation systems. These environmental problems generated growing concern among European public opinion, environmental organizations, and European institutions themselves, and laid the groundwork for incorporating environmental concerns into subsequent CAP reforms.

### The Crisis and Early Reforms (1985-1992)

The crisis of agricultural surpluses, the rising costs of the CAP, international trade pressures, and growing environmental concern led to the first significant reforms of the policy in the 1980s. In 1984, a milk quota system was introduced to limit milk production, which was one of the sectors with the largest surpluses and highest costs for the European budget. Milk quotas established a maximum limit on the amount of milk each farm could produce without incurring significant economic penalties, seeking to progressively reduce production and accumulated surpluses. This reform was considerably controversial, as it directly limited farmers' freedom to decide how much to produce, but it had moderate success in reducing milk surpluses and associated costs to the European budget.

In 1988, a package of measures known as the McSharry Gymnasium was introduced, seeking to control the growth of agricultural spending by introducing limits on the growth of agricultural aids and establishing compensatory aids for farmers who voluntarily reduced their production. This package was the direct prelude to the 1992 MacSharry reform, which was the most important and transformative reform of the CAP since its creation in the 1960s. The MacSharry reform, carried out by the Commissioner for Agriculture, the Irish Ray MacSharry, introduced a fundamental change in the design and philosophy of the CAP: the decisive step from a system based on guaranteed prices to a system based on direct payments to farmers.

Intervention prices were significantly reduced to gradually bring them closer to world prices, and compensatory direct payments were introduced calculated on the basis of each farmer's historical production to maintain their incomes despite the price reduction. This reform was negotiated during several intense months between Member States, the European Commission, and agricultural sector representatives, and was adopted in May 1992 after passionate parliamentary debates in both the European Parliament and national parliaments. The MacSharry reform was a turning point in the history of the CAP, as it changed the fundamental paradigm of European agricultural policy and laid the groundwork for all subsequent reforms.

The main instruments of the MacSharry reform included a 35% reduction in intervention prices for cereals, a 15% reduction in the milk price over five years, a reduction in the beef price through adjustments to export aids, and the introduction of compensatory direct payments per ton produced for cereals, per head of cattle, and per hectare of fodder crops. The compensatory direct payments partially offset the difference between the reduced prices and previous prices, allowing farmers to maintain their incomes without depending exclusively on market prices. This reform also laid the groundwork for incorporating environmental concerns into the CAP, as access to direct payments was conditional on compliance with basic environmental standards known as cross-compliance requirements.

### The Agenda 2000 Reform and Subsequent Reforms

The Agenda 2000 reform, adopted in March 1999 under the French presidency of the European Council, was the next major reform of the CAP and continued the path initiated by MacSharry. This reform, negotiated by the Commissioner for Agriculture, the German Franz Fischler, deepened the paradigm shift by further reducing intervention prices and increasing direct payments to compensate farmers for price reductions. Agenda 2000 also introduced the innovative concept of rural development as the second pillar of the CAP, complementing the first pillar of direct payments and market interventions with a second pillar of rural development measures co-financed by the European Agricultural Guarantee Fund and the Structural Funds. The second pillar of the CAP sought to address the specific challenges of rural development, such as the progressive depopulation of the countryside, the accelerated aging of the rural population, the need for economic diversification in rural areas, and the protection and restoration of the rural environment.

The 2003 reform, known as the decoupling reform, was the next transformative advance in the evolution of the CAP. This reform, also led by Commissioner Franz Fischler, completely decoupled direct payments from agricultural production, establishing a payment system based on historical reference criteria rather than each farmer's current or past production. Farmers received a fixed annual amount calculated on the basis of their historical production in a reference period, regardless of what they produced in the future, giving them complete freedom to decide what to cultivate according to market conditions and their own comparative advantages. This reform was a fundamental step toward agriculture more guided by market signals and more environmentally sustainable, as it eliminated direct incentives for overproduction and allowed farmers to adapt their production to market demand.

The 2003 reform also introduced the cross-compliance system, which linked access to all direct payments to compliance with a series of environmental, public health, animal welfare, and good agricultural and environmental soil condition standards. Non-compliance with these standards could result in proportional or total reductions in direct payments, constituting a powerful incentive for farmers to improve their environmental practices. Cross-compliance represented a compromise between advocates of a freer agriculture and those demanding greater environmental responsibility from the agricultural sector, and became a permanent element of CAP design.

The 2013 CAP reform, which defined the framework for the 2014-2020 budget period, introduced significant new innovations in the design and implementation of the policy. This reform, known as the Green CAP or greening, conditioned a substantial portion of direct payments on compliance with three mandatory environmental requirements known as greening requirements. These requirements included crop diversification on farms larger than 10 hectares, maintenance of existing permanent pastures, and reservation of at least 5% of the cultivable area of farms larger than 15 hectares for areas of ecological interest that could include riparian protection strips, hedgerows, isolated trees, fallow land, ponds, and nitrogen-fixing crops. The 2013 reform also significantly strengthened the second pillar of the CAP, expanding the range of available rural development measures and increasing their financial allocation through the possibility of voluntary transfers between pillars.

## Chapter 2: The Objectives of the CAP

### Fundamental Objectives Established in the Treaties

The objectives of the CAP are established in Article 39 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, which states that the common agricultural policy has as its objectives: to increase agricultural productivity, thereby ensuring a fair standard of living for the agricultural population; to guarantee the stability of markets; to guarantee the security of supplies; and to ensure that food is available at reasonable prices for consumers. These four fundamental objectives have been the cornerstone of the CAP since its creation and have served as a guide for all reforms and adaptations of the policy over the decades. To these original objectives, others of equal importance have been progressively added, such as environmental protection and ecosystem quality, sustainable rural development, food quality and safety, animal welfare, territorial cohesion, and contribution to the fight against climate change.

The objective of increasing agricultural productivity has been one of the fundamental pillars of the CAP since its earliest creation. The CAP has contributed significantly to increasing the productivity of European agriculture, which has multiplied several times since the 1960s. This spectacular increase in productivity has been the result of continuous investment in farm modernization, advanced mechanization of production processes, constant genetic improvement of crops and livestock through selection and breeding techniques, intensive but increasingly rationalized use of fertilizers and phytosanitary products, and widespread dissemination of advanced agricultural techniques transferred from scientific research to farms through agricultural advisory and extension systems. However, the increase in productivity has also generated significant problems that have required corrective interventions, such as chronic agricultural surpluses, systematic overproduction, and cumulative negative environmental impacts.

The objective of ensuring the stability of agricultural markets seeks to protect farmers from price fluctuations inherent to the nature of agricultural activity. Agriculture is subject to a multitude of risks that can cause dramatic price and income fluctuations, including climatic risks such as droughts, floods, frosts, and hailstorms; biological risks such as pests, plant diseases, and livestock epizootics; economic risks such as demand fluctuations, input price variations, and financial crises; and geopolitical risks such as international conflicts, trade sanctions, and disruptions to global supply chains. The CAP has developed a wide range of instruments to stabilize agricultural markets and protect farmers from these risks, including intervention prices, market regulation mechanisms, subsidized agricultural insurance, and mutual aid systems to cope with crises.

The objective of ensuring the security of food supplies seeks to ensure that Europe can feed its population autonomously, resiliently, and sustainably, without excessive dependence on external imports that could be compromised by international crises. Food security is a first-order strategic objective for the European Union, as it ensures food independence and the capacity to respond to unexpected crises such as global pandemics, armed conflicts that disrupt global supply chains, large-scale natural disasters, or geopolitical disruptions. The CAP has contributed decisively to maintaining sufficient internal agricultural production to cover the basic food needs of the European population, and in numerous productive sectors Europe is a net exporter of food products, reflecting the strength and competitiveness of the European agricultural sector at the global level.

The objective of ensuring that food is available at reasonable prices for consumers represents the fundamental balance between the interests of producers and consumers, and has been a constant in all CAP reforms. European consumers today benefit from a diverse food supply, high quality and safety standards, rigorous health control guaranteed by the European Food Safety Authority and national inspection systems, and food prices that represent a historically low proportion of disposable income. The CAP has promoted food quality through geographical indication protection systems such as Protected Designations of Origin and Protected Geographical Indications, which guarantee the authenticity and quality of traditional European products, and through the promotion of organic farming, which responds to growing consumer demand for sustainably and environmentally friendly produced products.

### Environmental and Sustainability Objectives

The environmental objectives of the CAP have gained progressive importance through successive reforms of the policy, becoming one of the fundamental pillars of the design and implementation of contemporary CAP. European agriculture recognizes that it has significant responsibility for environmental protection, given that it manages a substantial portion of the European territory and has a direct impact on natural resources, biodiversity, water and air quality, and climate. The environmental objectives of the CAP include protection and improvement of soil and water quality, significant reduction of greenhouse gas emissions from the agricultural sector, promotion and conservation of biodiversity in the agricultural landscape, sustainable and efficient use of natural resources such as water, energy, and materials, proactive adaptation to climate change, and contribution to the ecological and digital transition of the agri-food sector.

The environmental dimension of the CAP has historically developed through multiple complementary tools and mechanisms applied at different scales and with different degrees of obligation. The cross-compliance requirements establish that all farmers receiving CAP direct payments must comply with a series of mandatory standards in the areas of environment, public health, animal welfare, and good agricultural and environmental soil condition, non-compliance with which may result in proportional or total reductions in their direct payments. The eco-schemes established by the new 2023-2027 CAP are additional payments that reward farmers for voluntarily adopting agricultural practices particularly beneficial to the environment that go beyond normal obligations, such as precision farming, agroecology, regenerative agriculture, maintaining biodiversity on farms, and reducing the use of synthetic pesticides and fertilizers.

The rural development measures of the second pillar also include significant investments in organic farming and agro-environmental practices, restoration of degraded rural landscapes, protection and creation of natural habitats in the agricultural environment, improvement of surface and groundwater quality, and conservation of agricultural biodiversity, including preservation of traditional crop varieties and native livestock breeds that are in danger of disappearing. The CAP has also actively promoted organic farming, which is a production system that excludes the use of genetically modified organisms, synthetic chemical products such as pesticides and mineral fertilizers, and promotes the exclusive use of natural techniques for soil fertilization, biological pest and disease control, and long-term improvement of soil fertility and health.

The environmental objectives of the CAP also include active mitigation of climate change and resilient adaptation to its increasingly severe effects. Agriculture is responsible for approximately 10 percent of the European Union's total greenhouse gas emissions, primarily through methane emissions from livestock and manure management systems, nitrogen oxide emissions from the use of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, and progressive loss of soil organic carbon through excessive conventional tillage. The CAP actively promotes the reduction of emissions from the agricultural sector through incentivizing low-carbon farming practices, such as minimum or zero tillage, systematic use of cover crops and green manures, efficient and sustainable management of organic waste and manure, sustainable forestry and agroforestry, and production and use of renewable energies of agricultural origin.

### Social, Cohesion, and Territorial Objectives

The social objectives of the CAP seek to ensure a fair and decent standard of living for the entire European agricultural population, maintain economic activity and social vitality in rural areas, and prevent the accelerated depopulation of the countryside that threatens the social and cultural fabric of large regions of Europe. Agriculture plays a fundamental role in the territorial cohesion of the European Union, as it maintains economic activity and resident population in rural areas, actively contributes to the preservation of the landscape and rural cultural heritage, and provides valuable public goods such as environmental protection, biodiversity conservation, maintenance of rural landscapes, and national food security. However, European agriculture faces significant and growing social challenges, such as the accelerated aging of the agricultural population with an increasingly higher average age, the growing difficulty of attracting and establishing motivated and qualified young farmers, the progressive concentration of production in large farms that are increasingly fewer in number, and the accelerated depopulation of peripheral rural areas.

The CAP has developed a wide range of social instruments to address these structural challenges comprehensively. The first pillar direct payments provide farmers with a basic level of stable income that enables them to maintain the economic viability of their farms, cover production costs, and progressively improve their standard of living and that of their families. The second pillar rural development measures include specific and significant aids for the establishment of young farmers under 40 years of age who are setting up in agriculture for the first time, aids for investment in the modernization and diversification of existing agricultural farms, aids for economic diversification of rural areas toward non-agricultural activities such as rural tourism, artisanal industries, and business services, and aids for creating non-agricultural employment in rural areas that can complement agricultural incomes and revitalize the local economy.

The CAP has progressively introduced increasingly specific provisions on equity in the distribution of direct payments, establishing redistribution mechanisms that limit maximum payments per farm, establish decreasing payment tiers according to farm size, and expressly favor small and medium-sized farms that are the ones that really need support to maintain their economic viability. The new 2023-2027 CAP has gone further in this direction, establishing the obligation for Member States to apply redistributive payments that transfer a portion of payments from large farms to small and medium-sized ones, establishing a maximum payment per agricultural worker, and establishing progressive payment reductions for farms that exceed certain income or area thresholds.

The CAP has also actively promoted gender equality in the agricultural sector, through specific aids targeted at women farmers, measures to encourage women's entry into agricultural activity and farm management, and training and empowerment programs specifically directed at rural women. The CAP has also promoted the active social inclusion of farmers with disabilities, farmers belonging to ethnic and linguistic minorities such as the Roma, Sami, or linguistic minorities of cross-border regions, and farmers working under conditions of special economic or social vulnerability. These social measures are fundamental to ensuring that the CAP benefits all European farmers equitably, regardless of their farm size, geographic location, gender, or personal characteristics.

The territorial cohesion objectives of the CAP seek to maintain agricultural activity and population throughout the European territory, including areas disadvantaged by adverse natural conditions, areas with significant physical limitations such as mountainous terrain or extreme climate, and peripheral areas that suffer the negative effects of remoteness and geographical isolation. The CAP includes specific additional compensatory payments for farmers working in areas with significant natural limitations, such as mountain areas above certain altitudes, cold climate zones with especially short growing seasons, arid zones with scarce and irregular rainfall, and areas with low-quality soils that severely limit agricultural productivity. These compensatory payments recognize the additional costs of production in difficult environmental conditions and help maintain agricultural activity, resident population, and landscape in these areas that would otherwise suffer accelerated and irreversible depopulation.

## Chapter 3: Agricultural Subsidies

### The Direct Payment System

The direct payment system is the main and most visible financial instrument of the modern CAP, and represents the most significant portion of the total budget of European agricultural policy. Direct payments are direct financial transfers that the European Union pays to European farmers on an annual basis, regardless of what they produce or the prices they receive for their products, with the fundamental objective of maintaining their agricultural incomes, ensuring the economic viability of their farms, and contributing to the stability of the European agri-food sector. Direct payments are financed through the European Agricultural Guarantee Fund, which is the main financial instrument of the CAP and represents approximately 70 percent of the total budget of the policy. The EAGF finances both the first pillar direct payments and market intervention measures.

The direct payment system has evolved significantly and transformatively since its introduction with the MacSharry reform in 1992. Initially, direct payments were directly and closely linked to each farmer's production, calculated as an amount per unit produced, whether per ton of cereal, per liter of milk, or per head of cattle, meaning that farmers received an amount directly proportional to the total quantity they produced in each agricultural season. This close link between payments and production generated perverse incentives for overproduction and distorted agricultural markets, as farmers had a direct economic interest in producing the largest quantity possible regardless of actual market demand. With the historic 2003 reform, direct payments were completely decoupled from production, establishing a payment system based on historical or regional reference criteria that allowed farmers to freely decide what, how much, and how to produce according to market signals and their own competitive advantages.

The current CAP direct payment system, as designed for the 2023-2027 period, is structured in several complementary components. The base payment is calculated on the basis of payment rights assigned to farmers, generally according to historical criteria but with redistribution mechanisms. Eco-schemes constitute significant additional payments for verified compliance with agricultural practices particularly beneficial to the environment, organized in four mandatory schemes of which each farmer must choose at least one, and a fifth optional scheme. Redistributive payments are additional payments destined exclusively for the first hectares of each farm, with the objective of structurally favoring small and medium-sized agricultural farms and reducing persistent disparities between farms of different productive sizes.

Direct payments represent approximately 72 percent of the total CAP budget for the 2023-2027 period, equivalent to a total financial allocation of approximately 387 billion euros for the complete seven-year period. Direct payments are distributed significantly unevenly among EU Member States, as the historical distribution of payment rights reflects deep differences in the structure, size, and productivity of agriculture among the different European countries. Western and northern European countries, such as France, Germany, Spain, Italy, and Poland, receive a larger absolute proportion of direct payments than southern and eastern European countries that joined more recently, creating recurring political tensions and intense debate about the fairness and distributive justice of the policy.

### Direct Payments and Agricultural Incomes

Direct payments play an absolutely fundamental and often decisive role in the total incomes of European farmers, as they represent a substantial and in many cases majority proportion of their annual incomes. According to recent data from the European Commission and the European Observatory of Agriculture and Food, direct payments represent approximately 25 percent of the average total gross incomes of farmers in the European Union, although this proportion varies enormously and significantly among different Member States and different productive sectors. In some member countries and in certain particularly vulnerable agricultural sectors, direct payments can represent more than 50 percent of farmers' total incomes, eloquently demonstrating the structural dependence of European agriculture on CAP subsidies to maintain its economic viability.

Direct payments have contributed crucially to stabilizing European farmers' incomes and significantly reducing the inherent volatility of agricultural incomes. Incomes derived exclusively from the production and sale of agricultural products are naturally very volatile due to the multitude of factors beyond the farmer's control that affect both the quantity produced and the price obtained, including unpredictable weather conditions, price fluctuations in increasingly globalized markets, pest and disease infestations in plants and livestock, and seasonal and cyclical variations in consumer demand. Direct payments provide a basic and predictable level of income that acts as a financial safety net for farmers, protecting them from severe price drops and enabling them to maintain the economic viability of their farms even in particularly difficult years of poor harvests, low prices, or health crises such as those experienced recently.

The distribution of direct payments among farmers has been the subject of intense and permanent political debate at all levels of European government. Defenders of the current system argue that direct payments are absolutely necessary to maintain the overall economic viability of European agricultural farms, ensure the population's food security, preserve the rural landscape, and maintain economic activity in rural areas. Critics of the system argue with equal vehemence that direct payments disproportionately benefit large farms and economically more prosperous farmers, while small farms that really need support receive insufficient amounts, and that the system should be radically reformed to redistribute resources more equitably and fairly. The CAP has progressively introduced redistribution mechanisms, such as payment limits per farm, decreasing tiers, and mandatory redistributive payments, but these mechanisms have so far had a moderate and gradual impact on the overall distribution of funds among farmers of different sizes.

CAP direct payments have also been the subject of persistent international criticism for their potentially distortionary impact on world agricultural trade competition. Defenders of the CAP argue that direct payments are a legitimate and necessary instrument to compensate for the structurally higher production costs of European agriculture, which incorporates significantly more demanding quality, safety, environmental, and animal welfare standards than many international competitors, and to ensure that European farmers can compete on a relatively level playing field with farmers from other countries who receive equivalent or superior subsidies from their national governments. Critics argue that direct payments distort market signals, incentivize overproduction in certain sectors, and harm farmers in developing countries who do not have the financial resources to provide equivalent subsidies to their agricultural producers.

### Rural Development Payments

Rural development payments constitute the second pillar of the CAP and represent approximately 25 percent of the total budget of European agricultural policy, with a financial allocation of approximately 95.5 billion euros for the 2023-2027 period. These payments finance a wide and diversified range of measures designed to promote sustainable and balanced rural development, improve the structural competitiveness of agriculture, protect and restore the rural environment, maintain economic activity and social cohesion in rural areas, and contribute to the fight against climate change in rural areas. Rural development payments are co-financed by the European Union through the European Agricultural Fund for Rural Development and by the national governments of Member States, through a co-financing system whose proportion varies significantly according to the level of economic development of each region and the political priorities of each country.

Rural development measures include a wide variety of interventions adapted to the specific needs of each area and sector, including substantial aids for investment in the modernization of existing agricultural farms, aids for the creation of new agricultural farms, aids for economic diversification of rural activities toward rural tourism, artisanal industries, renewable energies, and business services, aids for creating non-agricultural employment in rural areas, specific aids for organic farming that compensate the additional costs of this production system, aids for the protection and restoration of the environment and biodiversity in rural areas, aids for the conservation of rural cultural and architectural heritage, and aids for vocational training and continuous technical advisory for farmers and rural entrepreneurs.

Rural development payments also include specific and prioritized aids for young farmers under forty years of age, which seek to actively facilitate the establishment of qualified and motivated young people in agriculture and ensure the sustainable generational renewal of the European agricultural sector. Young farmers can receive significant additional aids for the purchase of agricultural land, investment in the initial modernization of their farms, access to specialized vocational training, and development of viable and sustainable business plans. These aids are of crucial importance because European agriculture faces a serious and growing demographic aging problem, with an increasingly higher proportion of farmers over fifty-five years of age and a worrying shortage of young people willing and able to set up in agricultural activity and take over the management of family farms.

Rural development payments also include significant aids for agro-environmental-climate measures, which economically compensate farmers for voluntarily adopting agricultural practices that systematically go beyond the normal obligations established by legislation and generate measurable benefits for the environment and for the fight against climate change. These superior practices include voluntary maintenance of permanent pastures of high ecological value, sustainable and extensive management of grazing lands in mountain areas, protection and restoration of sensitive natural habitats such as wetlands, peatlands, and riparian forests, active conservation of traditional crop varieties and local livestock breeds in danger of abandonment, and significant and verified reduction in the use of synthetic chemical products such as pesticides, herbicides, and mineral fertilizers.

## Chapter 4: The Common Agricultural Market

### Principles of the Common Agricultural Market

The European Union's common agricultural market is one of the most integrated, regulated, and complex markets in the entire world, and constitutes an essential component of the functioning of the European single market as a whole. It is based on the same fundamental principles as the general European single market: the free movement of goods without tariff or quantitative barriers between Member States, the free movement of people with full labor and residential mobility, the free movement of services with equal conditions of provision, and the free movement of capital with full liberalization of financial movements. However, the agricultural market has specific structural characteristics and particularities that require special treatment within the framework of the single market, such as the fundamentally perishable nature of most food products, the strong dependence on variable weather conditions, the marked seasonality of production in many sectors, and the vital strategic importance of ensuring the food security of the entire European population.

The common agricultural market was formally established in the Treaty of Rome as an integral part of the European common market and has been subject to continuous development and successive reforms over the decades through successive CAP reforms. The fundamental principles of the common agricultural market include the complete free movement of all agricultural products between Member States without any type of internal tariff or quantitative barrier, the application of a uniform common customs tariff for all agricultural product imports from third countries, the granting of export aids that allow European agricultural products to compete effectively in international markets, and the implementation of active market intervention mechanisms to stabilize prices, ensure food security, and protect farmers from extreme market fluctuations.
