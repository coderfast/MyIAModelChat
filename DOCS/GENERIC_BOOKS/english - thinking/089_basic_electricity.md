Electricity is a physical phenomenon related to the presence and movement of electric charges. At the subatomic level, electric charge is a fundamental property of matter, like mass or spin. Electrons have negative charge, protons have positive charge, and neutrons have no charge. Understanding electricity is fundamental to modern technology, from electronic circuits to power grids and communications.

Electric charges interact with each other through the electric field, which is a force field that exists around any electric charge. Two charges of the same sign repel each other, and two charges of opposite sign attract each other, according to Coulomb's law: F = k q1 q2 / r^2, where k is Coulomb's constant (8.988 x 10^9 N m^2/C^2), q1 and q2 are the charges, and r is the distance between them. The electric force is extraordinarily strong: the repulsive force between two electrons is approximately 10^42 times more intense than the gravitational force between them. Electricity manifests itself in multiple natural phenomena, such as lightning, the aurora borealis, and static electricity.

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter that determines how an object interacts with electromagnetic fields. The electric field is a vector force field that exists around any electric charge or system of charges. It is defined as the force per unit charge that a positive test charge placed at a point in the field would experience: E = F/q. The electric field of a point charge is E = k q / r^2, and its direction is radial (outward for positive charges, inward for negative charges). Electric fields are graphically represented by field lines, which go from positive charges to negative charges. The superposition of electric fields states that the total field at a point is the vector sum of the fields due to each individual charge. The electric field is fundamental to understanding how charges move in electric circuits and how atoms and molecules interact.

The voltage source (such as a battery or generator) creates a potential difference across its terminals, which will drive the flow of charges in a circuit connected to it.

### 2.2 Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the applied voltage and inversely proportional to the resistance of the conductor: I = V/R, where I is the current (in amperes), V is the voltage (in volts), and R is the resistance (in ohms). This law was formulated by Georg Simon Ohm in 1827 and is one of the most fundamental relationships in electricity. One ohm is defined as the resistance of a conductor to which a voltage of one volt is applied and through which a current of one ampere flows. Ohm's law is valid for ohmic materials, whose resistance is independent of the applied voltage. Non-ohmic materials, such as semiconductors and electronic devices, do not strictly follow Ohm's law.

Electrical resistance depends on the geometry of the conductor and the properties of the material. It is calculated as R = rho L/A, where rho is the resistivity of the material (in ohm meters), L is the length of the conductor, and A is its cross-sectional area. Materials with low resistivity are good conductors, while those with high resistivity are good insulators. Resistivity varies with temperature: for metals, resistivity increases linearly with temperature, meaning that metals are worse conductors at high temperatures. For semiconductors, resistivity decreases exponentially with temperature, since more electrons acquire enough energy to jump to the conduction band. Superconductors have zero resistivity below their critical temperature and can carry current without energy dissipation.

### 2.3 Kirchhoff's Laws

<|thinking|>Analyzing about 'Basic Electricity'. First, the paragraph states: "Kirchhoff's laws are two fundamental principles for the analysis of complex electric circuits." Additionally, the text contains 9 sentences with 133 words in total. On the other hand, the text has a conversational and colloquial tone. Kirchhoff's laws are two fundamental principles for the analysis of complex electric circuits. The first law (Kirchhoff's current law) states that the algebraic sum of the currents entering a node is equal to zero: Sigma I = 0. This implies that the total current entering a point in a circuit is equal to the total current leaving that point. This law is a consequence of the conservation of electric charge. The second law (Kirchhoff's voltage law) states that the algebraic sum of all voltages around a closed loop in a circuit is equal to zero: Sigma V = 0. This implies that the sum of the voltage drops across the components is equal to the sum of the voltage rises in the sources. This law is a consequence of the conservation of energy.

Kirchhoff's laws allow solving circuits that cannot be analyzed simply by using Ohm's law directly. To apply these laws, reference directions are assigned to the currents in each branch of the circuit, and then the resulting system of equations is solved. If the result is positive, the actual direction of the current is the same as the assigned direction; if it is negative, the actual direction is opposite. Kirchhoff's laws are valid for all electric circuits, including direct current and alternating current circuits. In alternating current circuits, complex impedances are used that include resistances, capacitances, and inductances. Circuit analysis using Kirchhoff's laws is fundamental in electronic, electrical, and telecommunications engineering.

The electric power dissipated in a resistor is completely converted into heat (Joule effect). Alternating current (AC) is the flow of electric charges that periodically changes direction. In the domestic power grid, alternating current changes direction 50 times per second (50 Hz in Europe) or 60 times per second (60 Hz in the Americas). The waveform of alternating current is typically sinusoidal, and is described by V(t) = V0 sin(wt), where V0 is the amplitude (peak voltage), w is the angular frequency (2 pi f), and t is the time. The effective voltage (or RMS voltage) of a sinusoidal wave is V_rms = V0/sqrt(2), which is the equivalent direct current voltage that would produce the same power in a resistor. The effective voltage of the European power grid is 230 V, which corresponds to a peak voltage of approximately 325 V.

The frequency of an alternating current is the number of complete cycles per second, and is measured in hertz (Hz). The standard frequency in Europe is 50 Hz, while in the Americas and parts of Asia it is 60 Hz. The frequency must remain constant throughout the power grid, since variations can cause malfunctioning of electronic equipment and motors. Generators in power plants synchronize their frequency with that of the grid to maintain system stability. The phase of a sinusoidal wave indicates its point within the cycle, and is measured in degrees or radians. Two waves can be in phase (phase shift 0°), in phase opposition (phase shift 180°), or with any intermediate phase shift. The phase shift between voltage and current in a circuit depends on the nature of the circuit: resistive (in phase), capacitive (current leads voltage), or inductive (voltage leads current).

The power factor is the ratio between active (useful) power and apparent power in an alternating current circuit. It is defined as PF = cos(phi), where phi is the phase angle between voltage and current. A power factor of 1 means that all the power supplied is useful (pure resistive case). Industries with inductive loads (motors, transformers) typically have low power factors, and use capacitor banks to correct the power factor and improve efficiency. Electric companies penalize consumers with low power factors, since they require greater infrastructure to transport the same amount of useful power.

Alternating current (AC) circuit analysis is more complex than direct current (DC) analysis because voltages and currents vary with time. The most common method for analyzing AC circuits is the phasor method, which represents sinusoidal voltages and currents as complex numbers with magnitude and phase. A phasor V = V0 e^(jphi) represents a voltage with amplitude V0 and phase phi. Thévenin's theorem states that any linear circuit with two terminals can be replaced by a Thévenin voltage source (Vth) in series with a Thévenin resistance (Rth). Periodic inspection of electrical installations is fundamental to ensuring safety and correct operation. Visual inspections verify the condition of cables, connections, protections, and equipment. Electrical tests measure insulation resistance, protective conductor continuity, earth loop impedance, RCD trip current, and contact voltage. Infrared thermography detects hot spots in connections and equipment, indicating contact or overload problems before they cause failures. Power quality analyzers measure harmonics, flicker, phase imbalance, and power factor, identifying problems that could damage equipment or cause energy losses. Predictive maintenance programs use sensor data and trend analysis to predict failures and schedule repairs proactively, reducing downtime and maintenance costs.

IoT system security is a critical challenge due to the large attack surface and device resource limitations.
