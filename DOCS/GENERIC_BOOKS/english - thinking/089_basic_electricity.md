# Basic Electricity

## Chapter 1: Introduction to Electricity

### 1.1 What is Electricity

Electricity is a physical phenomenon related to the presence and movement of electric charges. At the subatomic level, electric charge is a fundamental property of matter, like mass or spin. Electrons have negative charge, protons have positive charge, and neutrons have no charge. The electromagnetic interaction is one of the four fundamental forces of nature, and is responsible for holding atoms together in molecules, for chemical reactions, and for virtually all of chemistry and biology. Electricity, together with magnetism, is a manifestation of the electromagnetic force, which was unified by James Clerk Maxwell in the 19th century. Understanding electricity is fundamental to modern technology, from electronic circuits to power grids and communications.

Electric charges interact with each other through the electric field, which is a force field that exists around any electric charge. Two charges of the same sign repel each other, and two charges of opposite sign attract each other, according to Coulomb's law: F = k q1 q2 / r^2, where k is Coulomb's constant (8.988 x 10^9 N m^2/C^2), q1 and q2 are the charges, and r is the distance between them. The electric force is extraordinarily strong: the repulsive force between two electrons is approximately 10^42 times more intense than the gravitational force between them. However, in normal matter, positive and negative charges cancel out, so macroscopic electrical effects are only observed when there is a charge imbalance. Electricity manifests itself in multiple natural phenomena, such as lightning, the aurora borealis, and static electricity.

Electricity has transformed human civilization since its discovery and development in the 18th and 19th centuries. Benjamin Franklin demonstrated in 1752 that lightning was an electrical discharge, and Alessandro Volta invented the first electric battery in 1800, providing the first stable source of direct current. Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831, which enabled the development of generators and transformers. Nikola Tesla and Thomas Edison led the "war of currents" in the 1880s, with Tesla promoting alternating current and Edison promoting direct current. Tesla's alternating current ultimately prevailed as the standard for electric power distribution, due to its ease of being transformed to different voltages. Electricity has become the most versatile and widely used form of energy in modern civilization, powering everything from electronic devices to entire industries.

### 1.2 Electric Charge and Electric Field

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter that determines how an object interacts with electromagnetic fields. The unit of electric charge in the International System is the coulomb (C), which is equivalent to the charge of approximately 6.24 x 10^18 electrons. Electric charge is quantized, meaning it can only take on multiples of the elementary charge e = 1.602 x 10^-19 C, which is the charge of a proton or an electron. Quarks, which are the fundamental constituents of protons and neutrons, have fractional charges of +/- 1/3 e or +/- 2/3 e, but are never found in isolation due to charge confinement. The principle of conservation of charge states that the total electric charge of an isolated system remains constant, although charges can be transferred from one object to another.

The electric field is a vector force field that exists around any electric charge or system of charges. It is defined as the force per unit charge that a positive test charge placed at a point in the field would experience: E = F/q. The electric field of a point charge is E = k q / r^2, and its direction is radial (outward for positive charges, inward for negative charges). Electric fields are graphically represented by field lines, which go from positive charges to negative charges. The superposition of electric fields states that the total field at a point is the vector sum of the fields due to each individual charge. The electric field is fundamental to understanding how charges move in electric circuits and how atoms and molecules interact.

### 1.3 Electric Potential and Potential Difference

The electric potential at a point is the electric potential energy per unit charge at that point, and is measured in volts (V). The potential difference (or voltage) between two points is the work needed to move a unit of charge between those two points, and is measured in volts. One volt is equivalent to one joule per coulomb (V = J/C). The potential difference is the driving force that causes electric charges to move in a circuit, similar to how a height difference causes water to flow in a hydraulic system. The voltage source (such as a battery or generator) creates a potential difference across its terminals, which will drive the flow of charges in a circuit connected to it.

The concept of electric potential is fundamental to understanding the operation of electric circuits. In a circuit, charges move from a point of high potential to one of low potential, releasing energy along the way. The voltage of a typical AA battery is 1.5 V, that of a car is 12 V, and that of the domestic power grid is 230 V (in Europe). Electric power, which is the rate of electrical energy transfer, is calculated as P = V I, where V is the voltage and I is the current. Transformers can change the voltage of alternating current, enabling efficient distribution of electric power over long distances. Understanding electric potential is essential for circuit design and for understanding phenomena such as short circuits and electric discharges.

## Chapter 2: Electric Circuits

### 2.1 Basic Components

An electric circuit is a closed path through which electric charges can flow. The basic components of a circuit are the voltage source (battery or generator), conductors (wires), and load components (resistors, light bulbs, motors, etc.). Circuits are classified as series and parallel based on how the components are connected. In a series circuit, components are connected one after another in a single path, and the current is the same at all points. In a parallel circuit, components are connected between the same two points, and the voltage is the same across each branch. Real circuits are usually combinations of series and parallel circuits, and their analysis requires the application of Kirchhoff's laws.

Conductors are materials that allow the easy flow of electric charges. Metals are the best conductors, especially silver, copper, and gold, because they have free valence electrons that can move easily through the material. Copper is the most widely used conductor in electrical installations due to its good conductivity and reasonable cost. Semiconductors, such as silicon and germanium, have conductivity intermediate between conductors and insulators, and their properties can be modified through doping, making them fundamental to electronics. Insulators, such as glass, porcelain, and polymers, have very few free electrons and do not allow current flow. Superconductors are materials that, below a certain critical temperature, have zero electrical resistance, making them ideal for high-efficiency applications.

### 2.2 Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the applied voltage and inversely proportional to the resistance of the conductor: I = V/R, where I is the current (in amperes), V is the voltage (in volts), and R is the resistance (in ohms). This law was formulated by Georg Simon Ohm in 1827 and is one of the most fundamental relationships in electricity. One ohm is defined as the resistance of a conductor to which a voltage of one volt is applied and through which a current of one ampere flows. Ohm's law is valid for ohmic materials, whose resistance is independent of the applied voltage. Non-ohmic materials, such as semiconductors and electronic devices, do not strictly follow Ohm's law.

Electrical resistance depends on the geometry of the conductor and the properties of the material. It is calculated as R = rho L/A, where rho is the resistivity of the material (in ohm meters), L is the length of the conductor, and A is its cross-sectional area. Materials with low resistivity are good conductors, while those with high resistivity are good insulators. Resistivity varies with temperature: for metals, resistivity increases linearly with temperature, meaning that metals are worse conductors at high temperatures. For semiconductors, resistivity decreases exponentially with temperature, since more electrons acquire enough energy to jump to the conduction band. Superconductors have zero resistivity below their critical temperature and can carry current without energy dissipation.

### 2.3 Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's laws are two fundamental principles for the analysis of complex electric circuits. The first law (Kirchhoff's current law) states that the algebraic sum of the currents entering a node is equal to zero: Sigma I = 0. This implies that the total current entering a point in a circuit is equal to the total current leaving that point. This law is a consequence of the conservation of electric charge. The second law (Kirchhoff's voltage law) states that the algebraic sum of all voltages around a closed loop in a circuit is equal to zero: Sigma V = 0. This implies that the sum of the voltage drops across the components is equal to the sum of the voltage rises in the sources. This law is a consequence of the conservation of energy.

Kirchhoff's laws allow solving circuits that cannot be analyzed simply by using Ohm's law directly. To apply these laws, reference directions are assigned to the currents in each branch of the circuit, and then the resulting system of equations is solved. If the result is positive, the actual direction of the current is the same as the assigned direction; if it is negative, the actual direction is opposite. Kirchhoff's laws are valid for all electric circuits, including direct current and alternating current circuits. In alternating current circuits, complex impedances are used that include resistances, capacitances, and inductances. Circuit analysis using Kirchhoff's laws is fundamental in electronic, electrical, and telecommunications engineering.

## Chapter 3: Direct Current

### 3.1 Direct Current Sources

Direct current (DC) is the flow of electric charges in a single direction along a conductor. The most common direct current sources are batteries and switched-mode power supplies. Batteries are electrochemical devices that convert chemical energy into electrical energy through redox reactions. A typical 1.5 V alkaline battery contains a zinc electrode (anode) and a manganese dioxide electrode (cathode) separated by a potassium hydroxide alkaline electrolyte. Lithium-ion batteries, used in mobile electronic devices, have much higher energy densities than alkaline batteries. Fuel cells convert hydrogen and oxygen into electricity and water, with efficiencies exceeding 60%. Switch-mode power supplies convert alternating current from the power grid into regulated direct current, and are used in most electronic devices.

Rechargeable batteries can be charged and discharged multiple times, making them more economical and sustainable than single-use batteries. Lead-acid batteries, invented in 1859, are still the most used in automobiles and backup power systems. Nickel-cadmium (NiCd) batteries were popular in portable electronic devices but have been replaced by nickel-metal hydride (NiMH) and lithium-ion batteries. Lithium-ion batteries have energy densities of 150-250 Wh/kg, cycle lives of 500-2000, and low maintenance. Electric vehicles use large-capacity lithium-ion batteries, with capacities of 50-100 kWh providing ranges of 200-500 km. Researchers are developing solid-state batteries, lithium-air batteries, and lithium-sulfur batteries that promise energy densities exceeding 500 Wh/kg.

### 3.2 Series and Parallel Circuits

In a series circuit, components are connected one after another in a single path. The current is the same through all components, but the voltage is divided among them. The equivalent resistance of resistors in series is the sum of the individual resistances: R_eq = R1 + R2 + R3 + ... The total voltage is divided among the components proportionally to their resistances: V1 = V_total x R1/R_eq. If one component fails (opens), all current stops flowing and the entire circuit shuts off. Old Christmas light strings used bulbs in series, so if one bulb burned out, all of them went out. Series circuits are simple but limited, since the failure of a single component affects the entire circuit.

In a parallel circuit, components are connected between the same two points, creating multiple paths for the current. The voltage is the same across all branches, but the current divides among them. The equivalent resistance of resistors in parallel is calculated as: 1/R_eq = 1/R1 + 1R2 + 1/R3 + ... For two resistors in parallel, R_eq = (R1 x R2)/(R1 + R2). The total current divides among the branches inversely proportional to their resistances: I1 = V/R1, I2 = V/R2, etc. If one component fails, the others continue to function, since they have independent paths for the current. Domestic electrical wiring uses parallel circuits so that each outlet and each light operates independently. Parallel circuits are more flexible and robust than series circuits, but require more wiring.

### 3.3 Electric Power and Energy

Electric power is the rate at which a component or circuit consumes or produces electrical energy. It is calculated as P = V I, where V is the voltage and I is the current. Using Ohm's law (I = V/R), power can also be expressed as P = V^2/R or P = I^2 R. Power is measured in watts (W), where 1 W = 1 V x 1 A. A 100 W light bulb consumes 100 joules of energy per second. The electrical energy consumed is the product of power and time: E = P x t, and is measured in joules or kilowatt-hours (kWh). A 1 kW device running for 1 hour consumes 1 kWh of energy. The electricity bill is calculated by multiplying the kWh consumed by the price per kWh.

The electric power dissipated in a resistor is completely converted into heat (Joule effect). This effect is used in heaters, irons, and electric stoves, but is undesirable in wires and electronic components, where it generates energy losses and heating. Electric wires are designed to minimize Joule losses by using low-resistivity conductors (copper) and adequate cross-sections. The voltage drop in a wire is calculated as V = I R, and must be less than 3% of the nominal voltage to ensure safe and efficient operation. Electronic devices use semiconductors to control and convert electric power with high efficiency, reducing Joule losses. Inverters convert direct current to alternating current, and rectifiers perform the reverse operation. The efficiency of these devices can exceed 95% in modern applications.

## Chapter 4: Alternating Current

### 4.1 Nature of Alternating Current

Alternating current (AC) is the flow of electric charges that periodically changes direction. In the domestic power grid, alternating current changes direction 50 times per second (50 Hz in Europe) or 60 times per second (60 Hz in the Americas). The waveform of alternating current is typically sinusoidal, and is described by V(t) = V0 sin(wt), where V0 is the amplitude (peak voltage), w is the angular frequency (2 pi f), and t is the time. The effective voltage (or RMS voltage) of a sinusoidal wave is V_rms = V0/sqrt(2), which is the equivalent direct current voltage that would produce the same power in a resistor. The effective voltage of the European power grid is 230 V, which corresponds to a peak voltage of approximately 325 V.

Alternating current is the standard for electric power distribution worldwide due to its ease of being transformed to different voltages. Transformers can step up voltage for long-distance transmission (reducing losses) and then step it down for distribution and local consumption. Power transmission at high voltages (110-400 kV) reduces Joule losses in the cables, since the current is lower for the same power. Distribution transformers reduce the voltage to safe levels for consumers (230 V in Europe, 120 V in the Americas). Alternating current also allows the direct use of induction motors, which are the most widely used motors in industry due to their simplicity, reliability, and low cost.

### 4.2 Frequency and Phase

The frequency of an alternating current is the number of complete cycles per second, and is measured in hertz (Hz). The standard frequency in Europe is 50 Hz, while in the Americas and parts of Asia it is 60 Hz. The frequency must remain constant throughout the power grid, since variations can cause malfunctioning of electronic equipment and motors. Generators in power plants synchronize their frequency with that of the grid to maintain system stability. The phase of a sinusoidal wave indicates its point within the cycle, and is measured in degrees or radians. Two waves can be in phase (phase shift 0°), in phase opposition (phase shift 180°), or with any intermediate phase shift. The phase shift between voltage and current in a circuit depends on the nature of the circuit: resistive (in phase), capacitive (current leads voltage), or inductive (voltage leads current).

The power factor is the ratio between active (useful) power and apparent power in an alternating current circuit. It is defined as PF = cos(phi), where phi is the phase angle between voltage and current. A power factor of 1 means that all the power supplied is useful (pure resistive case). A power factor less than 1 indicates that part of the power is returned to the source (reactive power), which reduces system efficiency. Industries with inductive loads (motors, transformers) typically have low power factors, and use capacitor banks to correct the power factor and improve efficiency. Electric companies penalize consumers with low power factors, since they require greater infrastructure to transport the same amount of useful power.

### 4.3 RLC Circuits

RLC circuits contain resistors (R), inductors (L), and capacitors (C) connected in series or in parallel. In a series RLC circuit with alternating current, the total impedance is Z = sqrt(R^2 + (XL - XC)^2), where XL = wL is the inductive reactance and XC = 1/(wC) is the capacitive reactance. The current in the circuit is I = V/Z, and it is phase-shifted with respect to the voltage by an angle phi = arctan((XL - XC)/R). When XL = XC, the total reactance is zero and the impedance is minimum (equal to R). In this condition, called resonance, the current reaches its maximum value. The resonant frequency is f0 = 1/(2 pi sqrt(LC)). Resonant circuits are used in radio and television tuners to select a specific frequency from the multiple signals received by the antenna.

RLC circuits have fundamental applications in electronics and telecommunications. Electric filters use RLC circuits to allow certain frequencies to pass and block others. Low-pass filters allow low frequencies to pass and block high frequencies, high-pass filters do the opposite, and band-pass filters allow a range of intermediate frequencies to pass. Electronic oscillators use resonant circuits to generate stable frequency sinusoidal waves. Magnetic coupling circuits, such as transformers and coupling coils, use inductances to transfer energy between circuits without direct electrical connection. Wireless charging systems use magnetic resonance to transfer energy to electronic devices without wires. RLC circuits are fundamental to the design of communication systems, signal processing, and automatic control.

## Chapter 5: Magnetism and Electromagnetism

### 5.1 Magnetic Field

The magnetic field is a vector field that describes the magnetic influence on moving charges, electric currents, and magnetic materials. It is measured in teslas (T) in the International System, where 1 T = 1 N/(A m). The Earth has a magnetic field of approximately 25-65 microteslas, which varies depending on latitude and altitude. Permanent magnets, such as neodymium magnets, can reach fields of 1-1.5 T, and superconducting magnets can reach fields of up to 20 T or more. The magnetic field of a current-carrying coil is proportional to the current and the number of turns, and its direction is determined by the right-hand rule. Magnetic fields are fundamental to the operation of generators, motors, transformers, and numerous electronic devices.

Materials are classified according to their behavior in magnetic fields. Diamagnetic materials (such as bismuth and copper) generate a weak magnetic field opposite to the applied field. Paramagnetic materials (such as aluminum and platinum) generate a weak magnetic field in the same direction as the applied field. Ferromagnetic materials (such as iron, nickel, and cobalt) have enormous magnetic susceptibility and can be permanently magnetized. Ferromagnetic materials are fundamental to permanent magnets, transformer cores, and motor windings. The Curie temperature is the temperature above which a ferromagnetic material loses its magnetic properties and becomes paramagnetic. For iron, the Curie temperature is approximately 770°C. Advanced magnetic materials, such as ferrites and rare-earth magnets, are essential to modern technology.

### 5.2 Electromagnetic Induction

Electromagnetic induction is the phenomenon by which a changing magnetic field induces an electromotive force (EMF) in a conductor. It was discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831 and is the fundamental principle of generators, transformers, and electric motors. The induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the conductor: V = -dPhi/dt, where Phi is the magnetic flux. The negative sign indicates that the induced EMF opposes the change that produces it (Lenz's law). A basic generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by rotating a conductor within a magnetic field, inducing an alternating current. Power plant generators use this principle to produce the electricity that feeds the grid.

Transformers are devices that use electromagnetic induction to change the voltage of alternating current. They consist of two coils (primary and secondary) wound around a common iron core. The transformation ratio is V1/V2 = N1/N2, where N1 and N2 are the number of turns of the primary and secondary coils. A step-up transformer increases voltage (N2 > N1) and a step-down transformer decreases it (N2 < N1). Transformers are essential for electric power distribution, since they allow stepping up voltage for long-distance transmission (reducing losses) and then stepping it down for consumption. Distribution transformers reduce voltage from 11-33 kV to 230/400 V for consumers. Power transformers in power plants can handle powers of hundreds of MVA with efficiencies exceeding 99%.

### 5.3 Electric Motors

Electric motors convert electrical energy into rotational or translational mechanical energy. The basic principle of a motor is the interaction between a magnetic field and an electric current, which generates a force on the conductor (Lorentz force: F = I L x B). Direct current motors use brushes and a commutator to periodically reverse the current in the rotating winding, maintaining rotation. Alternating current motors, such as induction motors, use rotating magnetic fields generated by polyphase currents. Induction motors are the most widely used in industry due to their simplicity, reliability, and low maintenance. Stepper motors are used in applications that require precise control of angular position, such as 3D printers and industrial robots.

Electric motors are fundamental to industry and modern transportation. It is estimated that electric motors consume approximately 45% of the world's electricity, and improving their efficiency is a key objective for energy conservation. High-efficiency motors (IE4 and IE5 classes) can achieve efficiencies exceeding 95%, while older motors may have efficiencies of 60-70%. Electric vehicles use permanent magnet motors or induction motors for traction, with efficiencies exceeding 90%. Axial flux motors are emerging as an alternative to traditional radial motors, offering higher torque density in a more compact volume. Linear motors, which produce linear motion instead of rotation, are used in magnetic levitation trains (maglev) and high-precision machine tools. The combination of high-efficiency motors with advanced power electronics is improving energy efficiency in multiple applications.

## Chapter 6: Generators and Power Plants

### 6.1 Generation Principles

An electric generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy through electromagnetic induction discovered by Faraday. The basic principle involves rotating a conductor (or conductor coils) within a magnetic field, inducing an EMF that drives the flow of electric current. Alternating current generators (alternators) use rotating magnets and stationary coils (or vice versa) to produce sinusoidal alternating current. Direct current generators (dynamos) use a segmented commutator to rectify the induced alternating current. The electric power produced by a generator is proportional to the applied mechanical torque and the rotational speed: P = tau x w. Modern generators use rare-earth magnets and laminated iron cores to maximize efficiency and power density.

The efficiency of a generator depends on multiple factors, including magnetic design, material quality, rotational speed, and load. Large generators in power plants can achieve efficiencies exceeding 98%, while small generators for industrial use have efficiencies of 85-95%. Losses in a generator include Joule losses (heat in the conductors), magnetic losses (hysteresis and eddy currents in the core), and mechanical losses (friction in bearings and ventilation). High-speed generators are used in hydroelectric and nuclear power plants, while slow-speed generators are used in wind turbines. Multipolar generators allow operation at low speeds while maintaining standard frequencies, which is useful in wind and tidal energy applications.

### 6.2 Thermal Power Plants

Thermal power plants convert thermal energy into electricity through thermodynamic cycles. Most thermal power plants use the Rankine cycle, which involves heating water to produce steam that drives a turbine connected to a generator. Coal power plants burn coal to heat water and produce steam. Natural gas power plants use Brayton cycles, where combustion gas directly drives a gas turbine. Nuclear power plants use the energy of nuclear fission to heat water and produce steam. The efficiency of a thermal power plant depends on the steam temperature and the coolant temperature, and is limited by the Carnot cycle. Modern combined-cycle (gas + steam) plants can achieve efficiencies exceeding 60%, while typical coal plants have efficiencies of 33-40%.

Thermal power plants represent approximately 60% of global electricity generation, but are the main source of CO2 emissions. Coal plants are the most polluting, emitting approximately 1 kg of CO2 per kWh produced. Natural gas plants emit approximately half the CO2 of coal plants, and are considered a transition technology toward cleaner sources. Nuclear plants do not emit CO2 during operation, but generate radioactive waste that requires special management. Researchers are developing carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies that would allow coal and gas plants to operate with significantly reduced emissions. The future of thermal power plants will involve integration with renewable technologies, efficiency improvement, and emission reduction.

### 6.3 Hydroelectric and Wind Power Plants

Hydroelectric power plants convert the gravitational potential energy of water into electricity. Water stored in a reservoir is channeled through hydraulic turbines that rotate electric generators. Reservoir plants can store energy and regulate supply, making them valuable for balancing the intermittency of other renewable sources. Run-of-river plants use the river flow without large reservoirs. Hydroelectric power provides approximately 16% of the world's electricity and is the largest source of renewable energy. The largest plants in the world, such as Itaipu (14 GW) and Three Gorges (22.5 GW), produce enormous amounts of electricity in a clean and renewable manner.

Wind power plants convert the kinetic energy of wind into electricity through wind turbines. Modern wind turbines have blades up to 80 meters in length and can reach capacities of up to 15 MW. Onshore and offshore wind farms are growing rapidly worldwide. Wind power provides approximately 7% of the world's electricity and is projected to reach 20-30% by 2050. Offshore wind turbines can harness stronger and more consistent winds than onshore turbines. Researchers are developing larger and more efficient wind turbines, lighter and stronger blade materials, and advanced control systems. The combination of wind power with battery storage is making wind energy an increasingly reliable and competitive power source.

## Chapter 7: Basic Electronics

### 7.1 Electronic Components

Electronic components are devices that control the flow of electric current in circuits. Passive components, such as resistors, capacitors, and inductors, cannot amplify signals or generate energy. Active components, such as transistors, diodes, and operational amplifiers, can control and amplify the flow of current. Resistors have a fixed or variable resistance and are used to limit current, divide voltage, and establish reference levels. Capacitors store energy in electric fields and are used for filtering, signal coupling, and temporary energy storage. Inductors store energy in magnetic fields and are used in filters, transformers, and resonant circuits. Diodes allow current to flow in only one direction and are used for rectification, protection, and light emission (LEDs).

Transistors are the fundamental components of modern electronics. Field-effect transistors (FETs) control current flow through an electric field and are used in amplifiers, switches, and digital circuits. Bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) control current flow through a base current and are used in analog signal amplifiers. Integrated circuits contain thousands to millions of transistors on a single silicon chip and form the basis of processors, memory, and modern electronic devices. Microcontrollers are integrated circuits that include a processor, memory, and peripherals on a single chip, and are used in embedded devices, automation, and robotics. Field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) allow configuring digital circuits for specific applications.

### 7.2 Digital Circuits

Digital circuits use two logic states (0 and 1) to represent and process information. Logic circuits implement logic operations such as AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, and XOR, which are the building blocks of all digital systems. Combinational circuits, such as multipliers, adders, and encoders, produce outputs that depend solely on current inputs. Sequential circuits, such as registers, counters, and state machines, have internal states that depend on the history of inputs. Digital circuits are more robust against noise than analog circuits, since they only need to distinguish between two voltage levels. Modern processors use CMOS technology, which combines NMOS and PMOS transistors to achieve low power dissipation and high speed.

Digital electronics is the foundation of modern computing. Processors (CPUs) execute program instructions sequentially, performing arithmetic and logic operations at speeds of billions of instructions per second. Memory stores data and instructions temporarily (RAM) or permanently (ROM, flash). Input/output devices allow interaction between the processor and the outside world. Embedded systems, which are specialized computers integrated into devices such as appliances, automobiles, and medical devices, use low-power, high-efficiency processors. AI systems, such as AI accelerators and neural processors, are specifically designed to run machine learning algorithms with high efficiency. Digital electronics continues to advance toward smaller sizes, higher speeds, and lower power consumption, following Moore's Law.

### 7.3 Analog and Digital Signals

Analog signals are continuous in time and amplitude, and represent information as continuous variations of voltage or current. Examples of analog signals are the human voice, music, light, and temperature. Digital signals are discrete in time and amplitude, and represent information as sequences of numerical values (typically binary). Analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) transform analog signals into digital signals, and digital-to-analog converters (DACs) perform the reverse operation. ADCs are used in audio, video, and sensor systems to digitize real-world information. DACs are used in audio players, televisions, and control systems to reconstruct analog signals from digital data.

Signal digitization has significant advantages over analog processing. Digital signals are more resistant to noise and degradation, since they can be perfectly regenerated. Digital processing allows advanced techniques such as compression, digital filtering, pattern recognition, and secure communication. Modern communication systems, such as WiFi, Bluetooth, and 5G, use digital modulation to transmit data efficiently and securely. Digital audio systems, such as CD, MP3, and streaming, use sampling and quantization to represent sound digitally. Digital imaging systems, such as digital cameras and LCD/OLED displays, use pixels to represent images digitally. The transition from analog to digital has transformed virtually all areas of technology, from communications to medicine and industry.

## Chapter 8: Electrical Safety

### 8.1 Electrical Hazards

Electricity can be dangerous if handled improperly. The main risks are electrocution, arc flash burns, fires, and explosions. Electrocution occurs when an electric current passes through the human body, causing muscle contractions, cardiac fibrillation, and internal burns. The current needed to cause ventricular fibrillation (potentially fatal) is only 30-50 mA for a few seconds. The voltage of the domestic power grid (230 V in Europe) is sufficient to cause death if the body establishes a conductive path between two points. Arc flash burns occur when an electric discharge jumps through the air between two conductors, generating temperatures exceeding 20,000°C. Electrical fires are caused by overheated wires, loose connections, and overloads in circuits.

Electrical safety standards establish requirements for the design, installation, and maintenance of electrical installations. The IEC 60364 standard establishes requirements for low-voltage electrical installations. Overcurrent protection devices (fuses and circuit breakers) protect cables and equipment against excessive currents. Residual current devices (RCDs) detect ground current leaks and cut the circuit in milliseconds, protecting against electrocution. Electric cables must have the appropriate cross-section for the current they carry and must be protected against mechanical damage. Outlets in wet environments (bathrooms, kitchens) must have additional protection, such as weatherproof covers and 30 mA RCD circuits.

### 8.2 Lightning Protection

Lightning is an atmospheric electrical discharge that can reach voltages of up to 100 million volts and currents of up to 200,000 amperes. Lightning protection is critical for buildings, electrical installations, and people. Lightning rods, invented by Benjamin Franklin in 1752, are metallic conductors connected to ground that intercept lightning and safely redirect the electric current to the ground. Modern lightning rods use air terminals, down conductors, and grounding systems that comply with IEC 62305 standards. High-risk buildings, such as hospitals and power plants, use Level I lightning protection systems, which can handle currents of up to 200 kA.

Lightning protection includes both structural protection and protection of electrical and electronic systems. Surge protective devices (SPDs) are used to protect electronic equipment against transient overvoltages caused by lightning or grid switching. SPDs are installed at the main electrical panel and at secondary panels, creating tiered protection zones. Signal and data cables must also be protected against overvoltages using specific SPDs. Early streamer emission (ESE) lightning protection systems use active tip technology that ionizes the air around the tip, increasing lightning capture efficiency. The combination of lightning rods, SPDs, and proper grounding provides comprehensive protection against the effects of lightning in electrical and electronic installations.

## Chapter 9: Renewable Energy and Electricity

### 9.1 Solar Photovoltaics

Solar photovoltaic energy converts the electromagnetic radiation of the Sun directly into electricity through the photovoltaic effect. Solar panels are composed of crystalline silicon cells or advanced materials such as perovskites. Monocrystalline silicon panels have efficiencies of 20-22%, polycrystalline panels 15-18%, and perovskite panels have achieved efficiencies exceeding 25% in the laboratory. Photovoltaic systems include the solar panel, the inverter (which converts DC to AC), and the monitoring system. Systems can be grid-connected, off-grid (with batteries), or hybrid. Solar energy is the fastest-growing renewable energy source in the world, and its cost has reduced by 90% since 2010.

Residential photovoltaic systems typically have capacities of 3-10 kW and cover the electrical consumption of a home. Commercial and industrial systems can reach capacities of hundreds of kW or MW. Large-scale solar plants, such as the Bhadla plant in India (2245 MW), are some of the largest solar installations in the world. Building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) use solar panels as building material, integrating them into facades, roofs, and windows. Researchers are developing transparent solar panels for windows, flexible panels for clothing, and solar concentration systems for high industrial temperatures. The combination of solar photovoltaics with battery storage is making solar energy an increasingly reliable and economical power source.

### 9.2 Wind Energy

Wind energy converts the kinetic energy of wind into electricity through wind turbines. Modern wind turbines have three blades and can reach capacities of up to 15 MW. The available power in the wind is proportional to the cube of its speed, making site selection critical. Onshore wind farms are built in areas with strong, consistent winds, such as hilltops and coastlines. Offshore wind farms harness stronger, more consistent winds, but are more expensive to build and maintain. Floating offshore turbines allow operation in deep waters where fixed-bottom foundations are not feasible. Wind power provides approximately 7% of the world's electricity and is projected to reach 20-30% by 2050.

Integrating large amounts of wind power into the grid poses challenges due to its intermittency. Energy storage systems, such as lithium-ion batteries and pumped-storage hydroelectric plants, are necessary to ensure a constant supply. AI-based weather forecasting algorithms improve wind generation forecasting. Demand-side management systems adjust electricity consumption to match wind energy availability. Researchers are developing larger and more efficient wind turbines, lighter and stronger blade materials, and advanced control systems. Wind energy is a key piece of the global energy transition and will contribute significantly to reducing CO2 emissions.

## Chapter 7: Alternating Current in Depth

### 7.1 Three-Phase Systems

Three-phase systems are the most common form of distribution and use of electric power in industrial and domestic settings. A three-phase system consists of three sinusoidal voltage sources phase-shifted 120 degrees from each other, which can be connected in star (Y) or delta configuration. In the star connection, each phase has a line voltage (between two phases) and a phase voltage (between phase and neutral), with the relationship V_line = sqrt(3) x V_phase. In the delta connection, the phase voltage equals the line voltage. Three-phase systems are more efficient than single-phase systems for transmitting power, since they use less conductor for the same power and provide more uniform power delivery. The power in a balanced three-phase system is P = sqrt(3) V I cos(phi), where V is the line voltage, I is the line current, and cos(phi) is the power factor.

Three-phase motors are simpler, more efficient, and cheaper than single-phase motors, and represent the majority of industrial motors. The three-phase induction motor, invented by Nikola Tesla in 1887, operates by creating a rotating magnetic field in the stator that induces current in the rotor. Induction motors are robust, economical, and do not require brushes, making them ideal for industrial applications. Synchronous three-phase motors are used in applications requiring constant speed, such as generators in power plants. Variable frequency drives (VFDs) allow controlling the speed of induction motors by varying the supply frequency, providing energy savings and precise control in applications such as pumps, fans, and conveyors. Three-phase systems are also used in heating, where three balanced resistors connected in star or delta provide uniform and efficient heating.

### 7.2 Alternating Current Circuit Analysis

Alternating current (AC) circuit analysis is more complex than direct current (DC) analysis because voltages and currents vary with time. The most common method for analyzing AC circuits is the phasor method, which represents sinusoidal voltages and currents as complex numbers with magnitude and phase. A phasor V = V0 e^(jphi) represents a voltage with amplitude V0 and phase phi. Impedance Z is the generalization of resistance for AC circuits, and is defined as the ratio between the voltage phasor and the current phasor: Z = V/I. The impedance of a resistor is Z_R = R, that of an inductor is Z_L = j omega L, and that of a capacitor is Z_C = 1/(j omega C), where omega = 2 pi f is the angular frequency. The total impedance of a series circuit is Z_total = Z_1 + Z_2 + ... + Z_n, and that of a parallel circuit is calculated similarly to parallel resistors.

The impedance triangle is a graphical representation that relates resistance R, reactance X, and impedance Z in a complex plane. Resistance R is the real part of the impedance, reactance X is the imaginary part, and impedance is the magnitude: Z = sqrt(R^2 + X^2). The impedance angle phi = arctan(X/R) determines the phase shift between voltage and current. When reactance is positive (inductive circuit), voltage leads current; when it is negative (capacitive circuit), current leads voltage. Resonance occurs when inductive reactance equals capacitive reactance (omega L = 1/(omega C)), and total impedance is minimum (series circuit) or maximum (parallel circuit). Resonant circuits are used in radio tuners, filters, and oscillators. The power factor cos(phi) measures the fraction of useful power consumed by the circuit, and a low power factor indicates inefficiency, since part of the power is continuously exchanged between the source and reactive components (inductors and capacitors).

## Chapter 8: Basic Electronics

### 8.1 Semiconductors and Diodes

Semiconductors are materials with electrical conductivity intermediate between conductors and insulators. Silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge) are the most widely used semiconductors, and their conductivity can be controlled through doping (adding impurities). N-type semiconductors have excess electrons (doped with phosphorus or arsenic), while P-type semiconductors have a deficit of electrons or holes (doped with boron or gallium). The P-N junction is formed when a P-type material and an N-type material are joined, creating a depletion zone where electrons and holes recombine. Current can easily flow through the P-N junction in the forward direction (from the P anode to the N cathode), but is blocked in the reverse direction. This unidirectional conduction property is the basis of the diode, a fundamental electronic component for alternating current rectification.

Diodes have multiple variants for different applications. The rectifier diode converts alternating current to direct current and is used in power supplies and chargers. The Zener diode is designed for reverse conduction at a specific voltage, used as a voltage regulator. Light-emitting diodes (LEDs) emit light when forward current is applied, and are used in lighting, indicators, and displays. LEDs are extremely efficient (up to 200 lumens/W) and long-lasting (50,000 hours), rapidly replacing incandescent and fluorescent lamps. Photodiodes generate current when illuminated and are used in light sensors, fiber optic communications, and solar panels. Schottky diodes have a lower forward voltage drop (0.2-0.3 V vs. 0.6-0.7 V for silicon diodes), making them ideal for high-efficiency applications.

### 8.2 Transistors

The transistor is the most important electronic component in modern electronics, since it allows amplifying signals and switching currents. It was invented in 1947 by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley at Bell Labs, and its development revolutionized technology, enabling the creation of integrated circuits and computers. Bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) have three terminals: collector, base, and emitter, and operate by controlling the collector current with a small base current. BJTs are used in analog signal amplifiers and digital circuits. Metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) are field-effect devices that control drain current with gate voltage. MOSFETs are the most widely used transistors in modern electronics due to their high input impedance, low power consumption, and high switching speed.

Transistors are the basic components of digital circuits, where they function as electronic switches. A MOSFET transistor can be in the "on" state (low resistance between drain and source) or "off" state (high resistance), representing the binary states 1 and 0. CMOS (Complementary MOS) technology uses pairs of NMOS and PMOS transistors to create logic circuits with minimal static power consumption. Modern processors contain billions of CMOS transistors on a chip of a few square centimeters, with transistor sizes of 3-5 nanometers. Moore's Law predicted that the number of transistors on an integrated circuit would double every two years, and although this trend has slowed, transistor density continues to increase. High electron mobility transistors (HEMTs) are used in high-frequency and power applications, such as radio frequency amplifiers and power converters.

### 8.3 Integrated Circuits

Integrated circuits (ICs) are devices that integrate multiple electronic components (transistors, resistors, capacitors, diodes) on a single semiconductor chip. ICs were independently invented by Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild Semiconductor) in 1958-1959, and their development has enabled the digital revolution. ICs are classified as Small Scale Integration (SSI, fewer than 100 components), Medium Scale Integration (MSI, 100-1000 components), Large Scale Integration (LSI, 1000-100,000 components), Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI, 100,000-10 million components), and Ultra Large Scale Integration (ULSI, more than 10 million components). Modern processors, such as Apple M2 and AMD Ryzen, contain up to 100 billion transistors on a single chip, and are the product of more than 60 years of continuous miniaturization.

Integrated circuits are manufactured using lithographic processes that create layers of semiconductor, conductor, and insulator material on a silicon substrate. The CMOS process uses ultraviolet photolithography to create transistor patterns with sizes of 3-5 nanometers in the most advanced chips. Semiconductor manufacturers such as TSMC, Samsung, and Intel compete to reach the smallest process nodes, since reducing transistor size allows higher density, lower power consumption, and higher speed. FPGAs (Field-Programmable Gate Arrays) are configurable integrated circuits that can be programmed to implement any logic circuit, used in rapid prototyping, signal processing, and high-performance computing. ASICs (Application-Specific Integrated Circuits) are integrated circuits designed for a specific application, such as cryptocurrency mining chips, deep learning processors, and device controllers. SoCs (System on a Chip) integrate multiple components (CPU, GPU, memory, connectivity) on a single chip, and are the foundation of smartphones and IoT devices.

## Chapter 9: Electric Power and Grids

### 9.1 Electric Power Generation

Electric power generation is the process of converting other forms of energy (mechanical, thermal, chemical, solar, nuclear) into electrical energy. Electric generators convert mechanical energy into electrical energy through electromagnetic induction: a conductor rotating in a magnetic field induces an electromotive force (EMF). Direct current generators use brushes and commutators to extract current, while alternating current generators use slip rings. Alternators are AC generators used in power plants, vehicles, and aircraft. The generated electric power depends on the rotational speed, the magnetic field strength, and the number of winding turns. Modern generators use rare-earth magnets (NdFeB) for stronger magnetic fields and higher efficiency.

Power plants convert fuels or energy sources into electricity on a large scale. Combined-cycle gas plants use gas turbines to generate electricity and harness the residual heat to produce steam that drives a second turbine, achieving efficiencies of 55-62%. Coal plants burn coal to generate steam that drives turbines, but are the most polluting source of electricity and are being progressively closed. Nuclear plants use uranium nuclear fission to generate heat, which produces steam to drive turbines, with efficiencies of 33-37%. Hydroelectric, wind, and solar plants generate electricity without combustion, with low operating costs and minimal emissions. The combination of different generation sources in an electrical system ensures a reliable and economical supply.

### 9.2 Transmission and Distribution

Electric power transmission transports electricity from generation plants to distribution substations through high-voltage lines. Transmission voltages range from 110 kV to 765 kV in Europe and from 115 kV to 765 kV in North America. High voltages reduce transmission losses, since the power lost in conductors is P_loss = I^2 R, and higher voltage allows lower current for the same power. Step-up transformers increase voltage at power plants, and step-down transformers reduce it at distribution substations. Transmission lines can be overhead (aerial cabling with high-voltage towers) or underground (buried cables). Underground lines are more expensive but have less visual impact and are less vulnerable to extreme weather conditions.

Electric power distribution transports electricity from substations to end consumers through medium-voltage lines (1-36 kV) and low-voltage lines (230/400 V in Europe, 120/240 V in North America). Distribution substations reduce voltage from medium to low for domestic and industrial supply. Distribution cables can be overhead (on wooden or concrete poles) or underground (in conduits or directly buried). Underground distribution is increasingly common in urban areas, where space is limited and reliability is critical. Modern distribution networks include sensors, automatic switches, and remote control systems that allow detecting and isolating faults quickly, minimizing supply interruptions.

### 9.3 Power Grid Management

Power grid management is the set of operations and technologies that ensure a reliable, safe, and economical electrical supply. The system operator (SO) coordinates generation, transmission, and distribution to balance supply and demand in real time. The generation-demand balance is critical, since electricity is not easily stored (except with batteries and other storage technologies), and generation must match demand at every instant. System operators use demand forecasting and renewable generation models to plan system operation. Wholesale electricity markets allow generators to bid their production and buyers to purchase it, establishing prices based on supply and demand. Electricity prices vary throughout the day, with demand peaks in the morning and evening, and valleys during the night.

The modern power grid is evolving toward a smart grid that integrates information and communication technologies to optimize operations. Smart meters (AMI) provide real-time consumption data, enabling dynamic pricing, demand response, and detection of technical and non-technical losses. The integration of variable renewable energy (solar and wind) requires flexibility in the system, which is obtained through energy storage, demand management, international interconnections, and flexible generation. Electric vehicles represent both an opportunity (mobile storage, demand management) and a challenge (increased demand, need for charging infrastructure) for the power grid. Cybersecurity of the power grid is a growing concern, since digitalization increases the attack surface and cyberattacks can have critical consequences for energy infrastructure.

## Chapter 10: Electrical Safety

### 10.1 Electrical Hazards

Electricity can be dangerous if not handled properly, since it can cause electric shocks, burns, fires, and electrocution. The severity of an electric shock depends on the current passing through the body, the duration of contact, the current's path through the body, and the resistance of the human body. Human body resistance ranges from 1000 ohms (wet skin) to 100,000 ohms (dry skin), and most electrocutions occur at voltages above 50 V in wet environments. A current of 1 mA causes a tingling sensation, 10 mA causes involuntary muscle contractions (and may prevent releasing a conductor), 100 mA is potentially fatal if it passes through the heart (ventricular fibrillation), and 1 A or more causes severe burns and cardiac arrest. Factors that increase risk include humidity, the presence of metals, direct contact with live conductors, and the lack of electrical protection.

Electrical burns are one of the most serious injuries caused by electricity, since the heat generated by the current can destroy deep tissue without significant external evidence. The entry burn (where current enters the body) and the exit burn (where it leaves) are usually more severe than the internal burn along the current's path. Arc flashes are discharges through the air between two electrodes, generating temperatures of up to 20,000°C, intense ultraviolet light, and pressure waves. Arc flashes can cause severe burns, temporary blindness, and explosion of nearby materials. Arc flash protection is achieved through appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), safety distances, and arc protection devices (APDs) in electrical panels.

### 10.2 Protection Systems

Electrical protection systems are devices and techniques designed to prevent electric shocks, fires, and equipment damage. Circuit breakers protect circuits against overloads and short circuits, opening the circuit when current exceeds a threshold value. Molded-case circuit breakers combine thermal protection (against overloads) and magnetic protection (against short circuits). Residual current devices (RCDs) detect the difference in current between the live conductor and the neutral, opening the circuit if there is a ground fault current exceeding 30 mA. RCDs are essential for protection against electric shocks in wet environments, such as bathrooms, kitchens, and outdoors. The combination of a molded-case circuit breaker and an RCD in a single device (RCBO) provides complete protection in a single module.

Grounding is a safety system that connects the metal parts of electrical installations to the ground, providing a low-resistance path for fault current. When a live conductor touches a metal casing, fault current flows through the grounding system and causes the RCD or fuse to trip, cutting off the power. Grounding systems include the protective conductor (PE), the ground electrode, earth rods, and equipotential conductors. The IEC 60364 standard establishes requirements for low-voltage electrical installations, including protection against electric shock, overcurrents, overvoltages, and fires. Electrical installations must be performed by qualified electricians and periodically inspected to ensure safety and regulatory compliance.

### 10.3 Electricity and Health

Exposure to low-frequency electromagnetic fields (50/60 Hz), such as those generated by transmission lines and household appliances, has been studied for decades. The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies low-frequency electromagnetic fields as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B) based on epidemiological studies that showed a slight association between prolonged exposure to high-intensity magnetic fields and childhood leukemia. However, current scientific evidence does not establish a clear causal relationship, and exposure levels under normal conditions are much lower than known effect thresholds. Protection standards establish exposure limits based on known effects (stimulation of nervous and cardiac tissue), with significant safety margins.

Static electricity is the accumulation of electric charges on the surface of materials, which occurs when two materials are rubbed together (triboelectric effect). Static electricity discharge can cause discomfort, damage to electronic equipment, and, in environments with flammable gases, fires or explosions. Static electricity prevention is achieved through grounding, air humidification, the use of conductive materials, and ionic neutralization devices. Antistatic materials are used in the electronics industry, in component manufacturing, and in work clothing to prevent discharges that could damage sensitive circuits. Electrostatic discharge (ESD) prevention is critical in semiconductor manufacturing, where a discharge of only 25 volts can permanently damage a transistor.

## Chapter 11: Power Electronics

### 11.1 Rectifiers and Inverters

Rectifiers are circuits that convert alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC), and are fundamental in power supplies, battery chargers, and direct current power transmission systems. Half-wave rectifiers use a single diode that conducts only during half a cycle of the AC signal, producing a pulsating DC signal that requires filtering. Full-wave bridge rectifiers use four diodes that alternate conduction during both cycles, producing a more uniform pulsating DC. Controlled rectifiers use thyristors (SCRs) instead of diodes, allowing output voltage control by varying the firing angle. Three-phase bridge rectifiers are used in high-power industrial applications, such as electric tractor battery charging, electric train power supply, and HVDC (High Voltage Direct Current) transmission.

Inverters convert direct current to alternating current, and are essential in photovoltaic systems, electric vehicles, UPS, and energy storage systems. Basic inverters use electronic switches (MOSFETs or IGBTs) that switch the DC signal to generate an approximately sinusoidal AC signal. Square-wave inverters generate a two-level AC signal, which is simple but has high harmonic content. Modified-wave inverters generate a signal with three or more levels, improving signal quality. PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) inverters generate variable-width pulses that, after filtering, produce a nearly sinusoidal AC signal with low harmonic content. Three-phase inverters are used in variable-speed motors, solar and wind generation plants, and in interconnecting energy storage systems with the power grid. Dual-source inverters allow bidirectional energy exchange between a DC system (battery, solar panel) and the AC grid, facilitating the integration of distributed energy resources.

### 11.2 DC-DC Converters and Chargers

DC-DC converters are power electronics circuits that change the voltage level of a direct current source, and are fundamental in electronic devices, electric vehicles, and renewable energy systems. Buck (step-down) converters reduce input voltage and are used in processors, memory, and low-voltage devices. Boost (step-up) converters increase input voltage and are used in photovoltaic systems to raise solar panel voltage to the level required by the inverter. Buck-boost converters can both reduce and increase voltage, providing design flexibility. Flyback and forward converters are isolated converters that provide galvanic isolation between input and output, important in safety applications. Resonant converters (LLC, series resonant) use resonance to achieve soft commutation, reducing losses and improving efficiency, and are used in battery chargers and high-efficiency power supplies.

Battery chargers are devices that supply controlled current to a battery to recharge it safely and efficiently. Lead-acid battery chargers use a three-stage charging strategy: bulk charging (constant current), absorption (constant voltage), and float (maintenance). Lithium-ion battery chargers use a CC-CV (constant current, constant voltage) charging strategy that maximizes charging capacity without overcharging the battery. Fast chargers for electric vehicles (DC fast charging) can provide 50-350 kW of power, recharging an electric vehicle to 80% in 15-30 minutes. Inductive chargers use varying magnetic fields to transfer energy without physical contact, and inductive chargers for electric vehicles that allow automatic charging when parked are being developed. Battery management systems (BMS) monitor the voltage, current, temperature, and state of charge of each cell, ensuring safe charging and maximizing battery life.

## Chapter 12: Telecommunications and Communication Electronics

### 12.1 Modulation and Demodulation

Modulation is the process of varying a high-frequency carrier signal according to a low-frequency information signal, enabling the transmission of information through communication channels. Amplitude modulation (AM) varies the amplitude of the carrier according to the information signal, and is used in medium-wave and short-wave radio. Frequency modulation (FM) varies the frequency of the carrier, providing better audio quality and greater noise resistance than AM, and is used in FM radio and television. Phase modulation (PM) varies the phase of the carrier according to the information signal. Phase-shift keying (PSK) encodes information in phase changes of the carrier and is used in digital communications. Quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) combines amplitude and phase modulation to transmit multiple bits per symbol, with variants such as 16-QAM, 64-QAM, and 256-QAM used in WiFi, LTE, and 5G.

Demodulation is the reverse process of modulation, which extracts the information signal from the modulated carrier signal. AM demodulators use envelope detectors or synchronous demodulators. FM demodulators use frequency discriminators or phase-locked loops (PLLs). Digital demodulators use digital signal processing (DSP) to demodulate PSK, QAM, and other digital modulations. Frequency-division multiplexing (FDM) assigns different frequency ranges to different communication channels, enabling simultaneous transmission of multiple signals. Time-division multiplexing (TDM) assigns different time intervals to different channels. Code-division multiplexing (CDM) uses unique codes to distinguish between different channels, and is the basis of CDMA and WCDMA communications.

### 12.2 Digital Communication Systems

Digital communication systems transmit information in the form of bits (0 and 1) using electromagnetic signals. Digital systems have advantages over analog systems: greater noise resistance, greater transmission capacity, ease of processing, and encryption. Channel coding techniques, such as error-correcting codes (ECC), detect and correct transmission errors. Convolutional codes and turbo codes are used in satellite and mobile communications. LDPC (Low-Density Parity-Check) codes and Reed-Solomon codes are used in data storage (CD, DVD, Blu-ray) and high-speed communications. Error detection and correction is fundamental to ensuring data integrity in the presence of noise and interference.

Wireless communications use the electromagnetic spectrum to transmit information without cables. Radio frequency (RF) communications include AM, FM, television, WiFi, Bluetooth, LTE, and 5G. Microwave communications are used in point-to-point links, satellite communications, and radars. Infrared communications are used in remote controls and short-distance communications. Fiber optic communications use light pulses to transmit data at high speeds over long distances. Satellite communications provide global coverage for television, telephony, navigation, and emergency communications. 5G communications use millimeter-wave frequencies (24-100 GHz) and massive MIMO to achieve transmission speeds of up to 20 Gbps and latencies below 1 ms, enabling applications such as autonomous vehicles, augmented reality, and the Internet of Things (IoT).

## Chapter 13: Embedded Systems and Microcontrollers

### 13.1 Microcontrollers

Microcontrollers are integrated electronic systems that combine a processor, RAM and ROM memory, and peripherals (UART, SPI, I2C, ADC, PWM) on a single chip. Microcontrollers are used in embedded devices where complexity, power consumption, and cost are critical. 8-bit microcontrollers such as Microchip's PIC and Atmel's AVR are used in simple applications such as remote controls, thermometers, and consumer devices. 32-bit microcontrollers such as STMicroelectronics' STM32 and Espressif's ESP32 provide higher performance and functionality, with ARM Cortex-M cores reaching frequencies of up to 480 MHz. 32-bit microcontrollers are used in industrial control systems, medical devices, home automation, and the Internet of Things (IoT).

Modern microcontrollers include advanced peripherals such as high-resolution analog-to-digital converters (ADCs), sigma-delta modulators for low-speed signals, CAN bus controllers for automotive applications, and wireless communication modules (WiFi, Bluetooth, LoRa). Popular microcontroller families include the STM32 series (ARM Cortex-M0/M3/M4/M7), the ESP32 series (Xtensa with WiFi and Bluetooth), the PIC32 series (MIPS), and the RP2040 series (ARM Cortex-M0+ with Python-programmable interface). Low-power microcontrollers, such as Texas Instruments' MSP430 series and Nordic Semiconductor's nRF52 series, are designed for battery-powered applications lasting years. Development kits such as Arduino and development boards such as Raspberry Pi Pico have democratized access to embedded electronics, allowing hobbyists and professionals to create intelligent devices with relative ease.

### 13.2 Internet of Things (IoT)

The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to the network of physical devices connected to the Internet that collect, process, and transmit data. IoT devices include environmental sensors, wearable devices, smart home appliances, surveillance systems, medical devices, and industrial sensors. A typical IoT system architecture includes sensors/actuators at the perception layer, a communication network (WiFi, Bluetooth, LoRa, NB-IoT, LTE-M) for data transmission, a cloud device management platform, and user applications for data visualization and analysis. IoT communication protocols include MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Streaming), CoAP (Constrained Application Protocol), and HTTP/HTTPS. IoT devices typically use low-power microcontrollers and low-resolution sensors to minimize energy consumption and costs.

Security in IoT is a critical challenge, since IoT devices are frequent targets of cyberattacks due to their large numbers and device resource limitations. Threats include unauthorized access, denial of service, malware injection, and espionage. IoT security strategies include data encryption (AES, RSA), device authentication, secure firmware updates, network segmentation, and anomaly monitoring. The Matter framework, developed by a consortium of companies including Apple, Google, Amazon, and Samsung, aims to standardize communication between smart home devices, improving interoperability and security. IoT data analytics uses artificial intelligence and machine learning techniques to extract valuable information from sensor data, enabling fault prediction, process optimization, and automated decision-making.

## Chapter 14: Automation and Industrial Control

### 14.1 Control Systems

Control systems regulate the behavior of processes and machinery to maintain variables at desired values. Open-loop control systems execute an action without measuring the result, such as a timer that turns on a light at a programmed time. Closed-loop (feedback) control systems measure the controlled variable and adjust the action to minimize the error between the desired and actual values. The PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) controller is the most widely used control algorithm in industry, with approximately 95% of industrial control applications. The proportional term adjusts the action proportional to the current error, the integral term eliminates steady-state error, and the derivative term predicts the error trend to improve stability. PID controllers are implemented in PLCs, microcontrollers, and DCS systems.

PLCs (Programmable Logic Controllers) are industrial computers designed to control manufacturing processes in real time. PLCs execute ladder logic, function block diagram, structured text, and other languages defined by the IEC 61131-3 standard. Modern PLCs include Ethernet communications, field buses (Profibus, Modbus, CANopen), and data analytics capabilities. SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) systems monitor and control industrial processes through graphical interfaces (HMIs), allowing operators to view process status, adjust parameters, and respond to alarms. DCS (Distributed Control Systems) are used in continuous processes such as chemical plants, refineries, and power plants, where reliability and availability are critical.

### 14.2 Industrial Sensors and Actuators

Sensors are devices that measure physical variables (temperature, pressure, level, flow, position) and convert the measurement into an electrical signal. Temperature sensors include thermocouples (which generate a voltage proportional to the temperature difference between two metals), RTDs (Resistance Temperature Detectors, which change their resistance with temperature), and thermistors (semiconductor resistors with high sensitivity). Pressure sensors include piezoelectric, capacitive, and strain gauge sensors. Level sensors use ultrasonic, radar, hydrostatic pressure, or capacitance. Flow sensors include turbine, electromagnetic, ultrasonic, and Coriolis types. Optical and magnetic encoders measure the position and angular velocity of motors and shafts, with resolutions up to 24 bits. Machine vision sensors use cameras and image processing algorithms for inspection, classification, and quality control.

Actuators convert control signals into movement or physical action. Electric actuators include direct current motors, stepper motors (which move in precise increments), servo motors (with position feedback), and solenoids (linear movement). Pneumatic actuators use compressed air to generate movement, with high speed and significant force. Hydraulic actuators use high-pressure oil to generate very large forces, used in heavy machinery, presses, and aircraft steering systems. Variable frequency drives (VFDs) control the speed of alternating current motors by varying the supply frequency and voltage, providing energy savings (up to 50% in pump and fan applications) and precise speed control. Servomotors with motion controllers enable precise positioning in applications such as robotics, machine tools, and material handling systems.

## Chapter 15: Energy and Smart Grids

### 15.1 Smart Grids

Smart grids are electrical systems that integrate communication, information, and automation technologies to optimize the generation, distribution, and consumption of electric power. Smart grids enable the integration of distributed renewable energy (rooftop solar panels, wind turbines), energy storage, electric vehicles as demand resources, and active consumer participation in the energy market. Smart meters (AMI, Advanced Metering Infrastructure) provide real-time consumption data, enabling dynamic pricing (kWh price that varies with demand), demand response (reducing consumption during peak hours), and automatic fault detection. Distribution management systems (DMS) monitor and control the distribution grid in real time, enabling automatic reconnection after faults, energy flow optimization, and power quality management.

Distributed generation is the production of electricity near the point of consumption, rather than in large centralized power plants. Distributed photovoltaic systems, micro gas turbines, cogenerators, and distributed batteries are examples of distributed generation. Distributed generation reduces transmission losses, improves supply reliability, and allows consumers to become energy producers (prosumers). Microgrids are electrical systems that can operate connected to the main grid or autonomously (island mode), providing reliability and resilience in case of grid failures. Microgrids are used on university campuses, military installations, data centers, and isolated communities. The transition from a centralized to a distributed power grid requires new regulatory frameworks, market mechanisms, and control technologies.

### 15.2 Electric Vehicles and the Grid

Electric vehicles represent both an opportunity and a challenge for the power grid. As an opportunity, EVs can function as distributed storage resources (Vehicle-to-Grid, V2G), providing grid balancing services during demand peaks. As a challenge, simultaneous charging of multiple EVs in the same area can overload the distribution grid, especially during peak hours. Intelligent EV charging management (smart charging) uses algorithms that optimize the timing and power of charging, minimizing grid impact and taking advantage of low-demand hours and high renewable energy availability. Bidirectional chargers allow energy flow between the EV and the grid (V2G) or between the EV and the building (Vehicle-to-Home, V2H). Pay-per-use systems and mobility-as-a-service (MaaS) platforms are transforming the relationship between mobility, energy, and the power grid.

EV charging infrastructure includes residential chargers (3.7-22 kW, charging in 6-8 hours), commercial chargers (22-150 kW, charging in 30-60 minutes), and rapid DC chargers (150-350 kW, charging to 80% in 15-30 minutes). Charging standards include CHAdeMO (Japan), CCS (Combined Charging System, Europe/US), and Tesla Supercharger (now open to other manufacturers in Europe). The rapid charger network on highways is critical for long-distance travel, and countries are investing in expanding this infrastructure. Solar-integrated chargers use photovoltaic panels to charge EVs directly with solar energy, reducing grid demand and CO2 emissions. Urban planning must integrate EV charging infrastructure into residential, commercial, and public spaces, considering available electrical capacity and citizens' mobility needs.

## Chapter 16: Electricity and Renewable Energy

### 16.1 Photovoltaic Grid Integration

The integration of photovoltaic systems into the power grid poses technical and economic challenges that require innovative solutions. The intermittency of solar generation (it only works during the day, with variations due to cloud cover) requires grid flexibility, which is obtained through energy storage, demand management, interconnections, and flexible generation. Regional variability of solar irradiation allows geographical diversification: when it is sunny in one region, it may be cloudy in another, reducing the total system variability. Modern solar inverters include grid support functions such as frequency regulation, power limiting, voltage control, and fast reconnection service. Solar monitoring and forecasting systems use satellite imagery and weather models to predict solar generation with accuracy.

Distributed photovoltaic systems (on building rooftops) and centralized systems (utility-scale plants) have different grid integration characteristics. Distributed systems reduce transmission losses and grid demand, but can cause voltage and power quality issues in distribution networks. Centralized systems generate electricity on a large scale with lower costs per kWh, but require dedicated transmission lines and can cause transmission losses. Solar-battery hybrids combine photovoltaic panels with battery storage to provide firm and dispatchable electricity. Solar-wind hybrids harness the temporal complementarity of both sources (the sun shines during the day, wind blows more at night and in winter). Long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) provide price stability for both developers and buyers.

### 16.2 Microelectrification and Distributed Solar Energy

Microelectrification is the provision of energy services at the household or community scale using decentralized renewable energy systems. Off-grid solar systems with batteries provide electricity to rural communities isolated from the central grid. Typical sizes range from domestic systems of 50-200 W to community microgrids of 10-100 kW. Pay-as-you-go (PAYG) is a business model that allows households to pay for solar systems in daily or weekly installments through mobile payments, making solar energy accessible to low-income households. Solar systems with reduced upfront payment and leasing models are alternatives that overcome the initial cost barrier.

Solar systems with battery storage are transforming the reliability and economics of distributed energy. Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries are preferred for stationary storage applications due to their long lifespan (over 6000 cycles), high safety, and competitive cost. Energy management systems (EMS) optimize battery charging and discharging, maximizing solar self-consumption and minimizing grid exchange. Hybrid systems combine multiple energy sources (solar, wind, diesel generator) with storage to provide reliable electricity supply in remote areas. Solar energy with storage is competing economically with diesel generators in many applications, with levelized cost of energy (LCOE) below 0.20 USD/kWh in the best locations.

## Chapter 17: Advanced Circuit Analysis

### 17.1 Thévenin and Norton Theorems

Thévenin's theorem states that any linear circuit with two terminals can be replaced by a Thévenin voltage source (Vth) in series with a Thévenin resistance (Rth). The Thévenin voltage is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals, and the Thévenin resistance is the equivalent resistance seen from the terminals with all sources turned off. Norton's theorem states that the same circuit can be replaced by a Norton current source (In) in parallel with a Norton resistance (Rn). The Norton current is the short-circuit current at the terminals, and the Norton resistance equals the Thévenin resistance. The relationship between both representations is Vth = In x Rth. These theorems are fundamental for simplifying complex circuits and calculating the power transferred to a load.

### 17.2 AC Signal Analysis

Phasor analysis allows solving alternating current circuits using complex numbers. A phasor V = Vm e^(jphi) represents a sinusoidal voltage V(t) = Vm cos(omega t + phi). The impedance Z = R + jX represents the opposition to current flow in an AC circuit, where R is the resistance (energy dissipation) and X is the reactance (energy storage). Power in an AC circuit is decomposed into active power P (W), reactive power Q (VAR), and apparent power S (VA): S = P + jQ. The power factor cos(phi) = P/S indicates the fraction of useful power. Capacitor banks are used to correct inductive power factor, reducing losses and improving system efficiency.

### 17.3 Resonance in RLC Circuits

Electrical resonance occurs when inductive reactance equals capacitive reactance (XL = XC), minimizing (series circuit) or maximizing (parallel circuit) total impedance. The resonant frequency is f0 = 1/(2pi sqrt(LC)). In a series resonant circuit, the current reaches its maximum value and the voltages across L and C can be many times greater than the source voltage. The quality factor Q = (1/R) sqrt(L/C) indicates circuit selectivity: a high Q means a narrow passband. Resonant circuits are used in radio tuners (frequency selectors), band-pass filters, oscillators, and antennas. Solid-state filters use resonant circuits to eliminate harmonics in power grids, improving power quality.

## Chapter 18: Domestic and Industrial Applications

### 18.1 Home Electronics

Modern household appliances incorporate multiple electronic systems that improve their operation, efficiency, and connectivity. Washing machines use microcontrollers to control the wash cycle, load sensors to adjust water and energy consumption, and high-efficiency permanent magnet motors. Refrigerators use variable-speed (inverter) compressors that adjust their power according to cooling demand, reducing consumption by 30-50% compared to conventional compressors. Microwave ovens use a magnetron to generate 2.45 GHz microwaves that heat water in food. Robot vacuum cleaners use distance sensors, cameras, and SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) algorithms to navigate and clean autonomously. Home automation systems allow centralized control of lighting, climate control, security, and appliances through mobile apps, voice assistants, or programmed automations.

### 18.2 Industrial Energy Efficiency

Industry represents approximately 30% of global energy consumption, and industrial energy efficiency is crucial for cost and emission reduction. Industrial electric motors represent 70% of industrial electricity consumption, and their optimization can reduce consumption by 20-30%. Variable frequency drives (VFDs) adjust motor speed according to demand, saving energy in pump, fan, and compressor applications. Waste heat recovery systems convert heat lost in industrial processes into useful electricity or heat. ISO 50001 energy management systems provide a systematic framework for continuous energy efficiency improvement. Energy auditors identify savings opportunities and evaluate the profitability of energy efficiency investments, with typical payback periods of 1-5 years.

### 18.3 Electrical Safety in Installations

Electrical safety in installations requires compliance with specific standards and regulations. The IEC 60364 standard establishes requirements for low-voltage electrical installations, including protection against electric shock, overcurrents, overvoltages, and fires. Residual current devices (RCDs) with 30 mA sensitivity are mandatory in circuits supplying outlets in bathrooms, kitchens, and outdoors. Grounding must have a resistance below 4 ohms for low-voltage installations. Electrical panels must include overcurrent protection (circuit breakers) and ground fault protection (RCDs). Electrical installations in public venues must meet additional requirements for emergency, security lighting, and accessibility. Periodic verification of electrical installations by an authorized installer is mandatory to ensure safety and correct operation.

## Chapter 19: Electrical Safety and Standards

### 19.1 European and Spanish Standards

Electrical standards in Europe are harmonized through European directives that member states transpose into their national legislation. The Low Voltage Directive (2014/35/EU) establishes safety requirements for electrical equipment in the range of 50-1000 V AC and 75-1500 V DC. The EMC Directive (2014/30/EU) regulates the electromagnetic compatibility of equipment, ensuring that it does not cause interference and is not susceptible to it. In Spain, the Low Voltage Electrical Regulation (REBT) is the main regulatory framework for electrical installations, with the ITC (Complementary Technical Instruction) specifying the technical requirements for each type of installation. The REBT classifies installations into groups according to their complexity, and requires a project designer, site manager, and authorized installer depending on the group.

### 19.2 Certification and Homologation

Electrical equipment must meet safety and electromagnetic compatibility requirements to be marketed in the European Union. The CE mark indicates compliance with applicable European directives and is mandatory for selling equipment in the EEA. The UKCA marking replaces the CE mark in the United Kingdom since Brexit. Notified bodies evaluate high-risk equipment (such as medical equipment and fire protection equipment) and issue conformity certificates. Testing laboratories perform electrical safety tests (insulation resistance, leakage current, temperature), EMC tests (radiated emissions, immunity), and functional tests. IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) standards provide technical requirements for electrical equipment and are adopted as harmonized standards in the European Union. IEC 60364 standards for installations, IEC 60947 for switchgear and protection equipment, and IEC 62310 for interference are some of the most relevant standards.

### 19.3 Inspection and Maintenance

Periodic inspection of electrical installations is fundamental to ensuring safety and correct operation. Visual inspections verify the condition of cables, connections, protections, and equipment. Electrical tests measure insulation resistance, protective conductor continuity, earth loop impedance, RCD trip current, and contact voltage. Infrared thermography detects hot spots in connections and equipment, indicating contact or overload problems before they cause failures. Power quality analyzers measure harmonics, flicker, phase imbalance, and power factor, identifying problems that could damage equipment or cause energy losses. Predictive maintenance programs use sensor data and trend analysis to predict failures and schedule repairs proactively, reducing downtime and maintenance costs.

## Chapter 21: Embedded Electronics and IoT

### 21.1 Modern Embedded Systems

Embedded systems are specialized computers integrated into devices that perform specific tasks. Unlike general-purpose computers, embedded systems are optimized for a specific application with requirements for cost, power consumption, size, and reliability. Modern microcontrollers such as the STM32H7 (ARM Cortex-M7, 480 MHz, 1 MB RAM) and the ESP32-S3 (Xtensa LX7, 240 MHz, 512 KB RAM) provide significant processing capabilities in low-cost chips. Embedded operating systems such as FreeRTOS, Zephyr, and Mbed OS manage tasks, memory, and peripherals in real time. Development frameworks such as Arduino, PlatformIO, and the ESP-IDF SDK simplify embedded application development, allowing developers to focus on business logic rather than hardware details.

### 21.2 Sensors and Actuators for IoT

IoT sensors include environmental sensors (temperature, humidity, pressure, air quality), motion sensors (accelerometers, gyroscopes, magnetometers), proximity sensors (ultrasonic, infrared, LiDAR), and light sensors (photoresistors, photodiodes). MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems) sensors combine mechanics and electronics at the micrometer scale, enabling the creation of tiny, inexpensive sensors for mobile and wearable devices. IoT actuators include stepper motors, servomotors, solenoids, relays, and dimmers. Energy harvesting systems allow IoT devices to operate without batteries, using solar, thermal, vibration, or RF energy from the environment.

### 21.3 Cloud Platforms and Edge Computing

Cloud IoT platforms such as AWS IoT Core, Azure IoT Hub, and Google Cloud IoT provide services for the registration, authentication, collection, and analysis of IoT device data. Edge computing processes data near the source (on the device or a local gateway) rather than sending it to the cloud, reducing latency, bandwidth, and costs. Edge computing frameworks such as AWS Greengrass, Azure IoT Edge, and KubeEdge allow running business logic, ML models, and local storage on edge devices. The combination of edge and cloud enables a hierarchical architecture where critical data is processed locally for fast responses, and aggregated data is sent to the cloud for long-term analysis. IoT device management includes OTA (Over-The-Air) updates, status monitoring, remote diagnostics, and device lifecycle management.

### 21.4 Security in IoT Systems

IoT system security is a critical challenge due to the large attack surface and device resource limitations. Threats include unauthorized device access, data interception, denial of service, malicious firmware injection, and physical attacks. IoT security strategies include data encryption at rest and in transit (AES-256, TLS 1.3), mutual authentication between devices and servers, signed and secure firmware updates, network segmentation, and anomalous behavior monitoring. Secure Elements provide secure hardware storage of cryptographic keys. IoT security frameworks such as ARM's PSA (Platform Security Architecture) and SESIP (Security Evaluation Standard for IoT Platforms) provide standards for evaluating and certifying IoT device security.
