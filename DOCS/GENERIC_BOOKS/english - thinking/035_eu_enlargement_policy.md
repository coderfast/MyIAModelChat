The enlargement policy is one of the most important political tools of the European Union, which allows the accession of new Member States to the organization through the fulfillment of a set of political, economic, and legal conditions known as the Copenhagen criteria. This policy has been a fundamental driver of European integration since the creation of the European Economic Community in 1957, and has allowed the transformation of an organization of six founding countries into a union of 27 Member States that covers most of the European continent. The enlargement policy is based on the principle that the European Union is open to all European countries that meet the accession criteria, and that enlargement benefits both the countries that accede and the European Union as a whole. The accession criteria, known as the Copenhagen criteria, were established by the Copenhagen European Council in 1993 and include political criteria (democratic stability, respect for human rights, protection of minorities, the rule of law, and the functioning of a market economy), economic criteria (the existence of a viable market economy and the capacity to cope with competition within the Union), and legal criteria (the capacity to assume the obligations of membership, including adherence to the political, economic, and monetary objectives of the Union). The enlargement policy also includes the acquis communautaire, which is the body of rights and obligations that constitutes the body of European law, and which candidate countries must adopt and implement in their entirety before accession. The European Commission supervises the accession process through the monitoring mechanism, which assesses the progress of candidate countries in implementing the reforms necessary to meet the accession criteria.

Despite the significant benefits, the enlargement policy faces important challenges that must be addressed to ensure its long-term success. One of the main challenges is the absorption capacity of the European Union, which refers to the Union's capacity to integrate new members without compromising its effectiveness, coherence, and cohesion. The political criteria include the stability of institutions that guarantee democracy, the rule of law, human rights, respect for minorities and their protection, and the existence of a viable market economy, as well as the capacity to cope with competition and market forces within the Union. The economic criteria include the existence of a viable market economy and the capacity to cope with competition and market forces within the Union, which implies the existence of market mechanisms, macroeconomic stability, currency convertibility, and the capacity to compete in the single market. The legal criteria include the capacity to assume the obligations of membership, including adherence to the political, economic, and monetary objectives of the Union, the acceptance of the acquis communautaire in its entirety, and the capacity to implement the acquis effectively. The European Commission assesses compliance with the Copenhagen criteria through the monitoring mechanism, which includes annual reports on the progress of candidate countries, Council recommendations, and political dialogues between the Commission and candidate countries. The acquis communautaire is the body of rights and obligations that constitutes the body of European law, and which candidate countries must adopt and implement in their entirety before their accession to the European Union. The acquis includes the founding treaty of the European Union, derived legislation (regulations, directives, and decisions), the general principles of European law, the case law of the Court of Justice, and the international agreements concluded by the European Union. The acquis is organized into 35 chapters covering areas such as the free movement of goods, the free movement of persons, the free movement of services, the free movement of capital, agriculture, fisheries, transport, energy, economic and monetary affairs, competition policy, trade policy, social policy, the environment, consumer affairs, justice and home affairs, science and technology, education and culture, the common foreign and security policy, and judicial and police cooperation. Candidate countries must demonstrate that they have the capacity to assume the obligations of the acquis communautaire, which implies the adoption of European legislation in their national legal systems, the creation of institutions capable of implementing the acquis, and the demonstration of a track record of effective implementation. The European Commission assesses the progress of candidate countries in implementing the acquis through the monitoring mechanism, which includes annual reports on progress in each chapter of the acquis, and which determines when a country is ready to start accession negotiations in each thematic area. The process of adopting the acquis is long and complex, and can take several years, as it requires significant political, economic, and legal reforms that affect multiple areas of public administration and the economy.

The accession process is the mechanism through which a country that meets the accession criteria can join the European Union, and consists of several stages that include the application for accession, the Commission's assessment, the accession negotiations, the signing of the accession treaty, and the ratification of the treaty by the Member States and the candidate country. The first stage is the application for accession, which is submitted by the country wishing to join the European Union, and which must be accompanied by a dossier demonstrating that the country meets the accession criteria. The European Commission assesses the application and issues an opinion on the country's readiness to start accession negotiations, which is submitted to the European Council for approval. If the European Council approves the Commission's opinion, accession negotiations are opened, which are organized in chapters corresponding to the areas of the acquis communautaire. The negotiations are based on the gradual consolidation approach, which involves the adoption, implementation, and effective application of the acquis in its entirety before accession. When negotiations in all chapters have been concluded satisfactorily, an accession treaty is signed between the European Union and the candidate country, which must be ratified by the national parliaments of all Member States and by the parliament of the candidate country. The accession process can take several years, depending on the speed at which the candidate country implements the necessary reforms and the complexity of the negotiations.
