Trade policy is one of the most important competences that the Member States have transferred to the European Union, making the EU a major actor on the international trade stage. The EU's exclusive competence in trade means that only the EU institutions, and not individual Member States, have the power to negotiate and conclude trade agreements with third countries, to establish common customs duties, and to adopt trade policy measures such as anti-dumping measures, trade defence measures and export restrictions. This centralization of trade policy has enabled the EU to negotiate trade agreements from a position of strength, as it represents a market of more than 450 million consumers with a combined GDP exceeding EUR 15 trillion, making it the world's largest market and a trading actor with significant negotiating power.

The EU's exclusive competence in trade is based on the Treaties of the European Union, which establish that the common trade policy is an integral part of the internal market and that the provisions relating to trade with third countries are uniformly applicable. The common customs tariff is one of the pillars of the European Union's trade policy and consists of the application of a uniform tariff on imports from third countries across all EU Member States. The common customs tariff aims to protect European producers from unfair competition from subsidised or dumped imports, and to provide a source of revenue for the EU budget. The common customs tariff is calculated as a percentage of the value of imports, and varies according to the type of product, with higher rates for agricultural products that require greater protection against international competition, and lower or zero rates for industrial products that the EU does not produce or produces in insufficient quantities to meet domestic demand. The common customs tariff is administered by the customs authorities of the Member States, which apply EU customs rules at the EU's external borders.

Anti-dumping measures are trade policy instruments that the EU can use to protect its producers against the importation of products sold at prices below their normal value, known as dumping prices. Dumping is an unfair trade practice that can harm European producers by creating unfair competition that reduces prices and profit margins of domestic producers. The EU's anti-dumping directive establishes the procedures for investigating dumping practices and for imposing anti-dumping measures, which may include anti-dumping duties, price commitments and import control measures. Anti-dumping investigations are initiated at the request of European producers affected by dumping practices, and are carried out by the European Commission, which assesses whether dumping exists, whether there is significant injury to European producers, and whether the imposition of anti-dumping measures is in the interest of the EU.

Anti-dumping duties are the most widely used instruments to combat dumping, and consist of the imposition of an additional duty on imported products sold at dumping prices. The amount of the anti-dumping duty is calculated as the difference between the dumping price and the normal value of the product, so that the anti-dumping duty eliminates the competitive advantage of dumping and restores fair competition in the European market. Anti-dumping duties may be definitive or provisional, and are usually imposed for a specified period, generally five years, which may be extended if dumping practices continue. The EU has imposed numerous anti-dumping duties on products imported from third countries, including steel products, chemical products, textile products, solar panels, wind turbines and electronic products, to protect European producers against unfair competition.
