# Common Diseases

## Chapter 1: Respiratory Diseases

### Asthma
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways that affects approximately 300 million people worldwide, making it one of the most common chronic diseases. This condition is characterized by inflammation and constriction of the bronchi, which impairs airflow to the lungs and causes symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing, especially at night or early in the morning. The severity of asthma varies enormously among patients, from mild cases that only require occasional treatment to severe cases that can be potentially life-threatening if not properly managed. Asthma triggers include allergens such as dust, mites, pollen, and fungi, respiratory infections, physical exercise, temperature changes, air pollution, tobacco smoke, and emotional factors such as stress.

Asthma diagnosis is generally based on the patient's clinical history, reported symptoms, and pulmonary function tests. Spirometry is the most common test for evaluating pulmonary function, measuring the amount of air the patient can exhale and the speed at which they can do so. Asthma treatment is divided into two main categories: long-term control therapy, which includes inhaled corticosteroids and leukotriene antagonists to reduce chronic inflammation, and quick-relief therapy, which includes rapid-acting bronchodilators such as salbutamol to treat acute symptoms. Effective asthma management requires regular monitoring of symptoms, adherence to treatment, and avoidance of known triggers. Patient education is fundamental to treatment success, as the majority of asthma management takes place at home.

### Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)
COPD is a chronic progressive respiratory disease characterized by permanent airflow limitation in the airways, generally caused by long-term exposure to harmful particles, with tobacco smoke being the most common cause. This disease affects approximately 380 million people worldwide and is the third leading cause of death globally. COPD encompasses two main conditions: chronic bronchitis, defined as persistent productive cough for at least three months per year for two consecutive years, and emphysema, which is the destruction of lung tissue leading to reduced gas exchange surface area. Symptoms of COPD include progressive difficulty breathing, chronic cough with sputum production, fatigue, weight loss, and cyanosis in advanced cases.

The diagnosis of COPD is confirmed through spirometry, which shows a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 70%, indicating airflow limitation that is not fully reversible. COPD treatment aims to reduce symptoms, improve exercise tolerance, prevent and treat exacerbations, and reduce mortality. Non-pharmacological interventions include smoking cessation, which is the most important measure, vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus, pulmonary rehabilitation, and supplemental oxygen therapy in cases of severe hypoxemia. Medications include long-acting bronchodilators (muscarinic antagonists and beta-2 agonists), inhaled corticosteroids, and combinations of these. In extreme cases, lung transplantation may be an option for selected patients.

### Pneumonia
Pneumonia is an infection that inflames the air sacs in the lungs and may cause them to fill with pus or fluid, making breathing difficult and reducing the amount of oxygen the lungs can transfer to the blood. It is one of the most common infectious diseases and one of the leading causes of death worldwide, especially in children under five years of age and older adults. Pneumonia can be caused by a wide range of organisms, including bacteria (such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Staphylococcus aureus), viruses (such as influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus, and SARS-CoV-2), fungi (such as Pneumocystis jirovecii), and parasites. Risk factors for pneumonia include advanced age, early childhood, smoking, alcoholism, chronic diseases such as diabetes and heart disease, and immunosuppression.

Pneumonia symptoms vary depending on the cause and severity, but typically include high fever, chills, cough with purulent sputum, difficulty breathing, pleuritic chest pain that worsens with deep breathing or coughing, and fatigue. Diagnosis is based on clinical symptoms, physical examination (which may reveal crackles on auscultation), and imaging studies, such as chest X-ray, which may show pulmonary infiltrates. Treatment depends on the cause: bacterial pneumonia is treated with antibiotics, viral pneumonia is generally managed with supportive care, and fungal pneumonia may require antifungals. Pneumococcal and influenza vaccination is an important preventive measure, especially for high-risk groups.

### Influenza
Influenza is an acute respiratory illness caused by the influenza virus, which spreads easily through respiratory droplets and direct contact. It is one of the most common infectious diseases in the world, with seasonal epidemics affecting millions of people each year. Influenza is characterized by the sudden onset of high fever, severe muscle pain, headache, fatigue, dry cough, sore throat, and nasal congestion. Although most people recover within one or two weeks, influenza can cause serious complications, especially in vulnerable groups, including pneumonia, encephalitis, heart failure, and death. It is estimated that influenza causes between 290,000 and 650,000 deaths worldwide each year.

The influenza virus is classified into types A, B, C, and D, with types A and B being responsible for seasonal epidemics. Type A is subclassified into subtypes based on the surface proteins hemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N), with subtypes H1N1 and H3N2 being the most common in humans. Diagnosis of influenza can be confirmed through rapid antigen detection tests or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests. Antiviral treatment, such as oseltamivir (Tamiflu) and zanamivir (Relenza), can reduce the duration and severity of the illness if administered within the first 48 hours of symptom onset. Prevention is based on annual vaccination, which is the most effective way to protect against influenza, along with hygiene measures such as frequent handwashing, covering the mouth when coughing, and avoiding close contact with sick people.

## Chapter 2: Infectious Diseases

### COVID-19
COVID-19 is a respiratory illness caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), which was first identified in Wuhan, China, in December 2019 and became a global pandemic in 2020. The disease is transmitted primarily through respiratory droplets and aerosols, and symptoms range from asymptomatic to critical illness. The most common symptoms include fever, dry cough, fatigue, loss of smell and taste, sore throat, headache, and difficulty breathing. In severe cases, it can cause severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multi-organ failure, and death. The highest-risk groups include older adults and people with underlying medical conditions such as heart disease, diabetes, obesity, and respiratory diseases.

COVID-19 diagnosis is performed through molecular tests (PCR) that detect the virus's genetic material, or antigen tests that detect viral proteins. COVID-19 treatment is primarily supportive, including oxygen therapy, intravenous fluids, and medications to reduce fever and pain. In severe cases, corticosteroids such as dexamethasone, anticoagulants to prevent thrombosis, and biological therapies may be used. COVID-19 vaccines, developed in record time, have been shown to be effective in preventing severe disease, hospitalization, and death. Prevention includes vaccination, wearing masks in enclosed indoor spaces, frequent handwashing, and social distancing.

### Tuberculosis
Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis that primarily affects the lungs but can affect other parts of the body, such as the kidneys, spine, and brain. It is one of the oldest and deadliest infectious diseases in the world, killing approximately 1.5 million people each year, mostly in low- and middle-income countries. TB is transmitted through respiratory droplets released when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or speaks. Not all infected people develop active disease; many have latent TB, where the bacteria remain inactive in the body without causing symptoms or being contagious. Risk factors for developing active TB include HIV infection, malnutrition, diabetes, smoking, and intravenous drug use.

Symptoms of active pulmonary TB include persistent cough for more than two weeks, sometimes with blood in the sputum, chest pain, unintentional weight loss, fever, night sweats, and fatigue. Diagnosis is made by identifying the bacterium in sputum through microscopy, culture, or molecular tests such as GeneXpert. TB treatment requires a six-month regimen of antibiotics, typically with an initial two-month phase of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol, followed by a four-month maintenance phase with isoniazid and rifampicin. The BCG vaccine (Bacillus Calmette-Guerin) provides partial protection against severe forms of TB in children but is not effective against pulmonary TB in adults. Prevention is based on early diagnosis, appropriate treatment of infectious patients, and prophylaxis in high-risk individuals.

### Malaria
Malaria is an infectious disease caused by parasites of the genus Plasmodium and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes. It is one of the most important tropical diseases in the world, causing approximately 400,000 deaths per year, most in children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa. The five Plasmodium species that infect humans are P. falciparum, the most dangerous and deadly; P. vivax, the most geographically common; P. ovale; P. malariae; and P. knowlesi, a zoonotic species. Malaria symptoms include high fever, chills, headache, nausea, vomiting, muscle pain, and fatigue. P. falciparum malaria can progress rapidly to severe complications, such as severe anemia, metabolic acidosis, renal failure, cerebral malaria, and death.

Malaria diagnosis is confirmed by identifying parasites in blood smears, using microscopy or rapid diagnostic tests that detect parasite antigens. Treatment depends on the Plasmodium species and geographic area, as drug resistance is a significant problem. Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) is the recommended treatment for P. falciparum malaria. Prevention is based on multiple strategies: use of insecticide-treated long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), indoor residual spraying (IRS) with insecticides, chemoprophylaxis for travelers to endemic areas, and mosquito control through elimination of breeding sites. Malaria vaccination is a recent development, with the RTS,S/AS01 vaccine (Mosquirix) recommended by the WHO for children in areas of high transmission.

### Sexually Transmitted Diseases
Sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) are infections transmitted through sexual contact, including vaginal, anal, and oral contact. The most common STDs include chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV), HIV/AIDS, and trichomoniasis. It is estimated that more than one million STDs are acquired worldwide every day. Many STDs are asymptomatic or cause mild symptoms, which facilitates their inadvertent transmission. Complications of untreated STDs can include pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, cancer (especially cervical cancer associated with HPV), pregnancy complications, and transmission to sexual partners and newborns.

STD diagnosis is performed through specific tests for each infection, which may include blood tests, urine tests, swabs of affected areas. Treatment varies by STD: bacterial infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis are treated with antibiotics; genital herpes is managed with antivirals that reduce the frequency and severity of outbreaks; HIV is treated with combination antiretroviral therapy that suppresses viral load; and HPV may resolve spontaneously, but genital warts can be treated with creams or procedures. Prevention includes correct and consistent use of condoms, HPV vaccination, regular screening tests, reducing the number of sexual partners, and abstinence.

## Chapter 3: Chronic Diseases

### Heart Disease
Heart disease is the number one cause of death in the world, responsible for approximately 17.9 million deaths per year. The most common form is coronary artery disease, caused by the buildup of fatty plaques (atherosclerosis) in the arteries that supply blood to the heart, which can lead to angina, myocardial infarction, and sudden death. Other types of heart disease include heart failure, arrhythmias, valvular disease, and congenital diseases. Risk factors for heart disease include hypertension, high cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, family history, and advanced age. Hypertension, which affects approximately 1,280 million adults worldwide, is one of the most important and modifiable risk factors.

Heart disease diagnosis includes electrocardiography (ECG), echocardiography, stress tests, coronary angiography, and coronary artery computed tomography. Treatment varies depending on the disease and may include medications such as antihypertensives, statins, antiplatelet agents, and anticoagulants, procedures such as coronary angioplasty with stent placement, and surgery such as coronary bypass grafting. Prevention is based on promoting healthy lifestyles: balanced diet low in saturated fats and sodium, regular physical activity (at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week), maintaining a healthy weight, smoking cessation, moderate alcohol consumption, and stress management. Cardiovascular screening programs can identify risk factors early and guide preventive interventions.

### Type 2 Diabetes
Type 2 diabetes is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by insulin resistance and/or relative insulin deficiency, which results in elevated blood glucose levels (hyperglycemia). It affects approximately 537 million adults worldwide, and this number is projected to reach 783 million by 2045. Type 2 diabetes accounts for 90-95% of all diabetes cases and is strongly associated with obesity, physical inactivity, poor diet, and family history. Symptoms include excessive thirst, frequent urination, unintentional weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, and slow wound healing. However, many people with type 2 diabetes are asymptomatic for years, which delays diagnosis and increases the risk of complications.

Complications of uncontrolled type 2 diabetes include cardiovascular diseases, diabetic neuropathy (nerve damage), diabetic nephropathy (kidney damage), diabetic retinopathy (retinal damage that can lead to blindness), diabetic foot (ulcers and gangrene that may require amputation), and increased risk of infections. Diagnosis is based on elevated fasting glucose levels (126 mg/dL or higher), blood glucose after 2 hours during the oral glucose tolerance test (200 mg/dL or higher), or hemoglobin A1c (6.5% or higher). Treatment includes lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, weight loss), oral medications such as metformin, and insulin in some cases. Prevention is based on promoting healthy lifestyles, especially in people with prediabetes.

### Hypertension
Hypertension, or high blood pressure, is a chronic condition in which blood exerts excessive force against the walls of the arteries, which over time can cause damage to the heart, blood vessels, kidneys, and other organs. It affects approximately 1,280 million adults worldwide and is the greatest modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Hypertension is classified into two categories: primary (essential), which accounts for 90-95% of cases and has no identifiable cause, and secondary, which is caused by another medical condition such as kidney disease, hormonal problems, or medication side effects. Normal blood pressure is below 120/80 mmHg; hypertension is diagnosed when systolic pressure is 130 mmHg or higher or diastolic pressure is 80 mmHg or higher consistently.

Hypertension is generally asymptomatic, which is why it is known as "the silent killer," and many patients do not know they have it until they develop complications. Diagnosis is made by repeated blood pressure measurements on different occasions. Treatment includes lifestyle changes (sodium reduction, DASH diet, physical exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation, alcohol moderation) and antihypertensive medications, which may include diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium channel blockers, and beta-blockers. Regular blood pressure monitoring is essential for evaluating treatment effectiveness and preventing complications.

### Cancer
Cancer is a group of more than 100 diseases characterized by uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells that can invade and destroy normal tissues. It is the second leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for approximately 10 million deaths per year. The most common types of cancer include lung cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, prostate cancer, stomach cancer, liver cancer, and cervical cancer. Risk factors for cancer include smoking (responsible for 22% of cancer deaths), alcohol consumption, poor diet, obesity, physical inactivity, sun exposure, infections (such as HPV, hepatitis B and C, and H. pylori), family history, and advanced age. Although some risk factors are non-modifiable, such as genetics and age, many risk factors can be modified through lifestyle changes.

Early diagnosis of cancer is crucial for improving treatment outcomes and includes screening tests such as mammography for breast cancer, Pap smears for cervical cancer, colonoscopy for colorectal cancer, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) tests for prostate cancer, and low-dose computed tomography for lung cancer in high-risk individuals. Cancer treatment depends on the type, stage, and location, and includes surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and hormonal therapy. Advances in cancer research, including precision medicine and immunotherapy, are improving treatment outcomes for many types of cancer. Prevention is based on reducing modifiable risk factors, vaccination against preventable cancers (HPV, hepatitis B), and regular screening tests.

## Chapter 4: Disease Prevention

### Health Promotion
Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over and improve their health by addressing the social, economic, and environmental determinants of health. Health promotion strategies include health education, creating supportive environments, developing personal skills, reorienting health services, and community action. Health education provides information and skills for making informed health decisions, including nutrition, physical activity, disease prevention, and appropriate use of health services. Creating supportive environments includes policies that promote clean air, safe drinking water, safe and nutritious food, and safe spaces for physical activity.

Health promotion campaigns can be effective in changing behaviors and improving health, especially when combined with environmental and policy interventions. Anti-tobacco campaigns, which include negative advertising, package warnings, and advertising restrictions, have significantly reduced tobacco consumption in many countries. Vaccination campaigns have eradicated smallpox and significantly reduced the incidence of many infectious diseases. Healthy eating and physical activity campaigns can promote healthy lifestyles and reduce the risk of chronic diseases. Research on the effectiveness of health promotion campaigns continues to provide evidence for improving interventions.

### Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Prevention
Disease prevention is classified into three levels: primary prevention, which seeks to prevent the onset of diseases before they occur; secondary prevention, which seeks early diagnosis and treatment of diseases in their early stages; and tertiary prevention, which seeks to reduce the impact of established diseases and prevent complications. Primary prevention includes interventions such as vaccination, promoting healthy lifestyles, environmental regulation, and protection against occupational risks. Secondary prevention includes screening tests such as mammography, colonoscopy, Pap smears, and blood tests to detect diseases in early stages when they are most treatable. Tertiary prevention includes rehabilitation, chronic disease management, and psychosocial support for people with established diseases.

The effectiveness of prevention interventions varies by disease and population. Vaccination is one of the most effective primary prevention interventions, having eradicated smallpox and significantly reduced the incidence of many infectious diseases. Screening tests can reduce cancer mortality by detecting the disease in early stages. Management of risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol can prevent cardiovascular disease. Promotion of healthy lifestyles, including balanced diet, physical activity, smoking cessation, and moderate alcohol consumption, can reduce the risk of many chronic diseases. Research on disease prevention continues to provide new evidence to guide effective interventions.

## Chapter 5: Vaccines

### History and Development
Vaccines are one of the most important achievements in modern medicine, having saved millions of lives by preventing serious infectious diseases. The history of vaccines begins with Edward Jenner, who in 1796 demonstrated that inoculation with cowpox pus could protect against smallpox, laying the foundation for modern vaccinology. Louis Pasteur developed the first vaccines against rabies and anthrax in the 19th century, and in the 20th century vaccines were developed against many important diseases, including diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, measles, polio, hepatitis B, and many others. Recent advances include messenger RNA vaccines, such as the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines, which represent a new paradigm in vaccine development.

The vaccine development process includes multiple stages: basic research to identify candidate antigens, preclinical studies in laboratory and animals, clinical trials in three phases to evaluate safety and efficacy in humans, regulatory review and approval, and post-marketing surveillance to detect rare adverse effects. Traditional vaccine development typically takes 10-15 years, but COVID-19 vaccines were developed in less than one year, an unprecedented achievement made possible by previous investment in research, international collaboration, and accelerated regulatory processes. Types of vaccines include live attenuated virus vaccines, inactivated virus vaccines, subunit vaccines, viral vector vaccines, messenger RNA vaccines, and DNA vaccines.

### Vaccination Programs
Vaccination programs are systematic strategies for administering vaccines to target populations to prevent infectious diseases. Childhood vaccination programs, which typically begin at birth and continue throughout childhood, are fundamental to public health and have dramatically reduced the incidence of many vaccine-preventable diseases. The vaccination schedule varies by country but generally includes vaccines against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, measles, rubella, mumps, hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b, pneumococcus, rotavirus, and varicella. School vaccination programs ensure that children are vaccinated before entering school, protecting them and reducing transmission in the community.

Vaccination programs also include vaccines for adults, such as the annual influenza vaccine, the herpes zoster (shingles) vaccine for older adults, the HPV vaccine for adolescents and young adults, and the pneumococcal vaccine for older adults and people with high-risk medical conditions. Emergency vaccination campaigns are used during disease outbreaks to control transmission quickly. Vaccination coverage, which is the percentage of the target population that has received all recommended doses, is an important indicator of program effectiveness. The WHO estimates that vaccination prevents between 2 and 3 million deaths per year, but global vaccination coverage is still insufficient to achieve the elimination of many vaccine-preventable diseases.

### Debate and Controversies
Vaccination has generated debate and controversy throughout its history, with concerns about vaccine safety, individual liberties, and vaccine mandates. The most notable controversy was the study published by Andrew Wakefield in 1998 that falsely linked the MMR vaccine (measles, mumps, rubella) to autism, a study that was retracted for fraud and that caused a significant reduction in vaccination coverage in several countries. Although the scientific evidence is overwhelming regarding the safety and efficacy of vaccines, anti-vaccination movements continue to promote misinformation about vaccine risks, which poses a threat to public health.

The debate over vaccine mandates involves ethical, legal, and public health considerations. Arguments in favor of mandatory vaccination include protection of public health, especially for people who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons (such as immunocompromised individuals), and prevention of disease outbreaks. Arguments against include individual freedom, informed consent, and distrust of government authorities and the pharmaceutical industry. Many countries have adopted intermediate approaches, such as mandatory vaccination for school or work access, with limited exemptions for medical or philosophical reasons. Effective communication of vaccine benefits and risks is essential for maintaining high vaccination coverage and preventing outbreaks of preventable diseases.

## Chapter 6: Hygiene

### Personal Hygiene
Personal hygiene refers to cleaning and body care practices that help prevent diseases and maintain health. Personal hygiene includes frequent handwashing with soap and water, daily oral hygiene, regular showering, hair washing, nail trimming, and cleaning ears and nose. Handwashing is the most important personal hygiene measure for preventing disease transmission, as hands are the primary vehicle for pathogen transmission. It is recommended to wash hands before and after preparing food, before eating, after using the bathroom, after blowing the nose, coughing, or sneezing, and after touching animals, garbage, or contaminated surfaces.

Oral hygiene is important for preventing dental and gum diseases, which can affect overall health. It is recommended to brush teeth at least twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, floss daily, and visit the dentist regularly. Proper personal hygiene also includes wearing clean clothes, regularly changing underwear, and foot care, including careful washing and drying between the toes to prevent fungal infections. Personal hygiene is especially important in vulnerable populations, such as children, older adults, and people with weakened immune systems.

### Food Hygiene
Food hygiene refers to practices that ensure food is safe for consumption, from production to preparation and consumption. Food hygiene includes careful washing of fruits and vegetables, proper cooking of meats and fish, separating raw and cooked foods to prevent cross-contamination, proper storage of perishable foods, and frequent handwashing, utensil cleaning, and kitchen surface cleaning. Proper cooking temperature is critical for killing pathogenic bacteria: meats should be cooked to internal temperatures of at least 74 degrees Celsius, and perishable foods should be kept out of the "danger zone" (between 4 and 60 degrees Celsius) for the shortest time possible.

Food hygiene in the food industry includes compliance with hygiene standards such as the Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) system, which identifies and controls food hazards at critical points in the production process. Food hygiene regulations establish requirements for cleaning and disinfecting facilities and equipment, staff training, pest control, and food traceability. Health inspections of restaurants, food manufacturers, and street vendors are essential for ensuring compliance with hygiene standards. Consumer education about food hygiene is important for preventing foodborne illnesses in the home.

### Environmental Hygiene
Environmental hygiene refers to conditions and practices that keep the environment clean and safe for human health. Environmental hygiene includes proper solid waste management, wastewater treatment, vector control (such as mosquitoes, rats, and flies), indoor and outdoor air quality, and hygiene of public and private spaces. Poor environmental hygiene can cause infectious diseases, poisoning, allergies, and other health problems. Proper solid waste management includes the collection, separation, transportation, and final disposal of waste in a way that minimizes the impact on human health and the environment.

Water hygiene is fundamental to public health, as consumption of contaminated water can cause diseases such as cholera, typhoid fever, hepatitis A, and dysentery. Treatment and distribution of safe drinking water is one of the most important public health interventions. Air hygiene, both indoor and outdoor, is important for preventing respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. Outdoor air pollution from fine particles, ozone, and other pollutants causes millions of premature deaths each year. Hygiene of public spaces, including cleaning streets, parks, and public buildings, contributes to community health and well-being. Environmental hygiene programs are essential for preventing disease and promoting public health.

## Chapter 7: Risk Factors

### Modifiable Risk Factors
Modifiable risk factors are conditions or behaviors that increase the probability of developing diseases and can be changed through individual or public health interventions. The main modifiable risk factors include smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, poor diet, physical inactivity, obesity, hypertension, high cholesterol, and poorly controlled diabetes. Smoking is the most important modifiable risk factor, responsible for 22% of worldwide cancer deaths and 70% of lung cancer deaths. Poor diet, characterized by high intake of saturated fats, sodium, added sugars, and ultra-processed foods, and low intake of fruits, vegetables, and fiber, significantly contributes to heart disease, diabetes, and cancer.

Modifying risk factors can significantly reduce the incidence and mortality of chronic diseases. Smoking cessation rapidly reduces the risk of cardiovascular disease and cancer. Reducing alcohol consumption can reduce the risk of liver disease, cancer, and injuries. Improving diet, including increasing consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and fish, and reducing consumption of saturated fats, sodium, and added sugars, can reduce the risk of heart disease, diabetes, and certain types of cancer. Increasing physical activity, with at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity per week, can reduce the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, certain types of cancer, and depression. Weight loss in people who are overweight or obese can significantly improve health indicators.

### Non-Modifiable Risk Factors
Non-modifiable risk factors are conditions that increase the probability of diseases but cannot be changed, including age, sex, family history, and genetics. Advanced age is the most important non-modifiable risk factor for most chronic diseases, as aging is associated with deterioration of the function of multiple organs and systems, including the immune, cardiovascular, nervous, and musculoskeletal systems. Sex also influences disease risk; for example, men have a higher risk of heart disease before menopause, while women have a higher risk of breast cancer and thyroid cancer. Family history of diseases, such as heart disease, diabetes, and cancer, may indicate a genetic predisposition to these diseases.

Although non-modifiable risk factors cannot be changed, their presence can inform prevention and surveillance strategies. People with a family history of heart disease may benefit from more frequent monitoring and early interventions for modifiable risk factors. Women with a family history of breast cancer may benefit from more frequent screening tests, such as mammography from an early age. People with a genetic predisposition to diseases may benefit from genetic counseling, which can provide information about risks and prevention options. Understanding risk factors, both modifiable and non-modifiable, is essential for personalized disease prevention and health promotion.

## Chapter 8: Early Detection

### Importance of Early Diagnosis
Early diagnosis of diseases is crucial for improving treatment outcomes and reducing morbidity and mortality. Many diseases, including cancer, heart disease, diabetes, and kidney disease, are more treatable when detected in early stages. Cancer detected in early stages generally has better prognosis than cancer detected in advanced stages; for example, breast cancer detected in stage I has a five-year survival rate of 99%, while breast cancer detected in stage IV has a survival rate of 28%. Early detection of diabetes can prevent or delay complications such as blindness, amputations, and kidney failure. Early detection of hypertension can prevent strokes, heart attacks, and heart failure.

Early detection strategies include population screening tests, symptom-based diagnosis, and risk factor monitoring. Population screening tests, such as mammography for breast cancer, colonoscopy for colorectal cancer, and Pap smears for cervical cancer, are administered to asymptomatic populations to detect diseases in early stages. Symptom-based diagnosis occurs when people seek medical attention due to concerning symptoms, and the physician performs evaluations to determine the cause. Risk factor monitoring, such as blood pressure, glucose, and cholesterol levels, can detect conditions before they cause symptoms. The combination of these strategies can significantly improve early disease detection.

### Screening Tests
Screening tests are examinations performed on asymptomatic populations to detect diseases in early stages when they are most treatable. The most common screening tests include mammography for breast cancer, recommended every one to two years for women aged 50 to 74; colonoscopy or fecal occult blood tests for colorectal cancer, recommended starting at age 45 to 50; Pap smears and HPV tests for cervical cancer, recommended every three to five years for women aged 21 to 65; and blood pressure tests for hypertension, recommended at least once a year for adults. Other screening tests include blood tests for diabetes and cholesterol, spirometry for COPD in high-risk individuals, and hearing and vision tests.

The effectiveness of screening tests depends on multiple factors, including the accuracy of the test, the availability of effective treatments for the detected disease, and the risks and benefits of screening. Screening tests have potential risks, including false positives that cause anxiety and unnecessary procedures, false negatives that give a false sense of security, and overdiagnosis that may lead to unnecessary treatments. Screening guidelines, such as those recommended by the US Preventive Services Task Force, evaluate evidence to determine which tests are effective and for which populations. The decision to undergo screening tests should be based on a discussion with a healthcare professional about individual benefits and risks.

## Chapter 9: Treatment

### Principles of Medical Treatment
Medical treatment aims to cure diseases, relieve symptoms, improve quality of life, and prevent complications. The basic principles of treatment include correct diagnosis, selection of the most effective treatment, monitoring the response to treatment, and adjustment as needed. Treatment can be pharmacological (medications), surgical (surgery), radiotherapeutic (radiation therapy), immunological (immunotherapy), or supportive (palliative care). The selection of treatment depends on the disease, its stage, the patient's overall health, the patient's preferences, and the availability of treatment options. Informed consent is a fundamental ethical principle that ensures patients receive complete information about the benefits, risks, and alternatives of treatment before accepting it.

Advances in modern medicine have transformed the treatment of many diseases. Precision medicine uses genetic and molecular information to personalize treatment, selecting the most effective therapy for each patient. Immunotherapy stimulates the patient's immune system to fight cancer and has shown remarkable results in several types of cancer. Targeted therapy uses drugs that attack specific molecular targets of cancer cells, reducing damage to normal cells. Telemedicine enables the delivery of healthcare services remotely, improving access to medical care in remote areas. Ongoing research is developing new treatments and improving existing ones for many diseases.

### Pharmacotherapy
Pharmacotherapy is the use of medications to treat, cure, or prevent diseases. Medications are classified into different categories based on their mechanism of action, therapeutic use, and safety profile. Generic medications, which contain the same active ingredients as brand-name medications but are manufactured by different companies, are generally more affordable and are as effective and safe as brand-name medications. Medication adherence, which is the degree to which patients take medications as prescribed, is a significant problem; it is estimated that 50% of patients with chronic diseases do not take their medications correctly, which can reduce treatment effectiveness and increase the risk of complications.

Drug safety is a priority, and adverse drug effects are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. Adverse effects can range from mild (nausea, headache) to serious (allergic reactions, organ damage). Drug interactions are another important problem, as some medications may interact with each other or with food, reducing their effectiveness or increasing the risk of adverse effects. Pharmacovigilance, which is the detection, evaluation, and prevention of adverse drug effects, is an essential function of regulatory agencies. Patient education on proper medication use, including dosage, schedule, side effects, and interactions, is essential for maximizing effectiveness and minimizing risks.

## Chapter 10: Public Health

### Public Health Concepts
Public health is the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life, and promoting health through organized efforts of society. Unlike clinical medicine, which focuses on treating individual diseases, public health focuses on the health of entire populations and on the social, economic, and environmental determinants of health. The pillars of public health include epidemiological surveillance, disease prevention, health promotion, public health protection, maternal and child health, and health service planning. Public health has achieved significant successes, including the eradication of smallpox, reduction of infectious diseases through vaccination, and improvement of hygiene and sanitation.

Social determinants of health are the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age that affect their health. These determinants include income and socioeconomic status, education, employment and working conditions, access to healthcare, neighborhood and physical environment, culture, and discrimination. Health inequalities, which are systematic differences in health between social groups, are largely driven by inequalities in social determinants. Promoting health equity is a fundamental goal of public health and requires addressing the underlying social determinants of health inequalities.

### Epidemiology
Epidemiology is the branch of public health that studies the distribution, determinants, and frequency of diseases and other health problems in populations. Epidemiologists use research methods to identify disease patterns, evaluate risk factors, measure the effectiveness of interventions, and guide public health policies. Epidemiological surveillance is the continuous monitoring of the distribution and patterns of diseases, enabling early detection of outbreaks and evaluation of trends. Epidemiological studies include observational studies (cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies) and experimental studies (randomized clinical trials). Epidemiology has been fundamental to understanding the transmission of infectious diseases, identifying risk factors for chronic diseases, and evaluating the effectiveness of public health interventions.

Current challenges in epidemiology include surveillance of emerging and re-emerging diseases, monitoring of chronic diseases and their risk factors, evaluation of the effectiveness of public health interventions, and prediction of disease outbreaks. Molecular epidemiology, which combines epidemiological methods with molecular techniques, enables identification of the cause of disease outbreaks and understanding of the biological mechanisms of diseases. Genetic epidemiology studies the influence of genetic factors on the distribution of diseases in populations. Environmental epidemiology studies the relationship between environmental factors and health. Epidemiological research continues to provide essential evidence to guide public health policies and practices.
## Chapter 11: Diabetes Mellitus

### Type 1 Diabetes

Type 1 diabetes is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the body's immune system attacks and destroys the beta cells of the pancreas, which are responsible for producing insulin. The total or partial absence of insulin causes elevated blood glucose levels that, if not properly treated, can cause acute complications such as diabetic ketoacidosis, a potentially life-threatening condition characterized by hyperglycemia, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis. Type 1 diabetes accounts for 5% to 10% of all diabetes cases and is usually diagnosed in children and young adults, although it can appear at any age. The exact cause is not fully understood, but it is believed to involve an interaction between genetic factors (associated with the HLA complex on chromosome 6) and environmental factors such as viral infections, early exposure to cow's milk proteins, and vitamin D deficiency. The global prevalence of type 1 diabetes is increasing at a rate of 3-4% per year, especially in children under 5 years of age.

Treatment of type 1 diabetes requires lifelong exogenous insulin administration, as the body cannot produce it. Insulin regimens include multiple daily injection (MDI) therapy, which combines rapid-acting insulin with long-acting insulin, and insulin pump therapy, which delivers continuous subcutaneous insulin. Available insulin types include ultra-rapid-acting insulins (lispro, aspart, glulisine), rapid-acting (regular), intermediate-acting (NPH), and long-acting (glargine, detemir, degludec). Glucose monitoring is performed with capillary glucometers, continuous glucose sensors (CGM) that measure levels every 1-5 minutes, and closed-loop insulin delivery systems (artificial pancreas) that automatically adjust insulin doses based on glucose levels. Therapeutic goals include maintaining hemoglobin A1c below 7% (in adults) and minimizing glycemic variability, reducing the risk of hypoglycemia.

Complications of type 1 diabetes are similar to those of type 2 and include diabetic retinopathy (leading cause of blindness in working-age adults), diabetic nephropathy (leading cause of end-stage renal disease), peripheral neuropathy (pain, numbness in extremities), autonomic neuropathy (orthostatic hypotension, gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction), coronary artery disease, peripheral arterial disease, and diabetic foot. Pancreatic islet transplantation is an option for patients with severe recurrent hypoglycemia, and simultaneous pancreas-kidney transplantation can cure type 1 diabetes in patients with end-stage renal disease. Recent advances include ultra-rapid insulins (faster insulin aspart), second-generation glucose sensors with improved accuracy, and increasingly sophisticated artificial pancreas systems.

### Diabetes Complications

Diabetes complications are classified as acute and chronic. Acute complications include diabetic ketoacidosis (more common in type 1), hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome (more common in type 2), severe hypoglycemia, and lactic acidosis. Chronic complications result from prolonged exposure to hyperglycemia and affect multiple organs. Microangiopathy (damage to small blood vessels) causes retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy. Macroangiopathy (damage to large vessels) causes coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral arterial disease. Non-enzymatic glycosylation of proteins, mediated by the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), contributes significantly to tissue damage.

Diabetic retinopathy is classified as non-proliferative (mild, moderate, and severe) and proliferative. The non-proliferative form is characterized by microaneurysms, retinal hemorrhages, hard exudates, and macular edema. The proliferative form is characterized by neovascularization, which can cause vitreous hemorrhage and retinal detachment. Diabetic macular edema can occur at any stage and is the most common cause of vision loss in diabetics. Treatment includes strict control of glucose, blood pressure, and lipids, as well as laser photocoagulation, intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF (ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab), and corticosteroids.

Diabetic nephropathy initially manifests with microalbuminuria (albumin excretion of 30-300 mg/day) and progresses to proteinuria and reduced glomerular filtration. Diabetes is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease in developed countries. Treatment includes angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), which reduce proteinuria and delay the progression of kidney disease, as well as SGLT2 cotransporter inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin), which have demonstrated additional renal benefits.

Diabetic neuropathy is the most common chronic complication, affecting up to 50% of patients with diabetes. Peripheral neuropathy presents as pain, burning, numbness, and tingling in the extremities, following a glove-and-stocking pattern. Autonomic neuropathy can affect the cardiovascular system (orthostatic hypotension, tachycardia), gastrointestinal system (gastroparesis, nocturnal diarrhea), genitourinary system (urinary retention, erectile dysfunction), and sudomotor system (excessive or decreased sweating). Diabetic foot, caused by the combination of peripheral neuropathy, angiopathy, and increased susceptibility to infections, is the leading cause of non-traumatic amputations. Prevention includes regular podiatric examinations, appropriate footwear, glucose control, and daily foot care.

### Diabetes Management and Treatment

Comprehensive diabetes management requires a multidisciplinary approach that includes physicians, nurses, endocrinologists, ophthalmologists, nephrologists, podiatrists, nutritionists, psychologists, and diabetes educators. Self-monitoring of glucose is fundamental for adjusting therapy and preventing complications. Individualized therapeutic goals depend on age, duration of disease, presence of complications, risk of hypoglycemia, and patient preferences. For most adults with type 2 diabetes, the goal is HbA1c below 7%, blood pressure below 130/80 mmHg, LDL cholesterol below 100 mg/dL (or 70 mg/dL in high cardiovascular risk), BMI below 25 kg/m2, and smoking cessation.

Pharmacotherapy for type 2 diabetes includes multiple classes of medications with different mechanisms of action. Metformin remains the first-line treatment, reducing hepatic glucose production and improving insulin sensitivity. Sulfonylureas (glimepiride, gliclazide) stimulate pancreatic insulin secretion. SGLT2 cotransporter inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, canagliflozin) promote renal glucose excretion and have demonstrated cardiovascular and renal benefits. GLP-1 receptor agonists (liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide) increase glucose-dependent insulin secretion, reduce appetite, and have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits. DPP-4 inhibitors (sitagliptin, linagliptin) increase incretin levels. Thiazolidinediones (pioglitazone) improve insulin sensitivity. Insulin is used when oral medications do not achieve adequate glycemic control.

### Prediabetes and Diabetes Prevention

Prediabetes is defined as fasting glucose levels of 100-125 mg/dL (impaired fasting glucose) or HbA1c of 5.7-6.4%, and affects approximately 541 million adults worldwide. People with prediabetes have a significantly higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and other complications. The Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) in the United States demonstrated that intensive lifestyle intervention (7% weight loss, 150 minutes per week of moderate physical activity) reduced the risk of diabetes by 58% compared to placebo, an effect that was maintained during 15 years of follow-up. Metformin also reduced the risk by 31%, being especially effective in people with BMI over 35 and women with a history of gestational diabetes.

Prevention strategies include promoting a Mediterranean diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and extra virgin olive oil, with moderate consumption of fish and dairy products and low consumption of red and processed meats, added sugars, and ultra-processed foods. Regular physical activity, including aerobic and resistance exercises, improves insulin sensitivity and body composition. Stress management, sleep quality, and reducing exposure to environmental pollutants may also influence diabetes risk. Community-based prevention programs, which include health education, access to sports facilities, and healthy food policies, are essential for addressing the diabetes epidemic at the population level.

## Chapter 12: Cancer

### Lung Cancer

Lung cancer is the cancer with the highest incidence and mortality worldwide, responsible for approximately 1.8 million deaths per year. It is classified into two main types: non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which accounts for 85% of cases, and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), which accounts for 15%. NSCLC is subclassified into adenocarcinoma (the most common type, especially in women and non-smokers), squamous cell carcinoma (associated with smoking, central), and large cell carcinoma. SCLC is extremely aggressive and is strongly associated with smoking, with a five-year survival rate below 7%. Smoking is responsible for 85% of lung cancers, and the risk increases with the number of cigarettes smoked per day and the duration of smoking. Other risk factors include radon exposure, asbestos, arsenic, chromium, nickel, air pollution, and family history.

Early diagnosis of lung cancer is difficult because symptoms usually appear in advanced stages. Symptoms include persistent cough, hemoptysis (blood in sputum), difficulty breathing, chest pain, unexplained weight loss, fatigue, and recurrent pneumonia. Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) is the recommended screening test for people aged 50 to 80 with a smoking history of 20 pack-years or more who have smoked within the last 15 years. Diagnosis is confirmed through bronchoscopy with biopsy, CT-guided transthoracic biopsy, or thoracoscopy. Staging uses the TNM system, which evaluates the primary tumor, regional lymph nodes, and distant metastases. Molecular profiling, including mutations in EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS, MET, RET, and HER2, is essential for guiding targeted therapy.

Lung cancer treatment depends on the type, stage, and molecular profile. Surgery (lobectomy, pneumonectomy, or wedge resection) is the treatment of choice in early stages. Adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy is used in locally advanced stages. Radiation therapy may be radical, adjuvant, or palliative. Immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, ipilimumab) has transformed lung cancer treatment, improving survival in advanced stages. Targeted therapy with EGFR inhibitors (erlotinib, osimertinib), ALK inhibitors (crizotinib, alectinib, ceritinib), and other targeted agents has improved outcomes in patients with specific mutations. Advances in stereotactic radiation therapy (SBRT) allow treatment of small early-stage lesions with millimetric precision.

### Breast Cancer

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide, with approximately 2.3 million new cases per year and 685,000 deaths. It is classified as infiltrating ductal carcinoma (the most common type, 70-80%), lobular carcinoma (10-15%), ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS), and special types such as inflammatory breast cancer and Paget's disease of the nipple. Risk factors include age (risk increases with age), family history (especially BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations), reproductive history (early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, first pregnancy after age 30), hormone replacement therapy, postmenopausal obesity, alcohol consumption, and chest radiation in youth. BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations confer a lifetime risk of 45-85% for breast cancer and 10-40% for ovarian cancer.

Breast cancer diagnosis is performed through mammography, breast ultrasound, breast MRI, and core needle biopsy or vacuum-assisted biopsy. Screening mammography is recommended every one to two years for women aged 50 to 74, and can begin at age 40 in high-risk women. The TNM staging system evaluates tumor size, nodal involvement, and metastases. Biological markers include estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), HER2, and the Ki-67 index, which are essential for guiding treatment. Molecular profiling of breast cancer includes luminal A (ER+/PR+, HER2-, low Ki-67), luminal B (ER+/PR+, HER2+ or high Ki-67), HER2-positive (ER-/PR-, HER2+), and triple-negative (ER-/PR-, HER2-).

Breast cancer treatment is multidisciplinary and includes surgery (conservative: lumpectomy or quadrantectomy; or radical: simple mastectomy, modified radical mastectomy, or skin/nipple-sparing mastectomy), postoperative radiation therapy, adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy (regimens such as AC-T, TC, TAC), hormonal therapy with tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane) for ER-positive cancers, anti-HER2 therapy with trastuzumab, pertuzumab, T-DM1 (ado-trastuzumab emtansine), and T-DXd (trastuzumab deruxtecan) for HER2-positive cancers, and immunotherapy with pembrolizumab for triple-negative cancers. Breast reconstructive surgery (with implants, autologous tissue flap, or combination) can be performed simultaneously or delayed after mastectomy.

### Colorectal Cancer

Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer worldwide, with approximately 1.9 million new cases per year and 930,000 deaths. It originates in the colon or rectum and generally develops from adenomatous polyps over a period of 10-15 years. Risk factors include age (over 50), family history of colorectal cancer or polyps, inflammatory bowel diseases (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease), genetic syndromes (familial adenomatous polyposis, Lynch syndrome), diet rich in red and processed meats, low fiber intake, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity. Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), caused by mutations in the APC gene, causes hundreds to thousands of colon polyps and a 100% cancer risk if untreated.

Diagnosis is performed through colonoscopy with biopsy, which is the gold standard, and screening tests such as fecal occult blood detection (immunological method), CT colonography, and fecal DNA tests. Screening guidelines recommend starting at age 45-50 and repeating every 10 years for colonoscopy, or every 1-3 years for fecal tests. TNM staging evaluates depth of invasion (T), nodal involvement (N), and metastases (M). Molecular analysis includes determination of microsatellite instability (MSI) or mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR), which is important for guiding immunotherapy, and mutations in KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF, which influence targeted therapy.

Colorectal cancer treatment includes surgery (partial or total colectomy, anterior rectal resection, abdominoperineal amputation), adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy (regimens with oxaliplatin such as FOLFOX or CAPOX), radiation therapy for rectal cancer, targeted therapy with anti-VEGF antibodies (bevacizumab, aflibercept, ramucirumab) and anti-EGFR (cetuximab, panitumumab) for RAS/BRAF wild-type cancers, and immunotherapy with pembrolizumab or nivolumab for cancers with high MSI or dMMR. Laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery allows minimally invasive procedures with faster recovery. Minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring through circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis is an emerging tool for guiding therapeutic decisions.

### Prostate Cancer

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men worldwide, with approximately 1.4 million new cases per year and 375,000 deaths. Most prostate cancers are adenocarcinomas that originate in the glands of the prostate. Risk factors include age (cancer is rare before age 50 and common after age 65), race (African American men have higher risk), family history (risk doubles if a first-degree relative is affected), diet rich in animal fats, and obesity. Prostate cancer is classified as low, intermediate, and high risk based on PSA level, Gleason score, and clinical stage. The Gleason scale evaluates histological tumor differentiation, with scores from 6 (low grade) to 10 (high grade).

Diagnosis is performed through determination of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) in blood, digital rectal examination, transrectal ultrasound, and ultrasound-guided prostate biopsy (typically 12 to 24 cores). Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) is increasingly used to locate suspicious lesions and guide fusion biopsy. The TNM staging system evaluates local extension (T), nodal involvement (N), and metastases (M). Prognostic factors include PSA level, Gleason score, percentage of positive cores, clinical stage, and presence of metastases.

Prostate cancer treatment is highly individualized and depends on tumor aggressiveness, patient life expectancy, and patient preferences. Active surveillance is an option for low-risk cancers (Gleason 6, PSA < 10, clinical stage T1c-T2a), involving close monitoring with PSA, digital rectal examination, and control biopsies, deferring curative treatment until evidence of progression. Radical prostatectomy (open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted) is an option for localized cancers with life expectancy greater than 10 years. External radiation therapy and brachytherapy are options for localized or locally advanced cancers. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) with GnRH agonists (leuprolide, goserelin) or GnRH antagonists (degarelix) is the foundation of systemic treatment. Androgen signaling inhibitors include androgen synthesis inhibitors (abiraterone), androgen receptor inhibitors (enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide), and agents that inhibit ARV7 activity. Radium-223, a radiopharmaceutical that deposits in areas of increased bone metabolism, is an option for metastatic prostate cancer with symptomatic bone metastases.

### Stomach and Liver Cancer

Stomach cancer is the fifth most common cancer worldwide, with approximately 970,000 new cases per year and 680,000 deaths. Most gastric cancers are adenocarcinomas, which are subdivided into intestinal type (associated with H. pylori, chronic atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and high-salt diet) and diffuse type (more aggressive, associated with CDH1 mutations). Risk factors include H. pylori infection (responsible for 60-70% of gastric cancers), smoking, diet rich in smoked and salty foods, low fruit and vegetable intake, family history, chronic atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and genetic syndromes (Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis). Incidence is higher in East Asia, Eastern Europe, and South America. Proximal and gastroesophageal junction gastric cancer has increased in incidence in Western countries.

Diagnosis is performed through endoscopy with biopsy, which is the gold standard, and may be assisted by high-definition endoscopy, chromoendoscopy (dye), and narrow-band light endoscopy. CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis is used for staging, and PET-CT may be useful for detecting distant metastases. TNM staging evaluates depth of invasion (T), nodal involvement (N), and metastases (M). Molecular analysis includes determination of HER2 status, PD-L1 (CPS), MSI/dMMR, and mutations in genes such as FGFR2, MET, RET, and Claudin 18.2.

Gastric cancer treatment includes surgery (partial or total gastrectomy with D2 lymph node dissection), neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy (regimens with platinum and fluoropyrimidine such as FLOT, SOX, or XELOX), radiation therapy, and targeted therapy. Trastuzumab is the first-line anti-HER2 targeted therapy, and trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) is an option for refractory HER2-positive cancers. Immunotherapy with nivolumab or pembrolizumab is used in cancers with PD-L1 CPS >= 5, and pembrolizumab is especially effective in cancers with high MSI/dMMR. Intraperitoneal chemotherapy with hyperthermia (HIPEC) is being evaluated for gastric peritoneal carcinomatosis.

Liver cancer is the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer death worldwide, with approximately 900,000 new cases per year and 760,000 deaths. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for 85-90% of primary liver cancers, and cholangiocarcinoma (bile duct cancer) accounts for 10-15%. The main risk factors include liver cirrhosis (underlying cause in 80% of cases), chronic hepatitis B infection (leading cause in Asia and Africa), and hepatitis C, alcoholism, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and primary biliary cirrhosis. The relationship between cirrhosis and HCC justifies biannual screening with ultrasound and AFP in patients with cirrhosis. The BCLC (Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer) classification is the most commonly used to guide treatment.

HCC treatment includes surgical resection (in patients with well-compensated cirrhosis and single tumor), liver transplantation (the treatment of choice in selected patients with cirrhosis and HCC within Milan criteria: one tumor <= 5 cm or up to 3 tumors <= 3 cm without vascular invasion), radiofrequency or microwave ablation (for tumors <= 3 cm), transarterial embolization (TACE), chemoembolization, radioembolization with Y-90, targeted therapy with sorafenib, lenvatinib, regorafenib, cabozantinib, and ramucirumab, and immunotherapy with atezolizumab + bevacizumab (first-line), durvalumab + tremelimumab (first-line), and nivolumab + ipilimumab (second-line).

### Leukemia and Lymphoma

Leukemias are cancers of the blood-forming tissues, including bone marrow and the lymphatic system. They are classified by speed of progression (acute or chronic) and cell type affected (myeloid or lymphoid). Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common acute leukemia in adults, with an incidence of 4-5 cases per 100,000 people per year and a median age at diagnosis of 68 years. It is characterized by the proliferation of abnormal myeloid blasts in the bone marrow, which interferes with normal blood production. Risk factors include prior chemotherapy exposure (especially alkylating agents), ionizing radiation, myelodysplastic syndromes, and prior myeloproliferative disorders. AML is classified according to the WHO system into groups with specific cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities, which have prognostic and therapeutic implications. The most common chromosomal translocations include t(8;21), t(15;17), inv(16) (favorable) and deletion of 5, deletion of 7, complex (unfavorable). Mutations in FLT3, NPM1, CEBPA, IDH1/2, TP53, and KIT are particularly important.

AML treatment includes remission induction (intensive chemotherapy with cytarabine and an anthracycline such as daunorubicin or idarubicin), consolidation (intensive chemotherapy with cytarabine, hematopoietic progenitor transplantation in high-risk patients, targeted therapies), and maintenance (in patients with acute promyelocytic leukemia). Targeted agents include midostaurin and gilteritinib for FLT3 mutations, enasidenib and ivosidenib for IDH2 and IDH1 mutations, respectively, and venetoclax (BCL-2 inhibitor) for elderly patients. Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), with the t(15;17) translocation, is treated with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide, achieving cure rates above 90%.

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common acute leukemia in children, with an incidence of 4-5 cases per 100,000 children per year, and a peak incidence between ages 2 and 5. B-cell ALL is the most common subtype (85%), and T-cell ALL accounts for 15%. Diagnosis is based on bone marrow biopsy with immunophenotyping (flow cytometry), cytogenetics, and molecular analysis. Prognostic factors include age, leukocyte count, cytogenetics (hypodiploidy, t(9;22) translocation, t(4;11) translocation are unfavorable; hyperdiploidy, t(12;21) translocation are favorable), and response to initial treatment. Treatment includes remission induction, consolidation/intensification, central nervous system therapy (prophylaxis with intrathecal chemotherapy and/or cranial radiation), maintenance, and, in high-risk patients, hematopoietic progenitor transplantation. Targeted therapies include blinatumomab (bispecific CD19-CD3 antibody) and inotuzumab ozogamicin (anti-CD22 antibody-drug conjugate) for relapsed/refractory B-cell ALL, and CAR-T cell therapy (tisagenlecleucel, brexucabtagene autoleucel) for children and young adults with B-cell ALL in second or greater relapse.

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is the most common leukemia in adults in Western countries, with a median age at diagnosis of 70 years and an incidence of 4.9 per 100,000 people per year. It is characterized by the accumulation of abnormal mature B lymphocytes in the blood, bone marrow, lymph nodes, spleen, and liver. CLL is classified according to the Rai (0-IV) or Binet (A-C) system based on disease extent. Prognostic factors include IGHV gene mutation (mutated = better prognosis), presence of 17p deletion (worst prognosis, chemotherapy resistance), 11q deletion, trisomy 12, and 13q deletion. Treatment has changed dramatically with the introduction of targeted agents: BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib), BCL-2 inhibitors (venetoclax), and monoclonal antibodies (obinutuzumab, rituximab). Chemotherapy (FCR: fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, rituximab) remains an option for young patients with good-prognosis CLL (IGHV mutated).

Lymphomas are cancers of the lymphatic system and are classified into Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL). HL accounts for 10% of lymphomas and is characterized by the presence of Reed-Sternberg cells in an inflammatory background. HL is classified into subtypes: nodular sclerosis (the most common, 70%), mixed cellularity, lymphocyte depletion, lymphocyte-rich, and not specified. Prognostic factors include Ann Arbor stage, presence of B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss > 10%), age, leukocyte count, and serum albumin. Treatment includes chemotherapy with ABVD (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) and radiation therapy, with cure rates above 80%. Second-line treatments include oxaliplatin, gemcitabine, bendamustine, and CAR-T cell therapy or hematopoietic progenitor transplantation. Brentuximab vedotin (anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugate) has improved outcomes in advanced and relapsed HL.

NHLs are a heterogeneous group of more than 60 subtypes of lymphoid cancers, accounting for 90% of lymphomas. The most common NHLs include diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL, the most common NHL, 30-40%), follicular lymphoma (20-30%), marginal zone lymphoma (10%), mantle cell lymphoma (3-5%), Burkitt lymphoma (1-2%), and peripheral T-cell lymphoma (10-15%). DLBCL is an aggressive lymphoma treated with immunotherapy (rituximab) and chemotherapy (R-CHOP: rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone), achieving cure rates of 60-70%. Follicular lymphoma is an indolent lymphoma that can be observed in early stages or treated with rituximab plus chemotherapy in advanced stages. Burkitt lymphoma is the fastest-growing human cancer and requires intensive chemotherapy with central nervous system prophylaxis.

### Skin Cancer (Melanoma)

Melanoma is the most aggressive skin cancer, responsible for the majority of skin cancer deaths despite representing only 1% of all cutaneous cancers. The global incidence of melanoma has increased in recent decades, with approximately 325,000 new cases and 57,000 deaths per year. Risk factors include intense intermittent sun exposure with sunburns in childhood and adolescence, history of sunburns, fair skin with freckles, large number of nevi (> 50), atypical nevi, family history of melanoma, genetic syndromes (xeroderma pigmentosum, dysplastic nevus syndrome), and immunosuppression. Melanoma originates in melanocytes, the pigment-producing cells of the skin, and is classified into subtypes: superficial spreading (70%), nodular (15%), lentigo maligna (10%), and acral (5%, including palms, soles, and under the nails).

Melanoma diagnosis is based on clinical evaluation using the ABCDE rule (Asymmetry, Irregular borders, Color variation, Diameter > 6 mm, Evolution) and dermoscopy, which allows non-invasive evaluation of suspicious lesions. Excisional biopsy with a 1-3 mm margin is the gold standard for histological diagnosis. Breslow thickness (depth of invasion in millimeters) is the most important prognostic factor. TNM staging evaluates tumor thickness (T), nodal involvement (N), and metastases (M). Molecular analysis includes determination of BRAF mutations (present in 40-50% of melanomas), NRAS (15-20%), and c-KIT (10-15%), which have therapeutic implications. Sentinel lymph node biopsy is recommended for melanomas with thickness >= 0.8 mm or with ulceration to evaluate nodal micrometastasis.

Melanoma treatment includes surgery (excision with 1-2 cm margin for in situ melanomas, 1-2 cm for melanomas <= 1 mm, 2 cm for melanomas > 1 mm), prophylactic lymphadenectomy in case of positive sentinel node, immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, ipilimumab, nivolumab + ipilimumab), targeted therapy with BRAF inhibitors (dabrafenib, vemurafenib, encorafenib) and MEK inhibitors (trametinib, cobimetinib, binimetinib) for melanomas with BRAF mutation, adjuvant therapy with pembrolizumab, nivolumab, or dabrafenib + trametinib in high-risk patients, and neoadjuvant therapy with immunotherapy combinations. Radiation therapy is used in specific situations, such as uveal melanoma or brain metastases. Checkpoint inhibitors have transformed the prognosis of advanced melanoma, with 5-year survival rates of 50% compared to less than 10% before immunotherapy.

### Precision Medicine in Cancer

Precision medicine in cancer is a therapeutic approach that uses genomic, molecular, and clinical information from the tumor and patient to personalize treatment, selecting the most effective therapy and minimizing adverse effects. Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have enabled complete exome or tumor genome sequencing, identifying driver mutations, copy number alterations, chromosomal rearrangements, and gene expression profiles that may be therapeutic targets. Cancer gene panels, which simultaneously analyze multiple genes relevant to therapeutic decision-making, are increasingly used in clinical practice. The World Health Organization (WHO) has published molecular classifications for multiple cancer types, establishing the standard for molecular diagnosis.

The main types of targeted therapies in cancer include: tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib for chronic myeloid leukemia with BCR-ABL, erlotinib and osimertinib for lung cancer with EGFR mutations, crizotinib and alectinib for lung cancer with ALK translocation), MAPK kinase inhibitors (vemurafenib and dabrafenib for melanoma with BRAF V600E mutation), PARP inhibitors (olaparib, niraparib, rucaparib for ovarian and breast cancer with BRCA1/2 mutations), monoclonal antibodies (trastuzumab for HER2+ cancer, bevacizumab anti-VEGF, cetuximab anti-EGFR), antibody-drug conjugates (ado-trastuzumab emtansine, trastuzumab deruxtecan, sacituzumab govitecan), and cellular therapies (CAR-T cells, chimeric antigen receptor T cells).

CAR-T cell therapy is a form of adoptive immunotherapy in which the patient's T cells are genetically modified to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) that recognizes a specific antigen on cancer cells. Approved CAR-T therapies include tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah), axicabtagene ciloleucel (Yescarta), lisocabtagene maraleucel (Breyanzi), and brexucabtagene autoleucel (Tecartus) for non-Hodgkin lymphomas and B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Adverse effects of CAR-T cells include cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), and prolonged cytopenia. Minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring through quantitative PCR, high-sensitivity flow cytometry, or circulating tumor DNA sequencing is essential for evaluating treatment response and guiding therapeutic decisions.

## Chapter 13: Cardiovascular Diseases

### Coronary Artery Disease

Coronary artery disease (CAD), also known as ischemic heart disease, is the leading cause of death and disability worldwide, responsible for approximately 8.9 million deaths per year. It is characterized by reduced blood flow to the heart muscle due to atherosclerosis, a chronic inflammatory process involving the accumulation of fatty plaques, cholesterol, calcium, and other substances in the walls of the coronary arteries. Plaques can be stable (with a thick fibrous cap) or unstable (with a thin fibrous cap and large lipid core), and unstable plaques are prone to rupture, which triggers thrombus formation that can completely block the artery and cause acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Risk factors for CAD include hypertension, elevated cholesterol (especially LDL), diabetes, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, family history of premature coronary artery disease (men < 55 years, women < 65 years), and advanced age.

The clinical presentation of CAD includes stable angina (oppressive chest pain triggered by physical activity and relieved by rest or nitroglycerin administration), unstable angina (rest pain or new-onset pain, with changing pattern), ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). Diagnosis is performed through electrocardiography (ECG), which may show ST-segment elevation or depression, T-wave inversion, and presence of pathological Q waves; cardiac necrosis biomarkers (high-sensitivity troponin I or T, CK-MB); echocardiography, which may show segmental motility abnormalities; stress tests (exercise or with imaging such as echocardiography or myocardial perfusion); and coronary angiography, which is the gold standard for evaluating coronary anatomy and stenosis severity.

CAD treatment includes lifestyle measures (Mediterranean diet, regular physical exercise, smoking cessation, weight control, stress management), anti-ischemic pharmacotherapy (beta-blockers such as metoprolol, bisoprolol, and carvedilol; nitrates such as sublingual nitroglycerin and isosorbide mononitrate; calcium channel blockers such as amlodipine and diltiazem; and ranolazine), cardiovascular event prevention pharmacotherapy (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, prasugrel; statins such as atorvastatin and rosuvastatin; ACEIs or ARBs; PCSK9 inhibitors such as evolocumab and alirocumab), and revascularization procedures (coronary angioplasty with stent implantation, preferably drug-eluting stents with everolimus or zotarolimus; and coronary bypass surgery with internal mammary artery, saphenous vein, or radial artery graft). The choice of revascularization type depends on coronary anatomy, left ventricular function, comorbidities, and patient preference, and is guided by the SYNTAX score and Heart Team.

### Heart Failure

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome resulting from any structural or functional abnormality that impairs the left ventricle's ability to fill with or eject blood adequately. It affects approximately 64 million people worldwide and is the leading cause of hospitalization in people over 65 years of age. It is classified by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) into HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, LVEF <= 40%), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF, LVEF 41-49%), and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF, LVEF >= 50%). Causes of HFrEF include coronary artery disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, severe hypertension, myocarditis, valvular disease, and genetic cardiomyopathies. Causes of HFpEF include hypertension, diabetes, obesity, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. HF symptoms include exertional dyspnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, orthopnea, peripheral edema, fatigue, exercise intolerance, and pulmonary congestion.

Diagnosis is confirmed through echocardiography, which evaluates LVEF, ventricular dimensions, diastolic function, pulmonary artery pressure, and valvular function. Biomarkers include B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and its N-terminal fragment (NT-proBNP), which are elevated in HF and correlate with severity. Etiological workup may include coronary angiography, cardiac MRI, endomyocardial biopsy, and genetic testing. HFrEF treatment has evolved significantly with the introduction of the four foundational therapies: angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers (carvedilol, bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs, spironolactone, eplerenone), and neprilysin/angiotensin inhibitors (ARNIs, sacubitril/valsartan). These four therapies have been shown to reduce mortality and HF hospitalizations.

HFpEF treatment includes control of blood pressure, atrial fibrillation, obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and comorbidities. Diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide) are used to relieve congestion. Newer agents such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin (SGLT2 inhibitors) have demonstrated benefits in HFpEF. Ventricular assist devices (VADs) are an option as a bridge to transplantation or as destination therapy in patients with end-stage HF. Heart transplantation remains the treatment of choice for end-stage HF, with a median survival of 12.5 years.

### Arrhythmias

Arrhythmias are disturbances in heart rhythm that can be benign or potentially life-threatening. They are classified as tachycardias (HR > 100 bpm), bradycardias (HR < 60 bpm), and extrasystoles (premature beats). The most common arrhythmias include atrial fibrillation (AF), atrial flutter, sinus node reentrant tachycardia, multifocal atrial tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia (VT), and ventricular fibrillation (VF). AF is the most common sustained arrhythmia, affecting 2-4% of the adult population and increasing with age (up to 10% in people over 80). It is classified as paroxysmal (resolves spontaneously in < 7 days), persistent (requires cardioversion), long-standing persistent (> 12 months), and permanent. AF increases the risk of stroke (CVA) by 5-fold, heart failure, and cardiovascular death.

Arrhythmia diagnosis is performed through 12-lead electrocardiography, Holter monitoring (24-72 hours), event recording, wireless monitoring (patch), electrophysiological study (EPS), and three-dimensional mapping. EPS is an invasive test that allows identification of the arrhythmia mechanism (reentry, automaticity, or triggered activity) and guides treatment with catheter ablation. The CHA2DS2-VASc scoring system is used to evaluate stroke risk in AF patients and guide the decision on anticoagulation: heart failure (1), hypertension (1), age >= 75 years (2), diabetes (1), prior stroke/TIA/thromboembolism (2), vascular disease (1), age 65-74 years (1), and female sex (1). HAS-BLED evaluates bleeding risk with anticoagulants.

AF treatment includes heart rate control (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers such as diltiazem and verapamil, digoxin, amiodarone), rhythm control (electrical or pharmacological cardioversion, catheter ablation of AF, antiarrhythmics such as flecainide, propafenone, amiodarone, dofetilide, ibutilide), and anticoagulation (direct oral anticoagulants [DOACs] such as apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, and dabigatran, or vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin with target INR 2.0-3.0). Catheter ablation of AF, which involves ablation of the pulmonary veins to electrically isolate the triggering focus, is increasingly used as first-line treatment for paroxysmal and persistent AF.

Ventricular arrhythmias (ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation) are potentially life-threatening and require urgent treatment. Sustained VT can cause hemodynamic instability, cardiac collapse, and death. Acute VT treatment includes synchronized electrical cardioversion if the patient is stable, or electrical shock (defibrillation) if the patient is unstable or in VF. Maintenance treatment includes antiarrhythmics (amiodarone, sotalol, dofetilide), catheter ablation of VT, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation for primary prevention (patients with LVEF <= 35% despite optimal medical therapy) and secondary prevention (survivors of sudden cardiac death). Brugada syndrome, long QT syndrome, short QT syndrome, ARVC syndrome, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy are conditions that predispose to ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death.

### Valvular Heart Disease

Valvular heart diseases are abnormalities of the heart valves that can cause stenosis (narrowing of the valve), insufficiency (regurgitation), or both. The most common causes include degenerative disease (senile aortic calcification, mitral valve prolapse), rheumatic disease (mitral stenosis, aortic stenosis), infectious endocarditis, congenital heart disease (pulmonary stenosis, tricuspid atresia), and aortic dissection. Aortic stenosis is the most common valvular surgery disease in developed countries, affecting 2-3% of people over 65 and 4-8% of people over 85. It is classified as severe (valve area < 1.0 cm2, mean gradient > 40 mmHg or peak velocity > 4.0 m/s), moderate (area 1.0-1.5 cm2), and mild (area 1.5-2.0 cm2).

Valvular disease diagnosis is performed through transthoracic echocardiography, which is the gold standard for evaluating valvular anatomy, function, severity, and hemodynamic consequences. Transesophageal echocardiography may provide higher resolution images for surgical planning. Cardiac catheterization is used to confirm hemodynamic severity and evaluate concomitant coronary artery disease. Symptoms of severe valvular disease include exertional dyspnea, angina, syncope, and signs of heart failure. The prognosis of severe symptomatic aortic stenosis is poor without treatment, with a median survival of 2-3 years.

Surgical treatment of valvular disease includes valve repair (preferred over replacement when possible, especially for the mitral valve) and valve replacement (mechanical or bioprosthetic). Mechanical replacements are more durable but require lifelong anticoagulation with warfarin (INR 2.5-3.5 for mitral valve, 2.0-3.0 for aortic valve). Bioprosthetic replacements do not require anticoagulation but have a limited lifespan of 10-20 years. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVI/TAVR) is an alternative to open surgery in high- or intermediate-surgical-risk patients and is expanding to low-risk patients. Percutaneous mitral valve repair with MitraClip is an option for patients with severe symptomatic mitral regurgitation who are not surgical candidates.

### Cerebrovascular Diseases

Cerebrovascular diseases are the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide, responsible for approximately 12.2 million deaths per year. Stroke is the most common acute manifestation and is classified as ischemic (85%) and hemorrhagic (15%). Ischemic stroke can be caused by atherothrombotic disease (large vessel disease), cardioembolism (atrial fibrillation), small vessel disease (lipohyalinosis), other determined cause, or undetermined cause. Hemorrhagic stroke is divided into intracerebral (parenchymal hemorrhage) and subarachnoid (hemorrhage in the subarachnoid space, usually from aneurysm rupture). Risk factors include hypertension (the most important modifiable risk factor), atrial fibrillation, diabetes, smoking, hypercholesterolemia, obesity, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, and sleep apnea.

Stroke diagnosis is performed through clinical evaluation (NIHSS score: National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) and neuroimaging tests. Urgent cranial CT is essential for differentiating ischemic from hemorrhagic stroke. Brain MRI with diffusion sequences (DWI) is more sensitive for detecting early ischemia. CT or MR angiography allows visualization of intracranial and extracranial vasculature. Echocardiography and Holter monitoring are performed to identify cardioembolic sources. Laboratory tests include complete blood count, glucose, electrolytes, renal function, coagulation, and troponin.

Acute ischemic stroke treatment includes intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase (rt-PA) or tenecteplase, which must be administered within the first 4.5 hours of symptom onset (ideally within the first 60 minutes, golden hour). Mechanical thrombectomy with aspiration catheter or stent-retriever is an option for patients with large vessel occlusion (internal carotid artery, proximal middle cerebral artery) within the first 6-24 hours, according to imaging criteria (ischemic penumbra). Supportive management includes blood pressure control (do not treat aggressively in the first 24-48 hours unless > 220/120 mmHg), glucose control, oxygenation, temperature, prevention of complications (pneumonia, urinary infections, deep vein thrombosis), and early rehabilitation. Secondary prevention of ischemic stroke includes antiplatelet agents (aspirin, clopidogrel, aspirin + clopidogrel), anticoagulants for AF, statins, blood pressure control, and lifestyle modifications.

Hemorrhagic intracerebral stroke treatment includes aggressive blood pressure control (target < 140/90 mmHg), correction of coagulopathy (protamine for heparin, vitamin K and PCC for warfarin, idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors), prevention and treatment of expanding hematoma, intracranial pressure management (head elevation, mannitol, hypertonic saline solution, therapeutic hypothermia), and surgical evacuation in selected cases (large superficial hematomas, intraventricular hemorrhage with hydrocephalus, vascular malformations). Primary prevention of hemorrhagic stroke includes blood pressure control, anticoagulation management, and detection and treatment of vascular malformations.

### Peripheral Arterial Disease

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a systemic manifestation of atherosclerosis that affects the arteries of the extremities, primarily the legs. It affects approximately 236 million people worldwide, and its prevalence increases with age (10-15% in people over 70) and is higher in men. Risk factors are the same as for coronary artery disease: smoking (the most important risk factor), diabetes (4 times higher risk), hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and chronic kidney disease. PAD is a marker of systemic cardiovascular disease, and patients with PAD have a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, stroke) and cardiovascular death. Most patients are asymptomatic, but symptoms include claudication (calf pain when walking that is relieved by rest), rest pain, ulcers, and gangrene.

PAD diagnosis is performed through the ankle-brachial index (ABI), which is the ratio of ankle systolic blood pressure to arm systolic blood pressure. An ABI < 0.9 indicates PAD, and an ABI < 0.4 indicates severe disease. Doppler ultrasound, CT angiography, MR angiography, and digital subtraction angiography are imaging tests to evaluate the location and severity of the disease. The Fontaine classification is used to stratify the disease by symptoms (Stage I: asymptomatic; Stage II: claudication; Stage III: rest pain; Stage IV: ulcers and gangrene). The Rutherford classification assigns grades from 0 to 6 based on clinical severity.

PAD treatment includes lifestyle modifications (smoking cessation, supervised exercise for 30-45 minutes at least 3 times per week, healthy diet), pharmacotherapy (aspirin, clopidogrel, statins, cilostazol for claudication, antihypertensives), and revascularization (angioplasty with stent, bypass surgery with saphenous vein or prosthetic graft, endarterectomy). Angioplasty with stent is the first-line treatment for iliac artery and superficial femoral artery lesions. Bypass surgery is preferred for iliac artery disease and long femoral-popliteal artery occlusion. Gene and cellular therapy are under investigation as future options for patients without revascularization options.

## Chapter 14: Mental Health

### Depression

Major depressive disorder is a mood disorder characterized by persistent feelings of sadness, loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia), changes in appetite and weight, sleep disturbances (insomnia or hypersomnia), fatigue, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, difficulty concentrating, psychomotor retardation, and recurrent thoughts of death or suicide. It affects approximately 280 million people worldwide and is the leading cause of disability globally, responsible for more than 700,000 suicide deaths per year. Lifetime prevalence is 15-20% for women and 7-12% for men, with typical onset between ages 20 and 30. Risk factors include family history of depression, personal history of mood disorders, stressful life events (loss of a loved one, divorce, unemployment), childhood trauma (physical, sexual, or emotional abuse), chronic diseases, substance use, and biological factors (neurotransmitter dysfunction such as serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis alterations, systemic inflammation, reduced neuroplasticity).

Depression diagnosis is based on DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition) or ICD-11 criteria, which require the presence of at least five symptoms for at least two weeks, including depressed mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure. Assessment scales include the PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9), the BDI (Beck Depression Inventory), and the HAM-D (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale). Differential diagnosis must exclude bipolar disorder, grief, anxiety disorders, personality disorders, hypothyroidism, anemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, and medication side effects. Depression can be classified as mild, moderate, or severe, and may present with psychotic features (delusions or hallucinations), catatonia, perinatal onset, or seasonal pattern.

Depression treatment includes psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), and emerging therapies. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the most studied and has demonstrated efficacy similar to pharmacotherapy in mild to moderate depression. Interpersonal therapy (IPT), behavioral activation therapy (BATD), and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) are also effective. Antidepressants include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs: fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine, citalopram), serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs: venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine, milnacipran), norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitors (bupropion), tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, clomipramine, imipramine), monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs: phenelzine, tranylcypromine), and others (mirtazapine, trazodone, agomelatine, vortioxetine, vilazodone). Treatment should be maintained for at least 6-12 months after remission to prevent relapse, and patients with three or more episodes may require lifelong treatment.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive therapy that uses magnetic pulses to stimulate specific areas of the brain (typically the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex) and has demonstrated efficacy in treatment-resistant depression. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) remains the most effective treatment for severe treatment-resistant depression, with remission rates of 50-70%, but is associated with transient cognitive effects. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) and deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subcallosal nucleus are options for refractory cases. Ketamine and its enantiomer esketamine (Spravato) are rapid-acting therapies for treatment-resistant depression, with antidepressant effects within hours. Psilocybin is being investigated as a potential therapy for treatment-resistant depression.

### Anxiety Disorder

Anxiety disorders are a group of mental disorders characterized by excessive feelings of fear, worry, and avoidance behaviors that significantly interfere with daily life. They include generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), panic disorder, specific phobia, agoraphobia, social anxiety disorder (social phobia), and separation anxiety disorder. GAD is characterized by excessive and persistent worry about multiple areas of life for at least 6 months, accompanied by restlessness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbances. Panic disorder is characterized by recurrent panic attacks (episodes of intense fear with physical symptoms such as palpitations, sweating, trembling, sensation of choking, chest pain, dizziness, fear of dying or going crazy) and persistent concern about the recurrence of attacks. Lifetime prevalence of anxiety disorders is 15-30%, with women being twice as affected as men.

Diagnosis is based on DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria, and is supplemented by assessment scales such as the GAD-7 (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7) for GAD, the Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS) for panic disorder, and the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS) for social anxiety. Differential diagnosis must exclude medical causes (hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, heart disease, pulmonary disease), substance adverse effects (caffeine, cocaine, amphetamines, corticosteroids), depressive disorders, and sleep disorders. Anxiety disorders frequently coexist with depression, substance use disorders, and other medical conditions.

Treatment includes psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the most effective, including gradual exposure to feared stimuli, cognitive restructuring, muscle relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing, and systematic desensitization. Medications include SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine) as first-line, SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), pregabalin (for GAD), benzodiazepines (alprazolam, lorazepam, clonazepam) for short-term use (risk of dependence), buspirone (for GAD), and hydroxyzine (acute anxiety). Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), and mindfulness meditation are effective complementary therapies. Regular physical exercise, reduced caffeine and alcohol consumption, and good sleep hygiene are important lifestyle interventions.

### Bipolar Disorder

Bipolar disorder is a mood disorder characterized by episodes of mania or hypomania alternating with episodes of depression. Bipolar I disorder is defined by the presence of at least one manic episode (elevated or irritable mood, increased energy, decreased need for sleep, flight of ideas, distractibility, risk-taking behaviors such as excessive spending, reckless sexual behavior, foolish investments), lasting at least 7 days or requiring hospitalization, usually followed by depressive episodes. Bipolar II disorder is defined by alternating hypomanic episodes (less severe than mania, lasting at least 4 days) and major depressive episodes. Cyclothymia or cyclothymic disorder is characterized by chronic mood fluctuations between hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet criteria for full episodes. The prevalence of bipolar disorder is 1-2%, with typical onset in adolescence or early adulthood, and it affects men and women equally.

Bipolar disorder diagnosis is frequently delayed (average of 10 years from the first episode) due to the varied presentation, confusion with unipolar depression (40% of bipolar patients are initially diagnosed with depression), and underestimation of hypomanic episodes. Detailed clinical history, evaluation of family history of bipolar disorder, and use of scales such as the Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ) and Hypomania Checklist (HCL-32) are useful tools. Differential diagnosis must exclude major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, personality disorders (especially borderline), substance use disorders, hyperthyroidism, and substance effects (antidepressants, steroids, stimulants).

Bipolar disorder treatment requires mood stabilizers to prevent relapse and treat acute episodes. Lithium is the oldest and most effective mood stabilizer, with demonstrated efficacy in preventing manic and depressive episodes, and anti-suicide effect. Monitoring serum lithium levels (therapeutic range: 0.6-1.0 mEq/L) and monitoring renal and thyroid function are essential. Anticonvulsants (valproate, lamotrigine, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine) are alternatives or adjuncts to lithium. Lamotrigine is particularly effective for preventing depressive episodes. Atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, risperidone, paliperidone, lurasidone, cariprazine) are effective for both manic and depressive episodes. Treatment of bipolar depressive episodes is particularly challenging, as antidepressants may precipitate mania or accelerate cycling. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is an option for severe treatment-resistant episodes. Psychoeducation, cognitive behavioral therapy, and regular social rhythms are important non-pharmacological interventions.

### Schizophrenia and Psychotic Disorders

Schizophrenia is a chronic psychotic disorder affecting approximately 24 million people worldwide (1 in 300 people), with similar prevalence in men and women, although onset is typically earlier in men (18-25 years) than in women (25-35 years). It is characterized by positive symptoms (hallucinations, usually auditory; delusions, which may be paranoid, grandiose, or referential; disorganized thinking and disorganized motor behavior), negative symptoms (flat affect, alogia, anhedonia, avolition, social isolation), and cognitive symptoms (deficits in attention, working memory, executive function). Risk factors include family history (risk is 10 times higher if a first-degree relative is affected), obstetric complications, prenatal infections, cannabis use in adolescence, migration, social adversity, and childhood trauma. The dopamine hypothesis postulates that hyperactivity in the mesolimbic pathway causes positive symptoms, and hypoactivity in the mesocortical pathway causes negative and cognitive symptoms.

Diagnosis is based on DSM-5 criteria, which require at least two symptoms from the positive, negative, or disorganized group for at least one month, with significant functional impairment. Assessment scales include the PANSS (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale) for symptoms, the CGI (Clinical Global Impression) for overall severity, and the GAF (Global Assessment of Functioning) for functioning. Differential diagnosis must exclude brief psychotic disorder, schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, substance-induced psychosis, medical illness-related psychosis, and mood disorders with psychotic features. Neuroimaging shows reduced brain volume, ventricular dilation, and white matter alterations.

First-line treatment includes antipsychotics, which act by blocking dopamine D2 receptors. Typical antipsychotics (haloperidol, chlorpromazine, fluphenazine) are effective for positive symptoms but cause more extrapyramidal effects (parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia, akathisia, dystonia). Atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, paliperidone, ziprasidone, lurasidone, cariprazine, brexpiprazole, lumateperone) have lower risk of extrapyramidal effects but higher risk of metabolic syndrome (weight gain, diabetes, dyslipidemia). Clozapine is the most effective antipsychotic, especially in treatment-resistant schizophrenia (inadequate response to at least two antipsychotics), but requires monitoring for agranulocytosis (weekly white blood cell count for the first 6 months, then biweekly). Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is an option for acute treatment-resistant episodes. Psychosocial interventions include psychoeducation, cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis, social skills training, cognitive rehabilitation, occupational support, and family support.

### Eating Disorders

Eating disorders (EDs) are psychiatric disorders characterized by persistent disturbances in eating behavior and body weight perception that significantly affect physical and psychological health. Anorexia nervosa is characterized by caloric restriction leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of gaining weight, and disturbance in the perception of weight or body shape. It has the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder (5-10%), from medical complications (malnutrition, arrhythmias, heart failure, osteoporosis) or suicide. Bulimia nervosa is characterized by recurrent episodes of binge eating (ingestion of an abnormally large amount of food with a sense of loss of control) followed by inappropriate compensatory behaviors (self-induced vomiting, laxative use, diuretics, enemas, fasting, or excessive exercise). Binge eating disorder is characterized by recurrent episodes of binge eating without regular compensatory behaviors, associated with marked distress. Other specified eating disorders include avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder, pica, rumination disorder, and orthorexia nervosa.

Diagnosis is based on DSM-5 criteria and clinical evaluation, which includes dietary history, nutritional status assessment (weight, BMI, body composition), medical complication assessment (complete blood work, ECG, bone densitometry, endocrinological evaluation), and psychopathological assessment. Assessment scales include the Eating Disorder Examination (EDE), the SCOFF, and the Eating Attitudes Test (EAT-26). Differential diagnosis must exclude other causes of weight loss or eating behavior disturbances (depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, gastrointestinal diseases, endocrinological conditions).

Treatment is multidisciplinary and includes psychotherapy, nutrition, medical treatment, and, in severe cases, hospitalization. Enhanced cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-E) is the first-line treatment for bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder. Family-based treatment (Maudsley Model) is the first-line treatment for adolescents with anorexia nervosa. Pharmacotherapy includes SSRIs (fluoxetine for bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder), tricyclic antidepressants (clomipramine), and lisdexamfetamine (for binge eating disorder). Hospitalization is necessary for patients with severe malnutrition (BMI < 15), hemodynamic instability, arrhythmias, self-harm, or suicidal ideation. Intensive residential and inpatient treatment programs are needed for patients who do not respond to outpatient treatment.

### Suicide and Prevention

Suicide is the second leading cause of death in people aged 15 to 29 worldwide, and responsible for more than 700,000 deaths per year. For every suicide death, it is estimated that there are 20 suicide attempts. Risk factors include mental disorders (depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, substance use disorders, personality disorders), history of suicide attempts, suicidal ideation, access to lethal means, stressful life events (job loss, divorce, bereavement), loneliness and social isolation, chronic medical conditions, chronic pain, and family history of suicide. Protective factors include strong social networks, access to mental health care, religiosity or spirituality, restrictive parenting patterns, parenthood and pregnancy, and restriction of access to lethal means.

Suicide risk assessment is an essential part of mental health care and includes evaluation of suicidal ideation (frequency, intensity, plan, previous attempts), presence of plans and means, presence of risk and protective factors, and the patient's ability to maintain safety. Assessment scales include the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS), PHQ-9 (item 9), and the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSI). The clinical interview should be empathetic, non-judgmental, and direct when asking about suicidal ideation and plans.

Suicide prevention is addressed at multiple levels. Universal prevention includes restriction of access to lethal means (firearm access control, bridge railings, packaging medications in single-dose units), public awareness campaigns, mental health promotion, and stigma reduction. Selective prevention targets high-risk groups (people with mental disorders, previous attempts, bereavement, unemployment) with interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy for suicide prevention (CBT-SP), dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), and problem-solving therapy. Indicated prevention targets people with suicidal ideation or recent attempts with intensive interventions, hospitalization if necessary, environmental safety, and close follow-up. School, workplace, and community suicide prevention programs are essential for creating protective environments.

## Chapter 15: Autoimmune Diseases and Allergies

### Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease that can affect virtually any organ in the body, including the skin, joints, kidneys, brain, lungs, heart, and blood. It is characterized by the production of autoantibodies, particularly antinuclear antibodies (ANA), which attack cellular nuclear components. It primarily affects women of childbearing age (female-to-male ratio of 9:1), with a prevalence of 20-150 per 100,000 people, being more common in people of African, Hispanic, and Asian ancestry. Predisposing factors include genetic predisposition (association with HLA-DR2, HLA-DR3, complement C1q, C2, C4), hormonal factors (estrogens can modulate the immune response), environmental factors (sun exposure, infections, smoking, certain medications such as procainamide, hydralazine, isoniazid), and stress.

SLE clinical presentation is extremely variable. The most common symptoms include non-erosive arthritis, fatigue, fever, weight loss, malar butterfly rash, photosensitive skin eruption, oral or nasal ulcers, alopecia, serositis (pleuritis, pericarditis, peritonitis), lupus nephritis, neurological symptoms (seizures, psychosis, stroke), and hematological manifestations (hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia). The 2019 EULAR/ACR classification system, which replaces the previous ACR/EULAR 2012 classification criteria, uses a scoring system that includes clinical and immunological criteria.

Diagnosis is based on the combination of clinical and immunological criteria. ANA are sensitive (> 95%) but not specific, and a negative ANA makes SLE diagnosis unlikely. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith antibodies are specific for SLE, and anti-dsDNA antibodies correlate with disease activity, particularly lupus nephritis. Antiphospholipid antibodies (anticardiolipin antibody, lupus anticoagulant, anti-beta2-glycoprotein I antibody) are associated with antiphospholipid syndrome, which causes arterial and venous thrombosis and pregnancy complications. Lupus nephritis is diagnosed through renal biopsy, which is classified according to ISN/RPS into six classes (I-VI), and guides treatment.

SLE treatment includes antimalarial medications (hydroxychloroquine, which reduces disease activity, flares, and complications, and is recommended for all SLE patients unless contraindicated), glucocorticoids (to control disease activity, using the lowest possible dose to minimize adverse effects), immunosuppressants (azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate) for moderate to severe disease, and biological therapies (belimumab, BAFF/BLyS inhibitor; rituximab, anti-CD20 antibody; anifrolumab, type I interferon receptor antibody). Lupus nephritis treatment includes induction with mycophenolate mofetil or cyclophosphamide pulses plus glucocorticoids, followed by maintenance with mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine. Prevention includes sun protection (using SPF 50+ sunscreen, avoiding direct sun exposure), vaccination (before starting immunosuppressants), monitoring of renal and cardiovascular function, and control of cardiovascular risk factors.

### Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease that causes synovial inflammation, progressive joint destruction, and disability. It affects 0.5-1% of the population, with a prevalence three times higher in women than in men, and a peak incidence between ages 40 and 60. Chronic synovial inflammation causes pannus (aggressive granulation tissue) that destroys cartilage, bone, and ligaments, deforming joints. Risk factors include genetic predisposition (HLA-DR4, HLA-DR1, HLA-DRB1*0401), smoking, obesity, infections (Epstein-Barr virus, Porphyromonas gingivalis), and hormones. Symptoms include prolonged morning stiffness (> 1 hour), symmetric joint pain and swelling (wrists, metacarpophalangeal, proximal interphalangeal, knees, ankles), fatigue, fever, weight loss, and extra-articular manifestations (rheumatoid nodules, vasculitis, interstitial lung disease, sicca syndrome, anemia of chronic disease, pericarditis).

Diagnosis is based on the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, which include joint involvement, serology (rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP), acute phase reactants (CRP, ESR), and symptom duration. Rheumatoid factor (RF) is sensitive (60-80%) but not specific, and anti-CCP antibodies are more specific (95%) and are associated with more aggressive disease. Anti-CCP antibodies may be present years before clinical onset. Imaging tests include hand and foot X-rays (erosions, joint space narrowing, periarticular osteoporosis), joint ultrasound (early detection of synovitis, erosions), and joint MRI (synovitis, erosions, bone edema).

RA treatment is based on the treat-to-target paradigm, with the goal of achieving remission or low disease activity within the first 3-6 months. Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) are the foundation of treatment: methotrexate is the first-line drug (15-25 mg/week with folic acid supplementation), followed by leflunomide, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, and biological therapies (tumor necrosis factor [TNF] inhibitors: adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, certolizumab, golimumab; IL-6 inhibitors: tocilizumab, sarilumab; B cell inhibitors: rituximab, abatacept; JAK inhibitors: tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib, filgotinib). Glucocorticoids are used as a rapid-acting bridge at the lowest possible dose and for the shortest time possible. Surgery (synovectomy, arthroplasty, arthrodesis) is reserved for severe deformities or joint destruction. Rehabilitation, occupational therapy, and psychological support are essential components of comprehensive management.

### Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a demyelinating autoimmune disease of the central nervous system that causes inflammation, demyelination, and neuronal degeneration. It affects approximately 2.8 million people worldwide, with a prevalence twice as high in women as in men, and a peak diagnosis between ages 20 and 40. It is classified as relapsing-remitting (RRMS, 85% of cases, with episodes of worsening followed by partial or complete recovery), secondary progressive (SPMS, gradual progression after RRMS), primary progressive (PPMS, gradual progression from onset without acute episodes), and progressive-relapsing (PRMS). The exact cause is unknown, but it is believed to involve an interaction between genetic factors (HLA-DRB1*15:01, more than 200 risk loci), environmental factors (vitamin D deficiency, Epstein-Barr virus infection, smoking, obesity in adolescence), and hormonal factors. The immunological hypothesis postulates that T and B lymphocytes cross the blood-brain barrier and initiate an autoimmune response against myelin.

MS symptoms are extremely variable and depend on the location of demyelinating lesions. The most common symptoms include visual disturbances (optic neuritis: painful unilateral vision loss, which partially recovers over weeks), muscle weakness, spasticity, gait disturbances, paresthesias and numbness, fatigue (present in 80% of patients), cognitive difficulty, bladder and bowel dysfunction, vertigo, diplopia, and nystagmus. Diagnosis is based on the 2017 McDonald criteria, which require demonstration of demyelination in time and space. Brain and spinal cord MRI with gadolinium is the gold standard, showing hyperintense T2/FLAIR lesions in periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal white matter. Cerebrospinal fluid may show oligoclonal bands and elevated intrathecal IgG. Evoked potentials show subclinical demyelination.

MS treatment includes relapse management (intravenous corticosteroids: methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3-5 days; plasmapheresis if no response), disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) to reduce the frequency and severity of relapses and delay progression, and symptomatic treatment. First-line DMTs include interferon beta (1a, 1b), glatiramer acetate, dimethyl fumarate, teriflunomide, fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod, ponesimod, alemtuzumab, natalizumab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, cladribine, diroximel fumarate, and peficitinib. DMTs are classified as high-efficacy therapies (natalizumab, alemtuzumab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, cladribine) and moderate-efficacy therapies (interferons, glatiramer acetate, fumarates). Symptomatic treatment includes baclofen and tizanidine for spasticity, amantadine and modafinil for fatigue, duloxetine for neuropathic pain, anticholinergics for bladder dysfunction, and cognitive behavioral therapy for depression.

### Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) comprises two chronic conditions of the gastrointestinal tract: Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). CD can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract (mouth to anus), with most frequent involvement of the terminal ileum and colon, and is characterized by transmural inflammation (affecting all layers of the intestinal wall), fistula formation, stenosis, and abscesses. UC affects only the colon and rectum, with inflammation limited to the mucosa and submucosa, and is characterized by bloody diarrhea, urgency, and abdominal pain. The incidence of IBD has increased in recent decades, especially in industrializing countries, with an incidence of 5-10/100,000 persons/year for CD and 10-20/100,000 persons/year for UC in Western countries. Risk factors include genetic predisposition (more than 240 risk loci, especially NOD2/CARD15 for CD), environmental factors (smoking, appendectomy, high-fat and high-sugar diet, stress, antibiotics in childhood), altered gut microbiota, and altered immune response.

CD clinical presentation includes abdominal pain (often in the right lower quadrant), diarrhea (usually non-bloody), weight loss, fever, fatigue, and extraintestinal manifestations (arthritis, erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, uveitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, biliary stenosis). UC presents with bloody diarrhea, rectal urgency, tenesmo, colicky abdominal pain, and weight loss. IBD complications include intestinal obstruction, fistulas, abscesses, perforation, massive bleeding, toxic megacolon (UC), and increased risk of colorectal cancer (especially in long-standing extensive UC). Diagnosis is based on endoscopy with biopsy (characteristic macroscopic and microscopic findings), imaging tests (ultrasound, CT, MRI to evaluate complications), blood tests (anemia, elevated CRP and ESR, iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiency), and fecal markers (fecal calprotectin, a non-invasive marker of intestinal inflammation).

IBD treatment includes pharmacological therapies, nutrition, surgery, and multidisciplinary management. 5-aminosalicylates (mesalazine, sulfasalazine) are the foundation of treatment for mild to moderate UC. Corticosteroids (prednisone, budesonide) are used to control acute flares but are not suitable for maintenance. Immunomodulators (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine, methotrexate, tacrolimus) are used to maintain remission. Biological therapies include TNF inhibitors (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab, golimumab), integrin inhibitors (vedolizumab, natalizumab), IL-12/23 inhibitors (ustekinumab), IL-23 inhibitors (risankizumab, guselkumab, mirikizumab), and JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib). Surgery is necessary in 30-50% of CD patients and 15-20% of UC patients (colectomy). Exclusive enteral nutrition is an effective therapy for pediatric CD.

### Allergies

Allergies are exaggerated immune responses to generally harmless substances (allergens) that can cause anything from mild symptoms to anaphylaxis, a potentially life-threatening allergic reaction. Allergy prevalence has increased significantly in recent decades, affecting 20-30% of the population in developed countries. The most common types of allergies include food allergy, allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, atopic dermatitis, drug allergy, insect venom allergy, and latex allergy. The most common allergens include foods (milk, egg, fish, shellfish, nuts, wheat, soy, peanuts), dust mites, pollen, animal hair, mold, insect stings (bees, wasps), and medications (penicillins, sulfonamides, NSAIDs).

Allergy pathophysiology includes sensitization (initial exposure to the allergen that induces specific IgE production by B lymphocytes), the effector phase (re-exposure to the allergen that crosses IgE bound to mast cells and basophils, causing degranulation and release of mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins), and late-phase inflammation (recruitment of eosinophils, Th2 lymphocytes, and other inflammatory cells). Food allergy can cause urticaria, angioedema, vomiting, diarrhea, asthma, and anaphylaxis. Allergic rhinitis causes sneezing, rhinorrhea, nasal congestion, and nasal itching. Allergic asthma causes wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, and coughing. Atopic dermatitis causes intense pruritus, xerosis, and eczematous lesions.

Allergy diagnosis is based on detailed clinical history, skin tests (prick test with allergens, intradermal test), specific serum IgE determination (ImmunoCAP), allergen provocation (bronchial provocation, nasal provocation, oral food challenge), and, in some cases, mediator determination (plasma histamine, tryptase). Food allergy is diagnosed through controlled oral food challenge, which is the gold standard, with skin tests or specific IgE as complementary tests. Differential diagnosis must exclude non-allergic causes (food intolerances, pharmacological effects, systemic diseases).

Allergy treatment includes allergen avoidance (essential for food and drug allergies), pharmacotherapy, and, in some cases, immunotherapy. Antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, desloratadine, fexofenadine) are first-line for urticaria, allergic rhinitis, and atopic dermatitis. Beta-2 agonists (salbutamol, formoterol) are first-line for allergic asthma, and inhaled corticosteroids (fluticasone, budesonide, beclometasone) are the control therapy for persistent asthma. Allergen immunotherapy (allergy vaccines) is the only therapy that modifies the natural history of allergy, inducing immunological tolerance. It is available for dust mite, pollen, animal hair, and insect venom allergy, and is administered subcutaneously (SCIT) or sublingually (SLIT). Epinephrine auto-injectors (EpiPen) are essential for patients at risk of anaphylaxis. Drug desensitization is used for penicillins and other drugs when no alternatives exist.

## Chapter 16: Advanced Infectious Diseases

### Viral Hepatitis

Viral hepatitises are inflammations of the liver caused by specific viruses, which can be acute or chronic and cause cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Hepatitis A (HAV) is transmitted by the fecal-oral route, usually through contaminated water or food, and causes a self-limited acute illness without chronicity. HAV vaccination is effective and recommended for travelers to endemic areas, children, and at-risk individuals. Hepatitis B (HBV) is transmitted through blood, sexual fluids, and mother-to-child, and causes acute infection that can progress to chronicity in 5-10% of adults (higher risk in children: 90% if infection occurs in the first year of life). Chronicity can cause cirrhosis (20-30% of chronically infected) and hepatocellular carcinoma (25% of chronically infected). The HBV vaccine is effective and recommended for all newborns and at-risk individuals. Antiviral treatment with entecavir or tenofovir suppresses viral replication and reduces the risk of complications.

Hepatitis C (HCV) is transmitted primarily through blood and causes chronicity in 75-85% of those infected. Chronicity causes cirrhosis in 10-20% of chronically infected individuals within 20 years and hepatocellular carcinoma in 1-4% annually of cirrhotic patients. HCV is the leading cause of liver transplantation in developed countries. Diagnosis is performed through anti-HCV detection (current or past infection) and confirmed with HCV RNA PCR (active infection). HCV genotyping is important for guiding treatment. Current treatments with direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) such as sofosbuvir/velpatasvir, glecaprevir/pibrentasvir, and daclatasvir/sofosbuvir achieve cure rates (SVR) above 95% in 8-12 weeks, with minimal side effects. Hepatitis D (HDV) only infects people with HBV and causes more severe liver disease. Hepatitis E (HEV) is transmitted by the fecal-oral route and is generally self-limited, but can cause severe illness in pregnant women and immunocompromised patients.

### HIV/AIDS

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) attacks the immune system, specifically CD4+ T lymphocytes, causing progressive immunodeficiency that, without treatment, progresses to acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), defined by the presence of an opportunistic infection or a CD4+ count < 200 cells/mm3. HIV is transmitted through blood, sexual fluids, and mother-to-child. Globally, approximately 39 million people live with HIV, and 1.3 million new infections occur per year. Treatment with combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) suppresses viral replication, allows immunological recovery, and transforms HIV from a fatal disease to a manageable chronic condition. cART regimens include nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs: tenofovir, emtricitabine, lamivudine, abacavir), non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs: efavirenz, rilpivirine, doravirine), protease inhibitors (PIs: darunavir, atazanavir, lopinavir), integrase inhibitors (INSTIs: dolutegravir, bictegravir, raltegravir, cabotegravir), and entry inhibitors (maraviroc, enfuvirtide, ibalizumab, fostemsavir). Dolutegravir is the WHO-recommended first-line INSTI.

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) with tenofovir/emtricitabine or injectable cabotegravir is effective for preventing HIV infection in high-risk individuals. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) with cART for 28 days after a risk exposure is effective if started within 72 hours. Prevention of mother-to-child transmission includes cART during pregnancy, elective cesarean section if viral load is > 1,000 copies/mL, and neonatal prophylaxis. The most common opportunistic infections include Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, cerebral toxoplasmosis, cryptococcosis, HIV encephalopathy, cytomegalovirus, tuberculosis, and Kaposi's sarcoma. Vaccination is important for preventing opportunistic infections (influenza, pneumococcus, hepatitis A and B, HPV, herpes zoster).

### Nosocomial Infections

Nosocomial infections or healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are infections acquired during a hospital stay that were not present or incubating at the time of admission. They affect 7-10% of hospitalized patients in developed countries and 10-30% in developing countries. The most common types include catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs, 30-40% of HAIs), surgical site infections (SSIs, 20-25%), ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP, 15-20%), catheter-associated bacteremia (CAB, 10-15%), and Clostridioides difficile (5-10%). Risk factors include invasive procedures (catheters, mechanical ventilation, surgery), prolonged hospital stay, antibiotic use, immunosuppression, advanced age, and comorbidities. Prevention includes hand hygiene, use of protective barriers, aseptic technique, catheter maintenance, antibiotic prophylaxis in surgery, early mobilization, and antibiotic de-escalation.

Antimicrobial resistance is one of the greatest threats to global public health. Multidrug-resistant pathogens include methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), carbapenem-producing Enterobacteriaceae (CPE), multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii. Resistance mechanisms include production of antibiotic-degrading enzymes (beta-lactamases, carbapenemases), modification of antibiotic targets, membrane permeability, and efflux pumps. The antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) strategy promotes rational antibiotic use to optimize clinical efficacy, minimize toxicity, and reduce resistance selection. Epidemiological surveillance of resistance, vaccination, development of new antibiotics, and research into therapeutic alternatives (bacteriophages, antimicrobial peptides, immunotherapy) are essential strategies.

## Chapter 17: Diagnostic Techniques

### Blood Tests

Blood tests are one of the most commonly used diagnostic tools in medicine, providing information about organ function, presence of infections, hematological disorders, cardiac and tumor markers, and levels of chemical substances in the body. The complete blood count (CBC) is the most common blood test and evaluates hemoglobin levels, hematocrit, red blood cell (erythrocyte) count, white blood cell (leukocyte) count, and platelet count. Normal values vary by age, sex, and laboratory, but general reference ranges include hemoglobin of 12-16 g/dL in women and 14-18 g/dL in men, white blood cell count of 4,000-11,000 cells/mm3, and platelet count of 150,000-400,000 cells/mm3. CBC abnormalities may indicate anemia (low hemoglobin), infection (leukocytosis with neutrophilia), leukemia (leukocytosis with blasts), thrombocytopenia (low platelets), or polycythemia (high hemoglobin).

Blood chemistry evaluates the function of multiple organs. Basic metabolic panels include glucose, urea, creatinine, electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate), calcium, and phosphorus. Hepatic panels include alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), total and fractionated bilirubin, and total proteins and albumin. Lipid panels include total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides. Cardiac markers include high-sensitivity troponin I or T (for myocardial infarction diagnosis), CK-MB (creatine kinase MB fraction), and BNP/NT-proBNP (for heart failure diagnosis). Inflammatory markers include C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and procalcitonin (for bacterial infections). Tumor markers include PSA (prostate), CA 125 (ovarian), CEA (colorectal), AFP (liver), and CA 19-9 (pancreas), but must be interpreted in clinical context and not as screening tests.

Coagulation tests include prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and fibrinogen. PT/INR evaluates the extrinsic and common coagulation pathways and is essential for monitoring warfarin therapy (target INR 2.0-3.0 for atrial fibrillation, 2.5-3.5 for mechanical valves). aPTT evaluates the intrinsic and common pathways and is prolonged in factor VIII, IX, XI, and XII deficiency, von Willebrand disease, and heparin. D-dimer is a fibrin degradation product that is elevated in deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and disseminated intravascular coagulation, but has low specificity in hospitalized patients. Routine laboratory tests are essential for diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment guidance in multiple clinical conditions.

### Imaging Tests

Imaging tests are fundamental for the diagnosis of multiple diseases, providing information about the anatomy, function, and pathology of organs and tissues. Plain radiography is the most common and cheapest imaging test and is essential for the diagnosis of fractures, pneumonia, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, intestinal obstruction, and pericardial effusion. CT (computed tomography) provides high-resolution cross-sectional images and is especially useful for the diagnosis of trauma, intracranial hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, pancreatitis, appendicitis, and cancer. CT with intravenous contrast enhances visualization of lesions and blood vessels but carries a risk of allergic reaction and contrast nephropathy. Low-dose CT is used for lung cancer screening in high-risk individuals.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the most sensitive imaging test for the diagnosis of central nervous system diseases (tumors, stroke, multiple sclerosis, disc herniation), musculoskeletal system diseases (ligament tears, meniscal injuries, tendinopathies, bone edema), and abdominal organ diseases (liver, pancreas, kidneys). MRI does not use ionizing radiation but has contraindications in patients with non-compatible pacemakers, metallic implants, severe claustrophobia, and first-trimester pregnancy. MRI with gadolinium contrast enhances lesion characterization, but gadolinium can cause nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with severe kidney disease (glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min).

Ultrasound uses sound waves to produce real-time images and is especially useful for diagnosing abdominal diseases (gallbladder, kidneys, pancreas, liver), cardiovascular system diseases (echocardiography to evaluate cardiac function and valvular disease, Doppler ultrasound to evaluate blood flow), thyroid, testicular, breast diseases, and during pregnancy. Ultrasound is portable, non-invasive, does not use radiation, and is relatively inexpensive, making it an ideal diagnostic tool in multiple clinical contexts. PET-CT (positron emission tomography combined with CT) is a functional imaging test that uses fluorine-18-labeled glucose (FDG) to detect areas of elevated glucose metabolism, being especially useful for diagnosis, staging, and treatment response monitoring in cancer, as well as for diagnosis of infections and inflammatory diseases.

### Biopsies

Biopsy is the gold standard for most cancers and many non-neoplastic diseases. It consists of obtaining and microscopically examining a tissue sample to evaluate its structure and cellular composition. Types of biopsy include core needle biopsy (CNB), which uses a large needle to obtain a tissue cylinder; fine-needle aspiration (FNA), which uses a thin needle to aspirate cells; excisional biopsy, which consists of complete surgical removal of a lesion; incisional biopsy, which consists of obtaining a representative portion of a large lesion; and endoscopic biopsy, which is performed during endoscopic procedures (bronchoscopy, colonoscopy, gastroscopy, laparoscopy). Biopsy may be image-guided (ultrasound, CT, MRI) to ensure representative sample acquisition, especially in deep or difficult-to-access lesions.

Histopathological examination of the biopsy includes formalin fixation, paraffin embedding, thin section cutting (4-5 micrometers), hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, and review by a pathologist. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) uses antibodies to detect specific proteins in cells, being essential for subtyping cancers (estrogen and progesterone receptors in breast cancer, HER2 in breast and gastric cancer, CK7 and CK20 in cancer of unknown origin, CD20 in lymphomas, CD3 in T-cell lymphomas, TTF-1 in lung cancer, PSA in prostate cancer). Molecular biopsy includes determination of genetic mutations (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS, HER2), microsatellite instability (MSI), and tumor mutational burden (TMB), which are essential for guiding targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Liquid biopsy is a non-invasive alternative that detects circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), and exosomes in blood, and is being used for early disease detection, minimal residual disease monitoring, and resistance detection.

## Chapter 18: Surgery and Surgical Procedures

### General Surgery

General surgery encompasses the diagnosis and treatment of diseases requiring surgical intervention, including abdominal, thoracic, vascular, endocrine, dermatological, and soft tissue pathology. The principles of general surgery include complete preoperative evaluation (clinical history, physical examination, laboratory tests, imaging studies, anesthesiological assessment), patient preparation (fasting, antibiotic prophylaxis, anticoagulation, comorbidity optimization), surgical technique (incision, exposure, hemostasis, dissection, resection or repair, closure), postoperative management (pain control, complication prevention, early mobilization, nutrition), and long-term follow-up. Types of general surgery include abdominal surgery (appendectomy, colectomy, hernia repair, splenectomy, nephrectomy), thoracic surgery (thoracotomy, thoracoscopy, lung resection), endocrine surgery (thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy, adrenalectomy, pancreatectomy), and soft tissue surgery (superficial tumor resection, hernia repair, obesity surgery).

Laparoscopic surgery is a minimally invasive surgical technique that uses small incisions (5-12 mm) and a video camera (laparoscope) to visualize intra-abdominal structures. Benefits of laparoscopic surgery compared to traditional open surgery include less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, better cosmetic outcome, lower risk of wound infection and incisional hernia. Indications for laparoscopic surgery include cholecystectomy (the most common laparoscopic procedure), appendectomy, hernioplasty, Nissen fundoplication, splenectomy, adrenalectomy, nephrectomy, colon resection, bariatric surgery, and numerous gynecological and urological procedures. Robot-assisted surgery (Da Vinci system) is an advanced form of laparoscopic surgery that provides improved three-dimensional vision, greater instrument precision and articulation, and elimination of surgeon tremor, enabling complex procedures with greater precision and less morbidity.

### Cardiac Surgery

Cardiac surgery is the surgical treatment of heart and great vessel diseases, including coronary artery disease, valvular disease, congenital heart disease, aortopathies, and arrhythmias. The most common procedures include coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), which uses internal mammary artery, saphenous vein, or radial artery grafts to restore blood flow to the heart muscle; surgical valve replacement or repair; aortic replacement for aneurysm or aortic dissection; congenital heart disease repair surgery; and surgical ablation of arrhythmias. Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) allows stopping the heart and diverting blood to a heart-lung machine while intracardiac procedures are performed. Off-pump surgery (without CPB) is performed without stopping the heart and may be preferable in high-risk patients.

Coronary artery bypass grafting remains the treatment of choice for patients with three-vessel coronary artery disease or left main coronary artery disease, especially in patients with diabetes mellitus or left ventricular dysfunction. Carotid endarterectomy is a surgical procedure that removes atheromatous plaques from the carotid arteries to prevent stroke. Aortic surgery includes open aortic replacement for abdominal and thoracic aortic aneurysm, and endovascular grafting (EVAR/TEVAR) is a minimally invasive alternative for selected patients. Advances in cardiac surgery include minimally invasive access surgery (mini-sternotomy, mini-thoracotomy), transcatheter valve repair (TAVR/TAVI, MitraClip), myxoma surgery, heart transplantation, and ventricular assist devices.

### Transplants

Transplantation is the transfer of an organ, tissue, or cells from a donor to a recipient to replace a lost organ function. Types of transplantation include solid organ transplantation (kidney, liver, heart, lung, pancreas, intestine), bone marrow transplantation (hematopoietic progenitors), corneal transplantation, skin transplantation, heart valve transplantation, and pancreatic islet cell transplantation. The donor may be living (kidney, portion of liver, portion of lung, bone marrow) or deceased (solid organs, cornea, tissues). Donor evaluation includes ABO and HLA compatibility testing, infection evaluation, organ function assessment, and informed consent. The preservation phase includes organ procurement, preservation in preservation solution (UW, HTK) at 4 degrees Celsius, and rapid transport to the transplant center.

The immune response to the transplant is the main cause of rejection and requires lifelong immunosuppression. Types of rejection include hyperacute rejection (minutes to hours, mediated by preformed antibodies), acute rejection (days to months, mediated by T cells and antibodies), chronic rejection (months to years, mediated by immunological and non-immunological mechanisms), and antibody-mediated rejection (AMR). Immunosuppression includes induction (lymphocyte-depleting antibodies: alemtuzumab, anti-thymocyte globulin), maintenance (calcineurin inhibitors: tacrolimus, cyclosporine; antimetabolites: mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine; corticosteroids: prednisone), and rejection treatment (methylprednisolone pulses, lymphocyte-depleting antibodies, plasmapheresis, immunoglobulin). Adverse effects of immunosuppression include increased risk of infections, increased risk of cancer (especially skin cancer and lymphomas), nephrotoxicity, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and osteoporosis. Transplant prognosis has improved significantly in recent decades, with five-year survival rates of 95% for kidney transplantation, 85% for liver, 75% for heart, and 60% for lung.

## Chapter 19: Medications and Pharmacology

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are drugs that inhibit the growth or destroy bacteria, being one of the most important medical interventions in the history of medicine. Antibiotics are classified by their mechanism of action: cell wall synthesis inhibitors (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams, vancomycin, daptomycin), protein synthesis inhibitors (macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, clindamycin, linezolid, tigecycline), nucleic acid synthesis inhibitors (quinolones, rifampin, metronidazole, nitrofurantoin), and metabolic inhibitors (sulfonamides, trimethoprim, dapsone). Antibiotic selection depends on the infected organ, predominant bacterial flora, local resistance patterns, drug safety profile, and patient allergies.

Rational antibiotic use is essential for preventing antimicrobial resistance. Antibiotic de-escalation is the strategy of switching from a broad-spectrum antibiotic to one with a narrower spectrum once the pathogen and its sensitivity are identified. Monitoring of aminoglycoside (gentamicin, tobramycin) and vancomycin levels is essential for optimizing efficacy and minimizing toxicity. Adverse effects of antibiotics include allergic reactions (rash, urticaria, anaphylaxis), organ toxicity (nephrotoxicity from aminoglycosides, ototoxicity from aminoglycosides and macrolides, hepatotoxicity from amoxicillin-clavulanate, neuropathy from metronidazole), suppression of normal flora (Clostridioides difficile infection), and drug interactions. Antibiotic prophylaxis in surgery is administered before the surgical incision to prevent surgical site infections, and antibiotic selection depends on the type of surgery and predominant pathogens.

### Analgesics and Anti-inflammatories

Analgesics are drugs that relieve pain and are classified as opioids (morphine, codeine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, fentanyl, methadone, tramadol, buprenorphine) and non-opioids (acetaminophen, NSAIDs, local anesthetics, antidepressants for neuropathic pain, anticonvulsants for neuropathic pain). Opioids act on the mu, kappa, and lambda receptors of the central nervous system, producing analgesia, euphoria, sedation, respiratory depression, constipation, nausea, and vomiting. Opioids are effective for severe acute pain and chronic non-cancer pain, but carry risks of tolerance, physical dependence, addiction, and fatal respiratory depression in overdose. The opioid crisis is a public health problem in multiple countries, and strategies to reduce opioid use include multimodal analgesia (combination of drugs with different mechanisms), extended-release opioids (for chronic pain), fentanyl patches (for severe chronic pain), and responsible prescribing programs.

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) inhibit cyclooxygenases (COX-1 and COX-2), reducing the production of prostaglandins and thromboxanes, which produces analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic effects. NSAIDs include aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, ketorolac, piroxicam, celecoxib, and etoricoxib. NSAIDs are effective for musculoskeletal pain, headache, menstrual pain, arthritis, and postoperative pain. Adverse effects of NSAIDs include gastrointestinal toxicity (gastritis, ulcer, gastrointestinal bleeding), renal toxicity (acute renal failure, fluid retention), cardiovascular toxicity (increased risk of myocardial infarction and stroke), platelet toxicity (inhibition of platelet aggregation), and hypersensitivity reactions (aspirin-exacerbated asthma). Selective COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib, etoricoxib) have lower gastrointestinal risk but higher cardiovascular risk.

### Psychopharmacology

Psychopharmacology is the use of drugs that act on the central nervous system to treat mental disorders, including antidepressants, anxiolytics, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, and stimulants. Antidepressants include SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine, citalopram), SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine, milnacipran), tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, clomipramine, imipramine, nortriptyline), MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline), bupropion, mirtazapine, trazodone, agomelatine, vortioxetine, and vilazodone. Anxiolytics include benzodiazepines (alprazolam, lorazepam, clonazepam, diazepam, midazolam), buspirone, pregabalin, and hydroxyzine. Mood stabilizers include lithium, valproate, lamotrigine, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, topiramate, and levetiracetam. Antipsychotics include typical (haloperidol, chlorpromazine, fluphenazine) and atypical (olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, paliperidone, ziprasidone, lurasidone, cariprazine, brexpiprazole, lumateperone). Stimulants include methylphenidate, amphetamines (lisdexamfetamine, amphetamine salts), and modafinil.

Pharmacovigilance is essential for detecting rare or late adverse effects of psychopharmacological drugs, and patients starting treatment with psychopharmacological drugs should be closely monitored, especially during the first weeks of treatment. Interactions between psychopharmacological drugs are frequent and can be serious, including serotonin syndrome (combination of SSRIs with MAOIs or triptans), QT prolongation (antipsychotics, tricyclic antidepressants), excessive sedation (combination of benzodiazepines with opioids or antipsychotics), and lithium toxicity (dehydration, renal failure, interactions with NSAIDs and diuretics). Discontinuation of psychopharmacological drugs should be gradual to avoid withdrawal symptoms, especially with SSRIs (discontinuation syndrome: dizziness, nausea, irritability, insomnia, paresthesias), benzodiazepines (insomnia, anxiety, seizures), and opioids (pain, diarrhea, chills, anxiety).

## Chapter 20: Rehabilitation and Quality of Life

### Physical Rehabilitation

Physical rehabilitation is a multidisciplinary therapeutic process that aims to improve physical function, reduce disability, and improve quality of life in people with functional limitations caused by diseases, trauma, or congenital conditions. Physical rehabilitation includes physiotherapy (strengthening, stretching, balance, coordination, cardiovascular endurance exercises, hydrotherapy, thermotherapy, electrotherapy), occupational therapy (activities of daily living training, environmental adaptation, assistive device use), speech therapy (language, swallowing, and communication rehabilitation), and rehabilitation psychology (emotional management of disability, motivation, psychological adaptation). Principles of rehabilitation include complete initial assessment, setting realistic and measurable goals, implementing an individualized treatment plan, continuous reassessment, and plan adjustment according to patient response.

Rehabilitation is used in multiple clinical conditions, including post-operative rehabilitation (after orthopedic, cardiac, neurological surgery), cardiac rehabilitation (after myocardial infarction, coronary bypass surgery, heart failure), neurological rehabilitation (after stroke, spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease), respiratory rehabilitation (COPD, cystic fibrosis, after thoracic surgery), oncological rehabilitation (during and after oncological treatment to reduce fatigue, improve function and quality of life), pediatric rehabilitation (cerebral palsy, developmental delay, genetic syndromes), and geriatric rehabilitation (fall prevention, strengthening, mobility). Evidence has shown that early and intensive rehabilitation improves functional outcomes, reduces hospital stay, prevents complications, and improves quality of life in multiple clinical conditions.

### Palliative Care and End-of-Life Care

Palliative care is comprehensive care for patients with serious and life-threatening illnesses, aimed at alleviating physical, psychological, social, and spiritual suffering, and improving quality of life for both the patient and their family. Palliative care can be provided at any stage of the disease, from diagnosis to end of life, and is compatible with curative treatment. Principles of palliative care include pain and other symptom relief, effective communication, shared decision-making, advance care planning, care coordination, psychosocial and spiritual support, and bereavement support. The most common symptoms in palliative patients include pain (present in 70-80% of patients with advanced cancer), dyspnea (50-70%), fatigue (60-80%), nausea and vomiting (30-50%), constipation (50-70%), anorexia (50-80%), insomnia (30-60%), anxiety (30-50%), and depression (25-50%).

Pain management in palliative care follows the WHO analgesic ladder: step 1 (non-opioid analgesics: acetaminophen, NSAIDs), step 2 (weak opioids: codeine, tramadol), step 3 (strong opioids: morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, fentanyl). Morphine is the reference opioid for moderate to severe pain, and the dose is adjusted according to clinical response without a maximum dose limit as long as adverse effects are managed (constipation with laxatives, nausea with antiemetics, sedation). Non-pharmacological pain treatments include relaxation techniques, physiotherapy, acupuncture, distraction, music therapy, and clinical hypnosis. End-of-life care includes the transition from curative to palliative care, end-of-life care planning (advance directives, health proxy designation), symptom management in the final hours or days, family support, and grief management. Euthanasia and assisted suicide are legally permitted in some countries (Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Canada, Colombia, New Zealand, parts of Australia, and US states) under strict eligibility conditions.

## Chapter 21: Pediatric Diseases

### Common Diseases in Children

Pediatric diseases differ from adult diseases in their presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, as children are not small adults. Acute respiratory infections are the most common reason for pediatric consultation and include upper respiratory tract infection (pharyngitis, tonsillitis, rhinopharyngitis, otitis media) and lower respiratory tract infection (bronchiolitis, pneumonia). Bronchiolitis, caused in 80% of cases by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), is the most common cause of hospitalization in infants under 6 months. Symptoms include wheezing, subcostal retractions, nasal flaring, drowsiness, and feeding difficulties. Treatment is supportive (nasal suctioning, secretion suctioning, oxygen if needed, hydration), and bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and routine antibiotics are not recommended. The RSV vaccine (nirsevimab) is available for high-risk infants.

Acute gastroenteritis is the second most common reason for pediatric consultation and the most common cause of diarrhea in children. The most common cause is rotavirus, followed by norovirus, enteric adenoviruses, and astrovirus. Bacterial causes include enteropathogenic Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, and Clostridioides difficile. Rotavirus diarrhea can be severe in infants and the elderly, causing severe dehydration and death in developing countries. The rotavirus vaccine is effective and recommended for all infants. Treatment of pediatric gastroenteritis is based on oral rehydration with oral rehydration solutions (ORS) containing sodium, potassium, chloride, citrate, and glucose. Intravenous rehydration is reserved for severe cases with significant dehydration or persistent vomiting that prevents oral rehydration. Probiotics (Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, Saccharomyces boulardii) may shorten the duration of diarrhea. Antimotility agents (loperamide) are contraindicated in children under 2 years of age.

Fever is one of the most common reasons for pediatric consultation. Fever in infants under 3 months requires urgent evaluation to rule out serious bacterial infection (sepsis, meningitis, urinary tract infection, pneumonia). The assessment of the febrile child includes evaluation of toxic appearance, presence of an identifiable infectious focus, and determination of the risk of serious bacterial infection. Risk factors include age < 3 months, temperature > 39 degrees Celsius, white blood cell count > 15,000/mm3, neutrophil count > 10,000/mm3, CRP > 20 mg/L, and procalcitonin > 0.5 ng/mL. Fever treatment includes acetaminophen (10-15 mg/kg/dose every 4-6 hours, maximum 60 mg/kg/day) and ibuprofen (5-10 mg/kg/dose every 6-8 hours, maximum 30 mg/kg/day). Aspirin is contraindicated in children under 16 years of age due to the risk of Reye syndrome. Evaluation of fever without a focus in infants under 3 months may require empiric hospitalization with intravenous antibiotics until bacterial infection is ruled out.

### Pediatric Nutrition

Adequate pediatric nutrition is essential for growth, development, and disease prevention. Exclusive breastfeeding is the ideal form of nutrition for infants during the first 6 months of life, and continued breastfeeding with introduction of complementary foods is recommended up to 24 months or beyond. Benefits of breastfeeding include lower risk of infections (respiratory, gastrointestinal, otitis media, meningitis), lower risk of chronic diseases in adulthood (obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cancer), better cognitive development, and stronger mother-child bonding. Infant formula is an alternative when breastfeeding is not possible or is contraindicated, and should be fortified with iron, folic acid, and vitamins. Introduction of complementary foods from 6 months of age should include foods of appropriate texture and nutritional value, including meats, fish, eggs, legumes, fruits, vegetables, and cereals, avoiding foods with choking risk (whole nuts, whole grapes, popcorn, hard raw carrots) and allergenic foods (peanuts, eggs, milk, wheat, soy, nuts, fish, shellfish), which can be introduced early (from 4-6 months) to prevent food allergies.

Pediatric malnutrition is a public health problem in developing countries, affecting approximately 149 million children under 5 years of age worldwide. Types of malnutrition include acute malnutrition (wasting and/or edema, BMI/Z < -2 standard deviations), chronic malnutrition (stunting, height/Z < -2 SD), and the combination of both. Risk factors include poverty, food insecurity, recurrent infections, inadequate breastfeeding, late or inadequate complementary feeding, and natural disasters. Treatment of severe acute malnutrition includes the stabilization phase (rehydration solutions, therapeutic feeding, electrolyte correction, infection treatment) and the recovery and rehabilitation phase (transitional therapeutic feeding, home feeding, psychosocial support). Prevention includes promoting breastfeeding, early introduction of appropriate complementary foods, vitamin A, zinc, iron supplementation, and zinc chewables for diarrhea, and school feeding programs.

## Chapter 22: Geriatric Diseases

### Dementia and Alzheimer's Disease

Dementia is a clinical syndrome characterized by acquired cognitive impairment that affects the ability to perform activities of daily living. Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60-70% of cases. AD is characterized by the accumulation of beta-amyloid plaques and tau protein tangles in the brain, which cause progressive neurodegeneration. Symptoms include episodic memory impairment (frequent forgetting, repeating questions, difficulty recalling recent events), language impairment (difficulty finding words, naming objects), visuospatial impairment (difficulty orienting, losing objects), executive function impairment (difficulty planning, organizing, making decisions), and behavioral changes (apathy, depression, agitation, psychosis, sleep disturbances). AD is classified into stages: preclinical (no symptoms, biomarker presence), mild cognitive impairment (mild symptoms without significant functional impairment), mild dementia (mild functional impairment, preserves autonomy), moderate dementia (significant functional impairment, requires assistance), and severe dementia (total dependence).

AD diagnosis is based on clinical evaluation (clinical history, cognitive status examination: Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE], Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA], neurocognitive tests), neuroimaging tests (CT or MRI to rule out reversible causes such as subdural hematomas, tumors, normal-pressure hydrocephalus, cerebrovascular disease; FDG PET-CT to evaluate cerebral metabolism; amyloid or tau PET to detect AD biomarkers), and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers (beta-amyloid 42/40 ratio, total tau protein, phosphorylated tau). Risk factors include advanced age, family history, APOE4 allele carriers, traumatic brain injury, depression, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, low educational level, and lack of physical and intellectual activity.

AD treatment includes non-pharmacological interventions (cognitive stimulation, regular physical activity, music therapy, pet therapy, daily living skills training, behavior management) and pharmacotherapy. Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) slightly improve cognitive and functional symptoms in mild to moderate stages but do not modify disease progression. Memantine (NMDA receptor antagonist) is effective in moderate to severe stages. Anti-beta-amyloid monoclonal antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab) are the first therapies that have demonstrated a modest but statistically significant reduction in cognitive decline in early AD in phase III clinical trials, representing a significant advance in AD treatment. Behavioral symptom management includes first-line non-pharmacological interventions and, if ineffective, pharmacotherapy (SSRIs for depression and anxiety, atypical antipsychotics for psychosis and severe agitation).

### Osteoporosis and Osteoarthritis

Osteoporosis is a metabolic bone disease characterized by reduced bone mineral density and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, which increases bone fragility and the risk of fractures. It affects approximately 200 million people worldwide, and one in three women and one in five men over 50 years of age will suffer an osteoporotic fracture. Risk factors include advanced age, female sex, early menopause, family history, low body weight, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, low calcium and vitamin D intake, prolonged corticosteroid use, and endocrine diseases (hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, Cushing's syndrome). Osteoporosis can be primary (postmenopausal or senile) or secondary to other diseases or medications.

Osteoporosis diagnosis is performed through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), which measures bone mineral density in the lumbar spine, hip, and femoral neck. Osteoporosis is diagnosed when the T-score is <= -2.5 standard deviations below the peak bone mass of young adults. Fracture risk factors are evaluated using the FRAX calculator, which incorporates bone density and clinical risk factors to predict 10-year fracture risk. Treatment includes calcium supplementation (1,000-1,200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800-2,000 IU/day), weight-bearing and resistance exercise, fall prevention, and pharmacotherapy. Medications include bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, zoledronic acid), denosumab (anti-RANKL monoclonal antibody), teriparatide (parathyroid hormone analog), romosozumab (anti-sclerostin monoclonal antibody), and raloxifene (selective estrogen receptor modulator).

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common joint disease, characterized by progressive degradation of articular cartilage, osteophyte formation, and low-grade synovial inflammation. It affects 10-15% of the adult population, and its prevalence increases with age, being the most common cause of disability in people over 65. The most frequently affected joints include knees, hips, hands (proximal and distal interphalangeal joints, trapeziometacarpal), spine (cervical and lumbar), and feet. Symptoms include mechanical joint pain (worsens with activity, improves with rest), morning stiffness (< 30 minutes), crepitus, limited range of motion, joint inflammation, and deformity. Risk factors include age, female sex, obesity, family history, joint trauma, ligamentous hyperlaxity, and heavy physical work.

OA diagnosis is based on clinical evaluation (clinical history, physical examination, X-rays showing joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cysts, and osteophytes). There is no cure for OA, and treatment focuses on symptom management and functional improvement. Non-pharmacological treatment includes patient education, weight loss (if overweight or obese), physical exercise (strengthening, aerobic, aquatic exercises), physiotherapy, assistive devices (canes, walkers, knee braces, insoles), and occupational therapy. Pharmacological treatment includes acetaminophen (first-line, but with limited efficacy), oral NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac) and topical (diclofenac, capsaicin), intra-articular corticosteroids (for acute flares), viscosupplementation with hyaluronic acid (for knee OA), and opioid analgesics (for severe pain resistant to other therapies). Surgery (hip or knee arthroplasty) is reserved for patients with severe disease that does not respond to medical treatment.

### Polypharmacy and Frailty

Polypharmacy is defined as the simultaneous use of five or more medications and is common in older adults, affecting 30-50% of ambulatory patients and 40-60% of hospitalized patients. Polypharmacy increases the risk of drug interactions, adverse effects, non-adherence, falls, hospitalizations, disability, and death. The medications most commonly involved in interactions include anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, opioids, hypoglycemics, antihypertensives, psychopharmacological drugs, and digoxin. Rational prescribing in the elderly should follow Beers criteria (potentially inappropriate medications in the elderly) and the STOPP/START criteria (Screening Tool of Older Person's Prescriptions). Medication review is a systematic process for evaluating the efficacy, safety, and necessity of each medication, with the goal of eliminating unnecessary medications, reducing doses, simplifying regimens, and preventing adverse effects.

Frailty is a clinical syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to external stressors, which increases the risk of disability, hospitalization, and death. It is defined as the presence of at least three of five criteria: unintentional weight loss (> 4.5 kg in the past year), self-reported exhaustion, muscle weakness (measured by grip strength), slow walking speed (> 6 seconds for 4.5 meters), and low physical activity. Frailty affects 10-15% of people over 65 and 25-50% of people over 85, and its prevalence increases with age, chronic disease, and poverty. Frailty is associated with metabolic syndrome, sarcopenia (loss of muscle mass and function), falls, fractures, disability, delirium, depression, and death. Frailty assessment in older adults should be part of comprehensive geriatric assessment, which includes functional evaluation (activities of daily living), cognitive (Mini-Mental), nutritional (Mini Nutritional Assessment), psychological (geriatric depression scale), social (social support, isolation), and medication review. Frailty treatment includes multidimensional interventions: physical exercise (resistance, balance, walking), nutrition (protein supplementation, vitamin D, caloric intake optimization), chronic disease management, medication review, cognitive stimulation, social interventions (combating isolation), and fall prevention (exercise, review of medications that cause dizziness, vision correction, elimination of home hazards).

## Chapter 23: Renal and Urological Diseases

### Chronic Kidney Disease

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is the progressive and irreversible loss of kidney function defined as the presence of structural or functional kidney abnormalities for more than 3 months, with health consequences. CKD is classified into five stages based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR): stage 1 (eGFR >= 90 mL/min/1.73 m2, with kidney damage), stage 2 (eGFR 60-89), stage 3a (eGFR 45-59), stage 3b (eGFR 30-44), stage 4 (eGFR 15-29), and stage 5 (eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 or dialysis). The most common causes of CKD include diabetes mellitus (40% of cases), arterial hypertension (30%), glomerulonephritis (10%), polycystic kidney disease (5%), and urinary tract obstruction. Risk factors include diabetes, hypertension, obesity, smoking, family history of kidney disease, advanced age, African American or Asian race, and autoimmune diseases. CKD affects 10-15% of the global population, and its prevalence increases with age (up to 40% in people over 70).

CKD symptoms are nonspecific and may be absent in early stages. Advanced symptoms include fatigue, weakness, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, peripheral edema, nocturnal dyspnea, pruritus, sleep disturbances, difficulty concentrating, and decreased visual acuity. CKD complications include anemia (from erythropoietin deficiency), renal osteodystrophy (from alterations in calcium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, and vitamin D), metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (the leading cause of death in CKD patients), uremic neuropathy, uremic pericarditis, and immunodeficiency. Diagnosis is based on eGFR determination (calculated from serum creatinine, age, sex, and race), urinalysis (measuring urine protein/creatinine ratio in 24-hour urine or morning urine proteinuria), renal ultrasound (to evaluate size, structure, and presence of obstruction), and renal biopsy (for etiological diagnosis in selected cases).

CKD treatment includes prevention and treatment of complications, reduction of disease progression, and preparation for renal replacement therapy. The most important intervention is strict blood pressure control (target < 130/80 mmHg), with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) as first-line, especially in patients with proteinuria. SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) have demonstrated significant renal benefits, reducing CKD progression and the risk of end-stage renal disease. Glucose control in diabetic patients (HbA1c < 7%), dietary modification (protein restriction to 0.8 g/kg/day in stages 3-5, sodium restriction to < 2 g/day, potassium and phosphorus restriction in advanced stages), smoking cessation, weight control, and regular physical exercise are fundamental interventions. Anemia treatment includes iron supplementation and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (epoetin alfa, darbepoetin alfa, methoxypolyethylene glycol-epoetin beta). Renal osteodystrophy treatment includes phosphorus restriction, phosphorus binders, calcitriol or vitamin D analog supplementation, and parathyroid hormone mimetics (cinacalcet).

Renal replacement therapy includes hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation. Hemodialysis is the most common type of dialysis, and consists of filtering blood through a semipermeable membrane to remove metabolic waste and excess fluids. It is typically performed three times per week, 3-4 hours per session, at a dialysis center or at home (home hemodialysis). Peritoneal dialysis uses the peritoneum as a filtration membrane, and is performed at home with manual exchanges (continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis) or with an automatic cycler (automated peritoneal dialysis). Kidney transplantation is the treatment of choice for end-stage CKD, providing better quality of life and survival than dialysis. The donor may be living (preferred) or deceased, and ABO and HLA compatibility is essential. Post-transplant immunosuppression includes calcineurin inhibitors, antimetabolites, and corticosteroids.
