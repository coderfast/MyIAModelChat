Christopher Columbus, born in Genoa (Italy) around 1451 and died in Valladolid (Spain) in 1506, was a navigator who believed he could reach Asia by sailing west across the Atlantic Ocean, an idea that was revolutionary for his time, as most Europeans believed the Atlantic Ocean was impassable and that Asia could only be reached by land or by sailing around Africa. Columbus convinced the Catholic Monarchs of Spain to finance his expedition, and on August 3, 1492, he departed from the port of Palos de la Frontera (Huelva) with three ships, the Santa Maria, the Pinta, and the Nia, and approximately ninety sailors. The diplomatic agreements between Portugal and Spain were fundamental in defining the spheres of influence of both kingdoms in world exploration. The Treaty of Alcagasovas, signed in 1479, ended the Castilian-Aragonese succession war and granted Portugal the monopoly of navigation and trade on the western coast of Africa, as well as the islands of Madeira, the Azores, Cape Verde, and Sao Tome. This treaty was a direct consequence of Columbus's discovery of America in 1492 and sought to prevent conflicts between the two Iberian powers. However, the Tordesillas line was extremely difficult to determine accurately at that time, as there were no precise tools for measuring longitude, which led to continuous disagreements over the ownership of territories such as Brazil, which was claimed by Portugal in 1500 when it fell east of the imaginary line.

The Mercator projection, created in 1569 by the Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator (1512-1594), revolutionized ocean navigation by allowing navigators to plot straight-line routes on the map while maintaining a constant direction (rhumb line). The Mercator projection is a cylindrical representation of the Earth's surface in which meridians and parallels are represented as perpendicular straight lines, which distorts areas near the poles but allows lines of constant bearing to be represented as straight lines on the map. This property was immensely practical for navigators, as they could draw a straight line between two points on the map and maintain a constant bearing using the compass, rather than having to calculate angles and distances precisely. The Mercator projection was published in 1569 in a map titled Nova et Aucta Orbis Terrae Descriptio, which showed the world with unprecedented precision and was used as a navigation map for more than three centuries. The main disadvantage of the Mercator projection is that it greatly distorts areas near the poles: Greenland, for example, appears the same size as Africa, although in reality it is fourteen times smaller. Despite this distortion, the Mercator projection remains one of the most widely used in modern cartography, particularly for navigation maps, internet maps, and thematic maps.

In France, mercantilism was expressed through Jean-Baptiste Colbert, minister of Louis XIV, who promoted the French manufacturing industry through state subsidies, protective tariffs, and the creation of royal manufactories. In England, mercantilism was expressed through the Navigation Act (1651), which prohibited English colonies from trading with foreign countries without the authorization of the Crown. Mercantilism was criticized by 18th-century economists, particularly by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776), who advocated free trade as the best way to promote economic prosperity.
