# The Legislative Process of the EU

## Chapter 1: Introduction to the Legislative Process

### 1.1 The Fundamental Principles of the Legislative Process

The legislative process of the European Union is the set of procedures through which the European institutions draft, debate, and adopt the legal acts that constitute the law of the Union. This process is governed by fundamental principles established in the Treaties, which include the primacy of EU law, respect for the fundamental principles of equal treatment, proportionality and subsidiarity, and the participation of citizens in decision-making through their elected representatives.

The principle of primacy of EU law implies that EU law takes precedence over the national law of the Member States in the areas it regulates. This principle ensures the uniformity and coherence of EU law in all Member States, and allows the European institutions to exercise their competences effectively. The primacy of EU law has been recognized by the Court of Justice in numerous rulings, such as the Costa v ENEL ruling of 1964.

The principle of equal treatment implies that all persons and entities that are subject to EU law must be treated equally, without discrimination on grounds of nationality, sex, race, religion, or other characteristics. This principle applies to all phases of the legislative process, from the proposal of legislation to its implementation. The principle of equal treatment ensures that European legislation is fair and equitable for all citizens and entities of the Union.

The principle of proportionality implies that the actions of the European institutions must not exceed what is necessary to achieve the objectives of the Treaties. This principle applies both to legislation and to the other actions of the institutions. The principle of proportionality ensures that European legislation is appropriate and necessary to achieve the pursued objectives, and that it does not impose disproportionate burdens on citizens and entities.

The principle of subsidiarity implies that the European Union should only act when and insofar as the objectives pursued cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, but can be better achieved at the Union level. This principle applies in the areas of shared competence, where both the Union and the Member States have competence to legislate. The principle of subsidiarity ensures that the European Union respects the competences of the Member States and that European legislation is limited to what is strictly necessary.

### 1.2 The Types of Legal Acts

The European Union adopts different types of legal acts, each with its own characteristics and adoption procedures. The most important legal acts are regulations, directives, decisions, recommendations, and opinions. Each type of legal act has a different scope of application and different legal effects, and is used for different purposes.

Regulations are legal acts of general application that are binding in their entirety and are directly applicable in all Member States. Regulations do not require transposition measures by the Member States, as they are directly applicable from the moment they enter into force. Regulations are used for matters that require uniform application in all Member States, such as the free movement of goods, competition, and the protection of personal data.

Directives are legal acts that bind the Member States as to the result to be achieved, but leave to the national authorities the choice of form and methods to achieve that result. Directives require transposition measures by the Member States, which must adapt their national legislation to achieve the result prescribed in the directive. Directives are used for matters in which a certain flexibility in implementation is permitted, such as labor law, consumer protection, and environmental policy.

Decisions are legal acts that are binding only on the persons to whom they are addressed. Decisions may be addressed to Member States, to natural or legal persons, or to other entities. Decisions are used for specific matters affecting specific addressees, such as the granting of State aid, the imposition of sanctions for failure to comply with EU law, and the resolution of disputes between Member States.

Recommendations and opinions are non-binding legal acts that do not create legal obligations for their addressees. Recommendations are acts by which an institution proposes a line of conduct without imposing it, while opinions are acts by which an institution expresses its view on a particular matter. Recommendations and opinions are used to guide the action of Member States and citizens, but they are not legally binding.

### 1.3 The Actors in the Legislative Process

The legislative process of the European Union involves different institutional actors that play specific roles in the drafting and adoption of legislation. The main actors are the European Commission, the European Parliament, the Council of the European Union, the European Council, and the Court of Justice of the European Union. Each of these actors has specific competences and responsibilities in the legislative process.

The European Commission is the body that has the right of legislative initiative in the European Union. Only the Commission can propose new European laws, and the European Parliament and the Council cannot adopt new laws without a prior proposal from the Commission. This right of initiative allows the Commission to control the legislative agenda and ensure that legislative proposals are consistent with the general policies of the Union.

The European Parliament is the legislative assembly of the European Union, composed of representatives elected by the citizens of the Member States. The European Parliament participates in the legislative process as co-legislator together with the Council of the European Union, and has powers of amendment, co-decision, and approval in the majority of legislative matters. The European Parliament also has powers of oversight over the Commission and the Council.

The Council of the European Union is the body that represents the governments of the Member States in the legislative process. The Council participates in the legislative process as co-legislator together with the European Parliament, and has powers of amendment, co-decision, and approval in the majority of legislative matters. The Council adopts decisions by qualified majority in most matters, but by unanimity in sensitive areas such as fiscal policy and foreign policy.

The European Council is composed of the heads of State or Government of the Member States and defines the general political guidelines of the Union. The European Council does not participate directly in the legislative process, but establishes the political priorities that guide the legislative action of the Commission, the European Parliament, and the Council. The European Council also drives the development of new policies and adopts decisions on strategic issues.

The Court of Justice of the European Union is the judicial body competent to interpret EU law and ensure its uniform application. The Court of Justice does not participate in the legislative process, but its case law has a significant impact on European legislation, as it interprets the provisions of the Treaties and of secondary legislation. The rulings of the Court of Justice are binding and constitute a source of law that complements legislation.

### 1.4 The Types of Legislative Procedures

The European Union uses different legislative procedures depending on the legislative matter and the provisions of the Treaties. The most important procedures are the co-decision procedure (ordinary legislative procedure), the consultation procedure, and the consent procedure. Each procedure has its own rules and participants, and is used for different types of matters.

The co-decision procedure is the ordinary legislative procedure of the European Union, in which the European Parliament and the Council share legislative power. In this procedure, the Commission submits a legislative proposal to the European Parliament and the Council, which examine and debate it. The European Parliament adopts its position at first reading, and the Council may accept the Parliament's position or propose amendments. If the Council accepts the Parliament's position, the law is adopted. If the Council proposes amendments, the text returns to Parliament for a second reading.

The consultation procedure is a procedure in which the Council consults the European Parliament before adopting a final position on a legislative proposal. In this procedure, the Council adopts a common position and submits it to the European Parliament, which may accept it, propose amendments, or reject it. If the Parliament accepts the Council's position, the law is adopted. If the Parliament proposes amendments or rejects the Council's position, the matter is referred to a conciliation committee to try to reach an agreement.

The consent procedure is a procedure in which the European Parliament must approve a legislative proposal before the Council can adopt it. In this procedure, the Commission submits a proposal to Parliament, which approves or rejects it. If Parliament approves the proposal, the Council may adopt it. If Parliament rejects the proposal, the Council cannot adopt it. This procedure is used in specific areas, such as common commercial policy and asylum policy.

### 1.5 The Role of the European Council in the Legislative Process

The European Council, composed of the heads of State or Government of the Member States, plays a fundamental role in the legislative process of the European Union, although it does not participate directly in the adoption of legislation. The European Council establishes the general political guidelines that guide the legislative action of the Commission, the European Parliament, and the Council, and defines the strategic priorities of the Union.

The European Council drives the development of new policies and adopts decisions on strategic issues that have a significant impact on the legislative agenda. For example, the European Council has adopted decisions on the creation of the Digital Single Market, the implementation of the European Green Deal, and the response to the COVID-19 pandemic. These decisions establish the political guidelines that the Commission must take into account when drafting legislative proposals.

The European Council also plays an important role in resolving deadlocks in the legislative process. When the European Parliament and the Council fail to reach an agreement on a legislative proposal, the European Council may intervene to drive a political compromise. The European Council may, for example, convene extraordinary summits to discuss pending legislative issues and seek political solutions.

The European Council also adopts decisions on the composition of the European institutions, such as the designation of the President of the European Commission and the members of the Commission. These decisions have a significant impact on the legislative agenda, as the Commission is responsible for proposing new European laws. The designation of a Commission President with specific political priorities can influence the direction of legislative action.

The role of the European Council in the legislative process is therefore a political and strategic role, which establishes the bases for the legislative action of the other institutions. The European Council does not participate directly in the adoption of legislation, but its political influence is fundamental to the functioning of the legislative process of the European Union.

## Chapter 2: The Commission's Proposal

### 2.1 The Right of Legislative Initiative

The right of legislative initiative is the exclusive power of the European Commission to propose new European laws. This right is established in Article 17 of the Treaty on European Union, which provides that the Commission has the right of initiative and that the Union shall exercise its legislative power in accordance with the ordinary legislative procedures. The right of legislative initiative is a fundamental power that allows the Commission to control the legislative agenda and ensure that legislative proposals are consistent with the general policies of the Union.

The Commission's right of legislative initiative has several exceptions provided for in the Treaties. In certain areas, such as the common foreign and security policy, the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy has the right of initiative together with the Commission. In other areas, such as cooperation in the field of justice and home affairs, the Member States also have the right to propose new laws. These exceptions are limited and apply only in specific matters.

The Commission's right of legislative initiative is a power that gives it great influence in the legislative process, as the Commission is the only institution that can propose new laws. This means that the European Parliament and the Council cannot adopt new laws without a prior proposal from the Commission. The right of legislative initiative allows the Commission to set the legislative agenda and ensure that legislative proposals are consistent with the general policies of the Union.

The Commission's right of legislative initiative is a power that must be exercised in a responsible and transparent manner. The Commission must ensure that its legislative proposals are appropriate, necessary, and proportionate, and that they take into account the needs and concerns of the Member States, citizens, and stakeholders. The Commission must also ensure that its legislative proposals are legally sound and can be effectively implemented.

### 2.2 The Process of Drafting Legislative Proposals

The process of drafting legislative proposals by the Commission is a complex process involving different stages and actors. This process begins with the identification of a legislative need, which can arise from different sources, such as the political priorities of the College of Commissioners, requests from the European Parliament or the Council, petitions from citizens, or the need to update existing legislation.

Once the legislative need has been identified, the Commission initiates a process of analysis and evaluation that includes data collection, impact assessment, and stakeholder consultation. Data collection includes obtaining data and studies on the matter to be legislated. Impact assessment evaluates the potential effects of the legislative proposal on the Member States, citizens, and businesses. Stakeholder consultation includes gathering opinions and comments from citizens, civil society organizations, businesses, and public authorities.

After completing the analysis and evaluation process, the Commission drafts a legislative proposal that includes the legal text and an explanatory memorandum. The draft is examined by the Commission's services and by the College of Commissioners, which may propose amendments and modifications. Once approved by the College of Commissioners, the proposal is submitted to the European Parliament and the Council for examination and approval.

The process of drafting legislative proposals is a rigorous and transparent process that ensures that proposals are appropriate, necessary, and proportionate. The Commission must ensure that its legislative proposals take into account the needs and concerns of the Member States, citizens, and stakeholders, and that they can be effectively implemented. The process of drafting legislative proposals is fundamental to the quality of European legislation.

### 2.3 The Explanatory Memorandum and the Impact Assessment

The explanatory memorandum is the document that accompanies every Commission legislative proposal and explains the reasons and objectives of the proposal. The explanatory memorandum includes a description of the current situation, the objectives of the proposal, the reasons why Union action is considered necessary, and an explanation of the means used to achieve the objectives. The explanatory memorandum is a fundamental document that allows the European Parliament and the Council to understand the context and objectives of the proposal.

The impact assessment is the document that evaluates the potential effects of the legislative proposal on the Member States, citizens, and businesses. The impact assessment includes an evaluation of the economic, social, environmental, and legal effects of the proposal. It also includes an evaluation of the costs and benefits of the proposal, and a comparison with possible alternatives. The impact assessment is a fundamental tool for ensuring that the proposal is appropriate, necessary, and proportionate.

The explanatory memorandum and the impact assessment are public documents that are available to citizens and stakeholders. These documents allow citizens to understand the objectives and effects of legislative proposals, and to participate in the legislative process in an informed manner. The Commission also publishes a summary of the impact assessment in a format accessible to citizens, in order to facilitate their understanding.

The explanatory memorandum and the impact assessment are fundamental tools for the quality of European legislation. These documents ensure that legislative proposals are appropriate, necessary, and proportionate, and that they take into account the needs and concerns of the Member States, citizens, and stakeholders. The Commission must ensure that its legislative proposals include a comprehensive and rigorous explanatory memorandum and impact assessment.

### 2.4 Stakeholder Consultation

Stakeholder consultation is a process by which the Commission gathers opinions and comments from citizens, civil society organizations, businesses, and public authorities before drafting a legislative proposal. The purpose of stakeholder consultation is to ensure that the legislative proposal takes into account the needs and concerns of all affected actors, and that it is appropriate and effective in achieving the pursued objectives.

The Commission uses different tools for stakeholder consultation, such as green papers, white papers, public consultations, and meetings with stakeholders. Green papers are documents that present a set of proposals and open a public debate on a particular issue. White papers are documents that present a set of concrete proposals and a strategy for implementing them. Public consultations are processes by which the Commission gathers opinions and comments via the Internet.

The Commission also holds meetings with stakeholders, such as civil society organizations, businesses, trade unions, and public authorities, to gather their opinions and comments on legislative proposals. These meetings may be bilateral or multilateral, and may be held at the national or European level. The Commission also establishes advisory committees that provide advice and opinions on specific areas of legislation.

Stakeholder consultation is a fundamental process for the quality of European legislation. This process ensures that legislative proposals take into account the needs and concerns of all affected actors, and that they are appropriate and effective in achieving the pursued objectives. The Commission must ensure that stakeholder consultation is transparent, inclusive, and representative of all actors affected by the proposal.

### 2.5 The Decision to Propose and its Publication

The decision to propose is the act by which the Commission decides to formally submit a legislative proposal to the European Parliament and the Council. This decision is taken by the College of Commissioners, which evaluates the necessity, appropriateness, and timeliness of the proposal. The College of Commissioners takes into account the opinions and comments gathered during the consultation process, the results of the impact assessment, and the political priorities of the Commission.

Once the decision to propose has been taken, the Commission drafts the final text of the proposal and publishes it on the Commission's portal. The proposal is published together with the explanatory memorandum, the impact assessment, and other relevant documents. The publication of the proposal allows citizens and stakeholders to access the information and participate in the legislative debate.

The proposal is officially submitted to the European Parliament and the Council for examination and approval. The European Parliament and the Council examine the proposal and debate it in their respective bodies. The Commission participates in the legislative process by defending its proposal and responding to questions from Parliament and the Council.

The decision to propose is a fundamental act of the legislative process, as it marks the formal beginning of the process of adopting the proposal. The Commission must ensure that its proposals are appropriate, necessary, and proportionate, and that they take into account the needs and concerns of the Member States, citizens, and stakeholders. The decision to propose is an exclusive power of the Commission that gives it great influence in the legislative process.

### 2.6 The Amendment of Legislative Proposals

The Commission may amend its legislative proposals at any time during the legislative process, both before and after their submission to the European Parliament and the Council. The amendment of legislative proposals is a tool that allows the Commission to adapt its proposals to the opinions and comments of Parliament, the Council, and stakeholders, and to ensure that the proposals are appropriate and effective in achieving the pursued objectives.

Before the formal submission of the proposal, the Commission may amend the draft proposal based on the opinions and comments gathered during the consultation process and the observations of the College of Commissioners. These amendments may include changes to the legal text, the explanatory memorandum, or the impact assessment. The Commission must ensure that the amendments are consistent with the objectives of the proposal and do not compromise its effectiveness.

After the formal submission of the proposal, the Commission may amend its proposal based on the amendments and comments of the European Parliament and the Council. The Commission may submit amendments at any time during the legislative process, both at first and second reading. These amendments may be a response to amendments by Parliament or the Council, or an adaptation to new circumstances or information.

The amendment of legislative proposals is a fundamental tool for the flexibility and effectiveness of the legislative process. This tool allows the Commission to adapt its proposals to the needs and concerns of affected actors, and to ensure that the proposals are appropriate and effective in achieving the pursued objectives. The Commission must ensure that the amendments are transparent and properly justified.

## Chapter 3: The Co-Decision Procedure (Ordinary Legislative Procedure)

### 3.1 First Reading in the European Parliament

First reading is the first phase of the co-decision procedure, in which the European Parliament examines and debates the Commission's legislative proposal. The procedure begins when the Commission submits its proposal to the European Parliament, which assigns it to the competent parliamentary committee for examination. The parliamentary committee examines the proposal and drafts a report that includes amendments and a position on the proposal.

The parliamentary committee's report is debated in the plenary session of the European Parliament, where MEPs debate the proposal and vote on the amendments. The European Parliament may approve the proposal without amendments, approve it with amendments, or reject it. If Parliament approves the proposal without amendments, the text is submitted to the Council for examination. If Parliament approves the proposal with amendments, the amended text is submitted to the Council for examination.

The European Parliament has a three-month period to examine and debate the proposal at first reading. This period may be extended by mutual agreement between Parliament and the Council. The European Parliament may request the opinion of the Court of Justice on the compatibility of the proposal with the Treaties, and may request additional information from the Commission.

First reading is a fundamental phase of the co-decision procedure, as it allows the European Parliament to exercise its legislative power and express its position on the Commission's proposal. The European Parliament may amend the proposal based on the needs and concerns of citizens, and may negotiate with the Council to reach a compromise. First reading is a democratic process that ensures that European legislation reflects the will of the citizens represented in Parliament.

### 3.2 The Council's Common Position

The common position is the position adopted by the Council of the European Union on the Commission's legislative proposal, after examining the European Parliament's position at first reading. The Council examines the proposal and Parliament's amendments, and adopts a common position that determines its stance on the proposal. The common position may be an acceptance of Parliament's position, a proposal of amendments, or a rejection of the proposal.

If the Council accepts the European Parliament's position in its entirety, the proposal is adopted as a Union legal act. In this case, no second reading procedure is necessary and the proposal enters into force. If the Council proposes amendments to Parliament's position, the amended text is submitted to Parliament for a second reading. If the Council rejects the proposal, the procedure begins anew with a new proposal from the Commission.

The Council has a three-month period to adopt its common position. This period may be extended by mutual agreement between the Council and the Parliament. The Council adopts its decisions by qualified majority in most matters, but by unanimity in sensitive areas such as fiscal policy and foreign policy. Voting in the Council is secret, unless the Council decides otherwise.

The Council's common position is a fundamental moment in the co-decision procedure, as it determines the Council's stance on the proposal and establishes the basis for negotiations with the European Parliament. If the Council proposes amendments, the European Parliament must decide whether to accept the amendments, propose additional amendments, or reject the Council's position. The Council's common position is a legal document that has binding effects in the legislative procedure.

### 3.3 The Conciliation Committee

The conciliation committee is a temporary body composed of representatives of the European Parliament and the Council, whose objective is to reach an agreement on a legislative text when Parliament and the Council fail to reach an agreement at second reading. The conciliation committee meets within a period of six weeks after Parliament has rejected the Council's position at second reading.

The conciliation committee meets in confidential sessions and negotiates a compromise text that is acceptable to both Parliament and the Council. The compromise text must be drafted by a representative of Parliament and a representative of the Council, and must be approved by a simple majority of the committee's members. If the committee reaches an agreement, the compromise text is submitted to Parliament and the Council for approval.

The European Parliament has a six-week period to vote on the compromise text in plenary session. Parliament may approve or reject the compromise text, but may not propose amendments. If Parliament approves the text, the Council has a four-week period to approve it as well. If the Council approves the text, the proposal is adopted as a Union legal act.

If Parliament or the Council reject the compromise text, or if the conciliation committee fails to reach an agreement, the proposal is considered not adopted. In this case, the Commission may submit a new proposal that takes into account the reasons for the failure of the negotiation. The conciliation committee is a fundamental mechanism for resolving deadlocks in the legislative process and for ensuring that European legislation is adopted effectively.

### 3.4 Second Reading

Second reading is the phase of the co-decision procedure in which the European Parliament examines the Council's common position and decides whether to accept it, propose amendments, or reject it. Second reading begins when the Council submits its common position to the European Parliament, which assigns it to the competent parliamentary committee for examination.

The European Parliament has a three-month period to examine and debate the Council's common position at second reading. Parliament may approve the common position without amendments, in which case the proposal is adopted as a Union legal act. Parliament may also propose amendments to the common position, in which case the amendments are submitted to the Council for examination. Parliament may also reject the common position, in which case the proposal is considered not adopted.

If Parliament proposes amendments, the Council has a three-month period to examine them and decide whether to accept them in their entirety, accept them partially, or reject them. If the Council accepts all of Parliament's amendments, the proposal is adopted as a Union legal act. If the Council accepts some but not all amendments, the amended text is submitted to the conciliation committee. If the Council rejects all amendments, the text is submitted to the conciliation committee.

Second reading is a fundamental phase of the co-decision procedure, as it allows the European Parliament and the Council to reach an agreement on the legislative text. If Parliament and the Council fail to reach an agreement at second reading, the matter is referred to the conciliation committee to try to reach a compromise. Second reading is a democratic process that ensures that European legislation reflects the will of both Parliament and the Council.

### 3.5 Political Agreement at Second Reading

Political agreement at second reading is the moment when the European Parliament and the Council reach an agreement on the legislative text without the need to resort to the conciliation committee. This agreement is reached when the Council accepts all of Parliament's amendments, or when Parliament accepts the Council's common position. Political agreement is a fundamental moment in the legislative process, as it allows the proposal to be adopted as a Union legal act.

Political agreement at second reading may be reached through negotiations between the representatives of Parliament and the Council. These negotiations may be held in different formats, such as informal meetings, bilateral negotiations, or negotiations through intermediaries. The negotiations are based on the principle of good faith and on the search for compromise solutions that are acceptable to both parties.

Once political agreement has been reached, the legislative text is formalized and submitted to the legal services of the institutions for legal verification. The legal services verify that the text is compatible with the Treaties and does not contain legal errors. If the legal services identify problems, the text is amended to correct them. Once the text has been verified, it is approved by the institutions and published in the Official Journal of the European Union.

Political agreement at second reading is a moment of satisfaction for the institutions, as it allows the adoption of legislation without the need to resort to conciliation mechanisms. Political agreement reflects the capacity of the institutions to work together and reach compromise solutions that benefit the citizens of the Union. Political agreement at second reading is an example of how the legislative process can function effectively when the institutions work in good faith and with the common objective of improving the lives of citizens.

## Chapter 4: The Conciliation Procedure

### 4.1 The Convening of the Conciliation Committee

The conciliation committee is convened when the European Parliament and the Council fail to reach an agreement on a legislative text at second reading. The convening of the committee is a decision taken jointly by Parliament and the Council, and must be carried out within a period of six weeks after second reading. The convening of the committee is a last-resort mechanism whose objective is to resolve deadlocks in the legislative process.

The conciliation committee is composed of representatives of the European Parliament and the Council, in equal numbers. The Parliament's representatives are appointed by the competent parliamentary committee, while the Council's representatives are appointed by the Council. The committee is jointly chaired by a representative of Parliament and a representative of the Council.

The conciliation committee meets in confidential sessions, which are not public, to ensure the smooth flow of negotiations. The committee's sessions are held at the European Parliament or the Council, depending on where the next plenary session of Parliament or the Council is to be held. The committee has a six-week period to reach an agreement, which may be extended by mutual agreement between Parliament and the Council.

The convening of the conciliation committee is a delicate moment in the legislative process, as it reflects the inability of the institutions to reach an agreement on their own. However, the conciliation committee is an effective mechanism for resolving deadlocks, as it allows the representatives of Parliament and the Council to negotiate directly and seek compromise solutions. The conciliation committee has proven to be an effective mechanism in the majority of cases, as the majority of texts submitted to conciliation are adopted after an agreement in the committee.

### 4.2 Negotiations in the Conciliation Committee

Negotiations in the conciliation committee are a complex process that requires flexibility, creativity, and good faith on the part of all parties. The objective of the negotiations is to reach a compromise text that is acceptable to both the European Parliament and the Council. The negotiations are based on the texts of Parliament and the Council, and seek to find solutions that reconcile the positions of both parties.

The negotiations in the committee focus on the fundamental points of disagreement that prevent agreement. These points may be of a legal, political, or technical nature. The representatives of Parliament and the Council negotiate point by point, seeking compromise solutions that satisfy the concerns of both parties. The negotiations may include mutual concessions, exchanges of support, and searches for alternative solutions.

The conciliation committee has a six-week period to reach an agreement. This period may be extended by mutual agreement between Parliament and the Council if the negotiations are progressing and it is considered that an agreement is possible. If the committee fails to reach an agreement within the period, the proposal is considered not adopted.

Negotiations in the conciliation committee are an example of how the legislative process can function effectively when the parties work in good faith and with the common objective of reaching an agreement. The conciliation committee has proven to be an effective mechanism for resolving deadlocks in the legislative process, and the majority of texts submitted to conciliation are adopted after an agreement in the committee. Negotiations in the committee are a democratic process that ensures that European legislation reflects the will of both Parliament and the Council.

### 4.3 The Compromise Text

The compromise text is the legislative text that results from negotiations in the conciliation committee. This text is drafted jointly by a representative of the European Parliament and a representative of the Council, and reflects the mutual concessions and compromise solutions reached during negotiations. The compromise text is a legal document that has the same force as the original texts of Parliament and the Council.

The compromise text must be approved by a simple majority of the members of the conciliation committee. If the text is approved, it is submitted to the European Parliament and the Council for final approval. If the text is not approved, the proposal is considered not adopted. The compromise text is a public document that is published on the Parliament and Council portals, and is available to citizens.

The European Parliament has a six-week period to vote on the compromise text in plenary session. Parliament may approve or reject the compromise text, but may not propose amendments. If Parliament approves the text, the Council has a four-week period to approve it as well. If the Council approves the text, the proposal is adopted as a Union legal act.

The compromise text is a fundamental moment in the legislative process, as it allows the proposal to be adopted as a Union legal act. The compromise text reflects the capacity of the institutions to work together and reach compromise solutions that benefit the citizens of the Union. The compromise text is an example of how the legislative process can function effectively when the institutions work in good faith and with the common objective of improving the lives of citizens.

### 4.4 Approval of the Compromise Text

Approval of the compromise text is the final phase of the conciliation procedure, in which the European Parliament and the Council vote on the text resulting from negotiations in the conciliation committee. This phase is crucial, as it determines whether the legislative proposal will be adopted or not as a Union legal act. The approval process has strict rules that ensure the transparency and legitimacy of the decision.

The European Parliament has a six-week period to vote on the compromise text in plenary session. Voting is by simple majority of the MEPs present. Parliament may approve or reject the compromise text, but may not propose amendments. This limitation is fundamental, as it ensures that the compromise text, the result of delicate negotiations, is not unilaterally modified by one of the parties. If Parliament approves the text, it is submitted to the Council for final approval.

The Council of the European Union has a four-week period to vote on the compromise text. Voting is by qualified majority in most matters. If the Council approves the text, the proposal is adopted as a Union legal act and published in the Official Journal of the European Union. If the Council rejects the text, the proposal is considered not adopted and the legislative procedure ends without result.

Approval of the compromise text is a moment of tension, as the failure of one of the parties to approve the text would mean the loss of all the work carried out during negotiations. However, experience shows that the majority of texts submitted to conciliation are approved by both parties, as the compromise text reflects a balance between the positions of Parliament and the Council. Approval of the compromise text is a testament to the capacity of the European institutions to reach agreements and legislate effectively.

### 4.5 Failure to Reach an Agreement and its Consequences

Failure to reach an agreement in the conciliation committee has significant consequences for the legislative process. If the conciliation committee fails to reach a compromise text within the established period, or if one of the parties rejects the compromise text, the legislative proposal is considered not adopted. This situation means that the legislation proposed by the Commission cannot be implemented, and that the objectives pursued with the proposal cannot be achieved through legislation.

Failure to reach an agreement may be due to different reasons, such as irreconcilable political differences between the European Parliament and the Council, pressure from interest groups that prevents mutual concessions, or the technical complexity of the matter that prevents finding compromise solutions. In some cases, failure to reach an agreement may be due to the lack of political will on the part of one of the parties to reach a compromise.

The consequences of failure to reach an agreement are multiple. First, the Commission must decide whether to submit a new proposal that takes into account the reasons for the failure of the negotiation. Second, citizens and stakeholders are left without the legislation they needed to resolve a particular problem. Third, failure to reach an agreement may weaken the credibility of the legislative process and of the European institutions.

However, failure to reach an agreement is not always negative. In some cases, failure to reach an agreement may be the result of a deliberate decision by Parliament or the Council to prevent the adoption of legislation that they consider inappropriate or harmful. In these cases, failure to reach an agreement is an expression of the democratic sovereignty of the institutions and a guarantee that European legislation is adopted only when there is sufficient consensus among the institutions.

## Chapter 5: The Consultation Procedure

### 5.1 The Council's Common Position in the Consultation Procedure

The consultation procedure is a legislative procedure in which the Council of the European Union adopts a common position on a legislative proposal and submits it to the European Parliament for examination. The consultation procedure is used in specific areas established in the Treaties, where Parliament has a consultative role rather than a co-decision role.

In the consultation procedure, the Council adopts a common position on the Commission's legislative proposal after consulting the European Parliament. The common position is the text that the Council considers appropriate to be adopted as a Union legal act. The Council submits the common position to the European Parliament, which has a three-month period to express its opinion on it.

The European Parliament may accept the Council's common position, in which case the proposal is adopted as a Union legal act. Parliament may also propose amendments to the common position, in which case the amendments are submitted to the Council for examination. Parliament may also reject the common position, in which case the proposal is considered not adopted.

The consultation procedure is a procedure that allows the Council to adopt legislation with the participation of the European Parliament, but without the need to reach an agreement between both parties. The consultation procedure is used in areas where the Council has a broader competence than Parliament, such as the common foreign and security policy, monetary policy, and cooperation in the field of justice and home affairs.

## Chapter 8: Legal Acts and their Effects

### 8.1 The Hierarchy of Norms in the European Union

The hierarchy of norms in the European Union is the order of priority between the different sources of EU law. This order of priority determines which is the superior norm that prevails over the lower norms in case of conflict. The hierarchy of norms in the European Union is composed of the constituent Treaties, secondary law (regulations, directives, and decisions), the general principles of law, and the case law of the Court of Justice.

The constituent Treaties are the supreme norm of the European Union and take precedence over all other sources of law. The Treaties establish the competences of the institutions, the objectives of the Union, and the fundamental rights of citizens. Any secondary law that is incompatible with the Treaties may be annulled by the Court of Justice.

Secondary law (regulations, directives, and decisions) takes precedence over the national law of the Member States in the areas it regulates. This principle of primacy ensures the uniformity and coherence of EU law in all Member States. Regulations take precedence over directives and decisions, as they are directly applicable and do not require transposition measures.

The general principles of law are implicit legal principles derived from the common constitutional traditions of the Member States and recognized by the Court of Justice. These principles include equal treatment, proportionality, legal certainty, and the protection of fundamental rights. The general principles of law take precedence over secondary law and are used by the Court of Justice to interpret and control the legality of institutional acts.

### 8.2 Entry into Force and Application of Regulations

Regulations are legal acts of general application that are binding in their entirety and are directly applicable in all Member States. Regulations enter into force on the date established in the regulation itself, which is generally twenty days after their publication in the Official Journal of the European Union. From the date of entry into force, regulations are directly applicable in all Member States without the need for transposition measures.

The direct application of regulations implies that citizens, businesses, and public authorities may invoke the provisions of regulations before national courts, and that national courts are obliged to apply the provisions of regulations in the cases brought before them. The direct application of regulations ensures the uniformity and coherence of EU law in all Member States, as regulations are applied in the same manner in all Member States.

Regulations may contain provisions establishing implementation measures by national authorities or European institutions. These implementation measures are necessary to ensure the effective implementation of regulations in specific areas. National authorities are obliged to adopt the necessary implementation measures and to ensure that regulations are effectively applied.

The application of regulations is subject to the control of the Court of Justice of the European Union. Citizens, businesses, and public authorities may challenge the application of regulations before national courts, which may request the Court of Justice to interpret the provisions of regulations. The Court of Justice ensures the uniform application of regulations in all Member States and resolves disputes that may arise from their application.

### 8.3 Transposition of Directives

Directives are legal acts that bind the Member States as to the result to be achieved, but leave to the national authorities the choice of form and methods to achieve that result. Transposition of directives is the process by which the Member States adopt the necessary measures to incorporate EU law into their national legislation and achieve the result prescribed in the directive.

The Member States have a determined period to transpose directives into their national legislation, which is generally two years from the publication of the directive in the Official Journal of the European Union. During this period, the Member States must draft and adopt the necessary legal, regulatory, and administrative provisions to achieve the result prescribed in the directive. The Member States must inform the Commission of the transposition measures adopted.

Transposition of directives poses significant challenges for the Member States, as they must adapt their national legislation to the requirements of the directives, which may involve significant modifications to their legal systems. The Member States have a certain flexibility to choose the form and methods to achieve the result prescribed in the directive, but must ensure that their transposition measures are appropriate and effective.

The European Commission has the responsibility to monitor that the Member States have correctly transposed directives into their national legislation. If a Member State has not transposed a directive or has done so inadequately, the Commission may initiate an infringement procedure against the Member State. The Court of Justice may impose financial sanctions on Member States that have not correctly transposed directives.

### 8.4 Application of Decisions

Decisions are legal acts that are binding only on the persons to whom they are addressed. Decisions may be addressed to Member States, to natural or legal persons, or to other entities. Application of decisions is a process that involves the execution of the obligations established in the decision by the addressees, and the control of their compliance by the competent authorities.

Decisions may impose specific obligations on their addressees, such as the granting of an authorization, the imposition of a sanction, or the adoption of specific measures. The addressees of decisions are obliged to comply with the obligations established in the decision within the established period. Failure to comply with the obligations established in a decision may lead to sanctions, including fines and other coercive measures.

European Union decisions are directly applicable in the Member States, which means that the Member States are obliged to apply them without the need for transposition measures. However, in some cases, decisions require implementation measures by national authorities to ensure their effective implementation. National authorities are obliged to adopt the necessary measures and to ensure that decisions are effectively applied.

Application of decisions is subject to the control of the Court of Justice of the European Union. The addressees of decisions may challenge them before the Court of Justice if they consider that there have been violations of the Treaties or of fundamental rights. The Court of Justice ensures the legality of decisions and resolves disputes that may arise from their application. Application of decisions is a fundamental aspect of the effectiveness of EU law and of the guarantee that institutional decisions are effectively executed.

### 8.5 Recommendations and Opinions

Recommendations and opinions are non-binding legal acts that do not create legal obligations for their addressees. Recommendations are acts by which an institution proposes a line of conduct without imposing it, while opinions are acts by which an institution expresses its view on a particular matter. These non-binding acts are important policy tools that allow the European institutions to guide the action of Member States and citizens without imposing legal obligations.

Recommendations are used by the European institutions to propose lines of conduct in areas where coordinated action is important but where binding legislation is not appropriate or necessary. For example, the Commission may recommend that Member States adopt specific measures to reduce unemployment, improve environmental protection, or promote innovation. Recommendations are not legally binding, but have significant political weight and may influence the action of Member States.

Opinions are used by the European institutions to express their views on specific issues. For example, the European Economic and Social Committee may issue opinions on Commission legislative proposals, and the Court of Justice may issue advisory opinions on the compatibility of international agreements with the Treaties. Opinions are not legally binding, but provide guidance and advice to the institutions and to the Member States.

Recommendations and opinions are important policy tools that allow the European institutions to influence the action of Member States and citizens without imposing legal obligations. These non-binding acts are flexible and can be adapted to changing needs and circumstances. However, recommendations and opinions cannot substitute for binding legislation, which is necessary to ensure the uniformity and coherence of EU law in all Member States.

## Chapter 9: Judicial Review

### 9.1 Infringement Proceedings

If the Court of Justice finds that there has been an infringement, it orders the Member State to take the necessary measures to fulfill its obligations. The Court of Justice may also impose financial sanctions on the Member State that fails to comply with its obligations.

Infringement proceedings are a fundamental mechanism for ensuring the uniform application of EU law in all Member States. Infringement proceedings are a testament to the importance of the rule of law in the European Union.

The Court of Justice examines the action and determines whether the contested act is compatible with the Treaties. If the Court of Justice finds that the act is incompatible with the Treaties, it annuls it in whole or in part. Annulment of the act has retroactive effects, which means that the act is considered as if it had never existed. However, the Court of Justice may decide that the effects of the annulled act are maintained for a limited period to avoid negative consequences.

Actions for annulment are a fundamental mechanism for ensuring the legality of acts of the European institutions and for protecting the rights of citizens and businesses. Actions for annulment are a testament to the importance of judicial review in the European Union.

Non-contractual liability actions are a judicial procedure by which the Court of Justice of the European Union determines whether the European Union must compensate for the damage caused by its institutions or its agents in the exercise of their functions. Non-contractual liability actions are a fundamental mechanism for ensuring the protection of the rights of citizens and businesses against illegal acts of the institutions. ### 9.4 Preliminary Rulings

Preliminary rulings are questions that the national courts of the Member States refer to the Court of Justice of the European Union for it to interpret or validate a provision of EU law. Preliminary rulings may be of two types: preliminary rulings on interpretation and preliminary rulings on validity. Preliminary rulings on validity have the objective of having the Court of Justice validate or annul an act of a Union institution that is challenged in litigation before a national court.

Preliminary rulings are a fundamental mechanism for ensuring the coherence and uniformity of EU law in all Member States. ### 11.1 Nature and Purpose of the Consultation Procedure

The consultation procedure is one of the oldest legislative procedures of the European Union and is used for the adoption of legal acts in which the Council requires the opinion of the European Parliament before adopting a decision. Unlike the co-decision procedure, in which the European Parliament acts as co-legislator together with the Council, in the consultation procedure Parliament's role is purely consultative, which means that its opinion is not binding on the Council. However, the Council is obliged to consult Parliament and to take into account its opinion before adopting the legal act, which gives Parliament significant influence in the legislative process.

The purpose of the consultation procedure is to ensure that the Council has the opinion of the European Parliament before adopting legislative decisions in specific areas. This procedure is used in matters in which the Treaties establish consultation as a mandatory requirement, such as certain areas of foreign policy, financial matters, and institutional issues. The consultation procedure reflects the intergovernmental nature of the Council, where the representatives of national governments maintain a predominant role in decision-making.

The consultation procedure has advantages and disadvantages. On the one hand, it allows the Council to act with greater speed and flexibility, as it is not bound by Parliament's opinion. On the other hand, it may generate tensions between the Council and Parliament when the Council decides not to follow Parliament's opinion, which may affect the democratic legitimacy of the decision. The consultation procedure is being progressively replaced by the co-decision procedure in the majority of legislative areas, as a consequence of the expansion of the European Parliament's competences.

### 11.2 Stages of the Consultation Procedure

The consultation procedure begins with the submission of a draft legal act by the European Commission to the Council, which in turn submits it to the European Parliament for its opinion. The European Parliament examines the proposal in its competent committees, which draft a report with their recommendations. The report is submitted to debate and vote in the Plenary of Parliament, which adopts a resolution with its opinion on the proposal. This opinion is submitted to the Council, which must take it into account before adopting the legal act.

The Council is not obliged to follow Parliament's opinion, but must justify its decision in case it decides not to follow it. The Council may adopt the legal act as proposed by the Commission, may modify it based on Parliament's recommendations, or may reject the proposal if it considers it inappropriate. The consultation procedure ends with the adoption of the legal act by the Council, which must be published in the Official Journal of the European Union.

The stages of the consultation procedure also include the possibility for the European Parliament to request additional information from the Commission or the Council during the examination of the proposal. Parliament may also request the appearance of members of the Commission or the Council to explain the objectives and content of the proposal. These information requests are an important tool for ensuring the transparency of the legislative process and the accountability of the institutions.

### 11.3 Areas of Application of the Consultation Procedure

The consultation procedure applies in specific areas established in the Treaties, which include certain matters of foreign and security policy, financial matters, and institutional issues. In the area of foreign and security policy, the consultation procedure is used for the adoption of decisions that define the Union's external action, that establish implementation measures for decisions in the field of foreign policy, or that adopt common Union positions in international organizations.

In the area of financial matters, the consultation procedure is used for the adoption of decisions on the Union's own resources system, on the multiannual financial framework, and on other financial matters affecting the budgets of the institutions. The consultation procedure also applies to institutional issues, such as the adoption of measures to ensure the proper functioning of the institutions and to improve the mechanisms for the protection of fundamental rights.

The scope of application of the consultation procedure is limited and is being progressively reduced as the co-decision procedure extends to new legislative areas. However, the consultation procedure remains relevant in the areas in which the Treaties establish it, and may regain importance in the event that changes to the Treaties broaden its scope of application.

## Chapter 12: The Consent Procedure

### 12.1 Nature and Characteristics of the Consent Procedure

The consent procedure is a legislative procedure in which the European Parliament has the power to approve or reject a legal act proposed by the European Commission, without the Council having the possibility to modify Parliament's decision. This procedure is used in specific areas in which the Treaties grant Parliament an absolute decision-making power, such as the approval of the Union Budget, the designation of certain offices, and the approval of international agreements.

The main characteristic of the consent procedure is that the European Parliament acts as the sole decision-making authority, which means that its decision is final and cannot be modified by other institutions. This gives Parliament significant power in the areas in which the procedure applies, as it may reject proposals that do not align with its preferences or that do not meet its requirements. The consent procedure is an expression of the democratic sovereignty of Parliament as the representative of European citizens.

The consent procedure differs from the co-decision procedure in that in the latter the Council and Parliament share decision-making power, while in the consent procedure Parliament has exclusive decision-making power. This difference reflects the strategic importance of the areas in which the consent procedure applies, where Parliament has a direct interest in ensuring that its preferences are respected.

### 12.2 Stages of the Consent Procedure

The consent procedure begins with the submission of a proposal by the European Commission to the European Parliament. Parliament examines the proposal in its competent committees, which draft a report with their recommendations. The report is submitted to debate and vote in the Plenary of Parliament, which adopts a resolution approving or rejecting the proposal. In case of approval, the legal act enters into force without the need for additional approval by the Council. In case of rejection, the proposal is returned to the Commission, which may modify it and resubmit it.

The European Parliament has a determined period to examine and vote on the proposal, which varies depending on the type of legal act and the provisions of the Treaties. If Parliament does not express itself within the established period, the proposal is considered tacitly approved. This provision has the objective of ensuring that the consent procedure proceeds within reasonable timeframes and that legislative decisions are not unnecessarily delayed.

The consent procedure also includes the possibility for the European Parliament to request additional information from the Commission or to negotiate with it the conditions of approval. These negotiations may result in modifications to the original proposal, which are incorporated into the final legal act. The consent procedure ends with the publication of the legal act in the Official Journal of the European Union.

### 12.3 Areas of Application of the Consent Procedure

The consent procedure applies in specific areas established in the Treaties, which include the approval of the Union Budget, the designation of certain institutional offices, and the approval of international agreements. In the budgetary area, the consent procedure ensures that the European Parliament, as budgetary authority together with the Council, has a decisive role in the approval of the Union's annual Budget.

In the area of designation of offices, the consent procedure is used for the designation of certain institutional offices, such as the President of the European Central Bank, the members of the Executive Board of the European Central Bank, and other offices that require Parliament's approval. This procedure ensures that institutional offices are designated with the support of Parliament, as the representative of European citizens.

In the area of international agreements, the consent procedure is used for the approval of certain international agreements that the Union concludes with third countries or with international organizations. Parliament's approval is a prerequisite for the entry into force of these agreements, which ensures that European citizens are represented in the conclusion of agreements that affect their interests.

## Chapter 13: The Role of the European Commission

### 13.1 The Monopoly of Legislative Initiative

The European Commission is the only institution of the European Union that has the monopoly of legislative initiative, which means that only the Commission can propose new European laws. This monopoly is enshrined in the Treaties and has the objective of ensuring that legislative proposals are drafted objectively, taking into account the general interest of the Union and not the particular interests of the Member States or of pressure groups. The Commission acts as "Guardian of the Treaties" and as the motor of the legislative process, ensuring that European legislation is coherent, effective, and compatible with the objectives of the Union.

The monopoly of legislative initiative gives the Commission a predominant role in the legislative process, as it is the institution that defines the legislative priorities and determines which issues should be regulated at the European level. The Commission submits its legislative proposals to the European Parliament and the Council, which examine and modify them if necessary before adopting them. This monopoly has been criticized by some as a limitation on the democratic nature of the legislative process, as it reduces the capacity of Parliament and the Council to propose new laws. However, the Treaties provide for exceptions to this monopoly in certain areas, such as foreign policy and defense.

The Commission uses different sources of information to draft its legislative proposals, including impact studies, public consultations, expert opinions, and stakeholder feedback. These information-gathering processes allow the Commission to evaluate the necessity and timeliness of a legislative proposal, and ensure that the proposal is proportionate, effective, and compatible with existing law. The Commission may also submit proposals at the request of the European Parliament or the Council, or in response to a Parliament resolution calling on it to exercise its legislative initiative.

### 13.2 The Process of Drafting Proposals

The process of drafting legislative proposals in the Commission is a complex process involving multiple services and stages. The process begins with the identification of a regulatory need, which can arise from different sources, such as the evaluation of existing policies, requests from other political bodies, the need to adapt legislation to new circumstances, or the transposition of international commitments. Once the regulatory need has been identified, the Commission initiates an information-gathering process that includes impact studies, public consultations, and legal analysis.

Impact studies are documents that evaluate the economic, social, environmental, and other consequences of a legislative proposal. Impact studies include an analysis of the problems the proposal aims to solve, an examination of the available policy options, an evaluation of the impacts of each option, and a comparison of the options based on their effectiveness, efficiency, and coherence. Impact studies are important tools for ensuring that legislative proposals are proportionate and that costs do not exceed expected benefits.

Public consultations are processes by which the Commission receives the opinion of citizens, businesses, civil society organizations, and other stakeholders on a legislative proposal. Public consultations may be conducted through online questionnaires, public meetings, seminars, or other mechanisms. The Commission has the obligation to take into account the opinions received in public consultations and to explain in the explanatory memorandum of the proposal how those opinions have been taken into account.

### 13.3 The Political Role of the Commission

In addition to its legislative role, the European Commission plays an important political role in the legislative process, as it defines the political priorities of the Union and coordinates the action of the institutions in the legislative area. The President of the Commission and the Commissioners play a prominent political role in promoting legislative proposals and in negotiating with the European Parliament and the Council to reach agreements on their content.

The Commission acts as mediator between the European Parliament and the Council during the legislative process, facilitating negotiations and seeking compromise solutions that satisfy both institutions. This mediation function is especially important in the co-decision procedure, where Parliament and the Council must reach an agreement on the final text of the legislative proposal. The Commission may propose compromise solutions and facilitate negotiations to reach an agreement at first or second reading.

The political role of the Commission also includes the defense of the legislative proposal before the European Parliament and the Council, explaining the objectives, content, and expected benefits of the proposal. The Commissioners may participate in Parliament and Council debates to explain the reasons for the proposal and to respond to questions and concerns of citizens' representatives and national governments.

## Chapter 14: The Co-Decision Process in Detail

### 14.1 Parliament's First Reading

Parliament's first reading is the first phase of the co-decision procedure, in which Parliament examines the Commission's legislative proposal and adopts a common position on it. The process begins when the Commission submits its proposal to the European Parliament, which refers it to the competent committee for examination. The competent committee drafts a report that includes a recommendation on Parliament's stance on the proposal, which may include amendments to the original text.

The committee's report is submitted to debate and vote in the Plenary of Parliament, which adopts a resolution with its common position on the proposal. Parliament's common position may be identical to the Commission's original proposal, may include modifications to the text, or may reject the proposal entirely. In case of approval, Parliament's common position is submitted to the Council for examination.

Parliament's first reading is an important phase of the co-decision process, as it defines Parliament's initial position on the proposal and establishes the basis for negotiations with the Council. Parliament uses the first reading to express its preferences on the content of the proposal, to propose modifications to the text, and to lay the groundwork for its negotiation strategy with the Council.

### 14.2 The Council's Common Position

The Council's common position is the second phase of the co-decision procedure, in which the Council examines Parliament's common position and adopts its own position on the proposal. The Council may accept Parliament's position without modifications, may propose amendments to Parliament's position, or may reject Parliament's position and maintain its own stance. The Council's common position is submitted back to the European Parliament for examination.

The Council adopts its common position by qualified majority, which requires the favorable vote of at least 55% of the Member States representing at least 65% of the Union's population. The Council may include in its common position amendments to Parliament's position, which may be substantive or formal. Substantive amendments modify the text of the proposal, while formal amendments correct errors, improve clarity, or adapt the text to formal requirements.

The Council's common position is an important document that reflects the stance of national governments on the legislative proposal. The Council takes into account the opinions of the Member States, the recommendations of preparatory committees, and stakeholder feedback when adopting its common position. The Council's common position is the basis for negotiations with the European Parliament in the conciliation phase.

### 14.3 Parliament's Second Reading

Parliament's second reading is the third phase of the co-decision procedure, in which Parliament examines the Council's common position and decides whether to accept it, propose amendments, or reject it. If Parliament accepts the Council's position without modifications, the legislative proposal is considered adopted. If Parliament proposes amendments to the Council's position, these amendments are submitted to the Council for examination. If Parliament rejects the Council's position, the conciliation procedure is activated.

The European Parliament examines the Council's common position in the competent committee, which drafts a report with its recommendations. The report is submitted to debate and vote in the Plenary of Parliament, which adopts a resolution with its position on the Council's position. Parliament may approve amendments to the Council's position, which are submitted to the Council for examination. The Council has a determined period to examine Parliament's amendments and decide whether to accept or reject them.

Second reading is a critical phase of the co-decision procedure, as it determines whether the institutions can reach an agreement without the need to resort to conciliation. If the Council accepts all of Parliament's amendments, the legislative proposal is considered adopted. If the Council rejects some or all of Parliament's amendments, the conciliation procedure is activated to try to reach an agreement on a common text.

### 14.4 The Conciliation Procedure

The conciliation procedure is the fourth phase of the co-decision procedure, which is activated when the European Parliament and the Council fail to reach an agreement on the text of the legislative proposal at second reading. The conciliation procedure has the objective of facilitating an agreement between both institutions on a common text that can be adopted. The conciliation procedure is carried out within the framework of a conciliation committee, composed of representatives of Parliament and the Council, which meets for a determined period to negotiate the common text.

The conciliation committee has a six-week period to reach an agreement on a common text. During this period, the representatives of Parliament and the Council negotiate the remaining differences and seek compromise solutions that satisfy both parties. The conciliation committee may hold multiple sessions and may request the assistance of the European Commission to facilitate negotiations.

If the conciliation committee reaches an agreement on a common text, this text is submitted to a second vote in the European Parliament and in the Council for final approval. Parliament votes by simple majority, while the Council votes by qualified majority. If both institutions approve the common text, the legislative proposal is considered adopted. If one of the institutions rejects the common text, the legislative proposal is considered rejected and cannot be resubmitted within the following twelve months.

## Chapter 15: European Parliament Committees

### 15.1 Committee Functions

The committees of the European Parliament are internal bodies that play a fundamental role in the legislative process, as they are responsible for examining legislative proposals, drafting reports, and formulating recommendations for the Plenary of Parliament. Each committee specializes in a specific thematic area, such as agriculture, the environment, human rights, the economy, or industry, and has members with experience and knowledge in that area. Committees are the engine of Parliament's legislative work, as they are where legislative proposals are analyzed in detail and amendments are drafted that are submitted to vote in the Plenary.

The functions of committees include examining legislative proposals submitted by the European Commission, drafting reports with recommendations on Parliament's stance, organizing public hearings with experts and stakeholders, drafting own-initiative reports on issues relevant to their area of specialization, and monitoring the implementation of European legislation. Committees also participate in interparliamentary cooperation with national parliaments and in exchanges with other parliaments.

The committees of the European Parliament are fundamental to the quality of the legislative process, as they allow detailed and expert analysis of legislative proposals. Committee members have specialized knowledge in their areas of responsibility, which allows them to evaluate the implications of legislative proposals and propose well-founded amendments. Committees also provide a forum for debate and negotiation between the different political groups, which facilitates the search for consensus and the building of legislative majorities.

### 15.2 Committee Organization

The European Parliament has twenty standing committees and two subcommittees, each of which specializes in a specific thematic area. The standing committees include the Committee on Economic and Monetary Affairs, the Committee on Foreign Affairs, the Committee on Human Rights, the Committee on the Environment, Public Health and Food Safety, the Committee on Industry, Research and Energy, the Committee on the Internal Market and Consumer Protection, the Committee on Transport and Tourism, the Committee on Regional Development, the Committee on Employment and Social Affairs, the Committee on Women's Rights and Gender Equality, the Committee on Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs, the Committee on Budgets, the Committee on Budgetary Control, the Committee on Legal Affairs, the Committee on Constitutional Affairs, the Committee on Culture and Education, the Committee on Petitions, and the Committee on Economic and Monetary Affairs.

Each committee is composed of a determined number of members, which varies depending on the importance of the thematic area and the total number of MEPs. Committee members are designated by the political groups, which distribute the seats based on their representation in the Plenary. Committees elect their president and vice-presidents, who direct the work of the committee and represent the committee before the other institutions.

Committees normally meet once a month during the European Parliament's plenary weeks, and may hold extraordinary meetings when the need arises. Committee meetings are public and may be followed in real time via the Internet, which ensures the transparency of their work. Committees may also hold public hearings, in which experts, civil society representatives, and other stakeholders may present their opinions on issues relevant to the committee's area.

### 15.3 The Report Drafting Process

The report drafting process is the central process of the work of European Parliament committees, and consists of preparing documents that contain the committee's recommendations on a legislative proposal or on another relevant issue. The report drafting process begins with the appointment of a rapporteur, who is the committee member responsible for preparing the report. The rapporteur is designated by the committee president based on the preferences of the political groups and the member's experience in the thematic area.

The rapporteur drafts a report that includes an analysis of the proposal, a series of amendments, and a recommendation on Parliament's stance. The draft report is submitted to the committee for examination, and other committee members may propose amendments to the draft. The committee examines the draft report in one or several meetings, and approves the amendments by simple majority. The final report is submitted to vote in the Plenary of Parliament, where MEPs approve the amendments by simple majority.

The report drafting process is a democratic process that ensures that Parliament's stance on a legislative proposal is the result of an open and transparent debate between committee members and the Plenary. The rapporteur plays a fundamental role in this process, as they are responsible for coordinating the work of the committee, presenting the report to the Plenary, and defending the committee's stance before the other MEPs.

## Chapter 16: Citizen Participation

### 16.1 Citizens' Rights of Participation

Citizens of the European Union have the right to participate in the legislative process through different mechanisms established in the Treaties. These rights include the right to submit a European citizens' initiative, the right to consult legislative documents, the right to attend Council meetings in which legislative acts are discussed, and the right to address the European Parliament, the Council, and the Commission. These rights have the objective of strengthening the democratic nature of the legislative process and bringing the institutions closer to citizens.

The European citizens' initiative is a mechanism that allows citizens to propose new European laws to the European Commission. To exercise this right, a minimum of one million citizens from at least seven Member States must sign an initiative proposing a measure in an area in which the Commission has competence to submit legislative proposals. The Commission has the obligation to examine the initiative and to decide whether or not to submit a legislative proposal. The European citizens' initiative is an important mechanism of participatory democracy that allows citizens to directly influence the legislative agenda of the Union.

Citizens also have the right to consult legislative documents, including legislative proposals, amendments, reports, and opinions. All legislative documents are published on the institutions' Internet portals and can be downloaded free of charge. Citizens also have the right to attend Council meetings in which legislative acts are discussed, unless the Council decides otherwise for reasons of security or public interest.

### 16.2 Public Consultations

Public consultations are processes by which the European Commission receives the opinion of citizens, businesses, civil society organizations, and other stakeholders on legislative proposals or on public policy issues. Public consultations may be conducted through online questionnaires, public meetings, seminars, or other mechanisms. The Commission has the obligation to publish the results of public consultations and to explain in the explanatory memorandum of the proposal how the opinions received have been taken into account.

Public consultations are an important tool for ensuring that the legislative process is transparent and that legislative proposals take into account the opinions and concerns of stakeholders. The Commission regularly organizes public consultations on different policy areas, including the environment, health, the economy, justice, and education. Citizens and stakeholders may participate in public consultations via the Internet, which facilitates the participation of a large number of people.

Public consultations may significantly influence the content of legislative proposals, as the Commission has the obligation to take into account the opinions received and to explain how they have been taken into account. Public consultations also provide the Commission with valuable information about the needs and concerns of citizens and stakeholders, which allows it to draft more appropriate and effective legislative proposals.

### 16.3 Public Hearings

Public hearings are events organized by European Parliament committees in which experts, civil society representatives, and other stakeholders may present their opinions on legislative proposals or on public policy issues. Public hearings are an important mechanism of citizen participation, as they allow citizens to express their opinions directly before MEPs and to participate in the legislative debate.

Public hearings are generally organized in the initial phases of the legislative process, when the competent committee is examining a legislative proposal. Speakers invited to public hearings include academic experts, civil society organization representatives, business representatives, national government representatives, and other relevant actors. Public hearings are public and may be followed in real time via the Internet, which ensures the transparency of Parliament's work.

Public hearings provide MEPs with valuable information about different perspectives on a legislative proposal, which allows them to make more informed decisions. Public hearings also provide citizens with the opportunity to participate directly in the legislative process and to make their voices heard before the European institutions.

## Chapter 17: The Co-Evolution of European Law and National Law

### 17.1 The Relationship between European Law and National Law

The relationship between European law and national law is one of the most complex and fundamental issues of EU law. This relationship is governed by principles such as the primacy of European law, direct effect, and sincere cooperation, which determine how the two legal orders interact. The primacy of European law implies that, in the areas in which the Union has competence, European law takes precedence over national law, which means that the Member States cannot adopt norms that contradict European law.

Direct effect is a principle that allows citizens to directly invoke European law before national courts, without the need for national law to have implemented the European provisions. This principle ensures that citizens can effectively benefit from their European rights, and that the Member States comply with their European obligations. Sincere cooperation is a principle that obliges the Union institutions and the Member States to cooperate with each other to ensure compliance with EU law.

The co-evolution of European law and national law is a dynamic process in which both legal orders mutually influence each other. European law influences national law through the transposition of directives, the application of regulations, and the interpretation of national law in light of European law. National law influences European law through the participation of the Member States in the legislative process, the case law of national courts, and the experiences of implementation of European legislation.

### 17.2 Transposition of Directives

Transposition of directives is the process by which the Member States adapt their internal legislation to achieve the result prescribed in a European directive. Directives bind the Member States as to the result to be achieved, but leave to the national authorities the choice of form and methods to achieve that result. Transposition of directives is an obligation of the Member States, which must adopt the necessary measures to incorporate European law into their internal legislation within the periods established in the directive.

The process of transposition of directives includes the drafting of bills or regulations that implement the provisions of the directive, the approval of these bills by the national legislative bodies, and the publication of the transposed norms in the national official journals. The Member States must also notify the European Commission of the transposition measures adopted, so that the Commission can verify that the transposition has been correctly carried out.

Failure to comply with the obligation of transposition of directives may lead to infringement proceedings by the European Commission, which may result in the condemnation of the Member State by the Court of Justice and the imposition of financial sanctions. Transposition of directives is a complex process that requires coordination between different levels of government and between different policy areas.

### 17.3 Judicial Cooperation

Judicial cooperation is a mechanism that facilitates the coherent application of European law in all the Member States. This cooperation manifests itself in different forms, including preliminary rulings, judicial dialogue, and cooperation in the field of enforcement of judicial decisions. The mechanism of preliminary rulings is one of the most important, as it allows national courts to request the Court of Justice of the European Union to interpret European law when a question arises about its interpretation or validity in a specific case.

Judicial dialogue is an informal mechanism of cooperation between national courts and the Court of Justice, which allows national courts to exchange opinions and experiences on the application of European law. This dialogue is facilitated through conferences, seminars, and other judicial cooperation activities. Judicial dialogue contributes to the creation of a common culture of interpretation of European law in all the Member States.

Cooperation in the field of enforcement of judicial decisions is another important aspect of judicial cooperation. National courts must enforce the judicial decisions of other Member States and the rulings of the Court of Justice of the European Union. This cooperation is facilitated through judicial cooperation instruments, such as the Brussels I Regulation and the Brussels II Regulation, which establish rules for the recognition and enforcement of judicial decisions in civil and commercial matters.

## Chapter 18: Monitoring the Application of Legislation

### 18.1 Infringement Procedures

Infringement procedures are mechanisms used by the European Commission to ensure that the Member States comply with their obligations under EU law. These procedures are initiated when the Commission becomes aware that a Member State has not communicated the transposition measures of a directive, has not correctly applied a regulation, or has adopted a measure that contradicts European law. Infringement procedures are fundamental to ensuring the effectiveness of EU law and to protecting the rights of citizens.

The infringement procedure begins with a pre-litigation phase, in which the Commission notifies the Member State of its preliminary observations on the infringement and requests it to submit its observations. If the Member State does not correct the infringement, the Commission may issue a reasoned opinion establishing the measures that the Member State must adopt to comply with its obligations. If the Member State does not comply with the reasoned opinion, the Commission may bring the matter before the Court of Justice of the European Union to rule on the infringement.

The Court of Justice may condemn the Member State for infringement and may impose a financial sanction, which is calculated based on the severity of the infringement and its duration. Financial sanctions may be significant and have the objective of deterring Member States from infringing their European obligations. Infringement procedures are an effective mechanism for ensuring that Member States comply with EU law and for protecting the rights of citizens.

### 18.2 Monitoring of Implementation

Monitoring the implementation of European legislation is a continuous process that has the objective of verifying that the Member States are correctly implementing European legislation in their national legislation. The European Commission is the main body responsible for monitoring implementation, and uses different tools to verify compliance, including information gathering, inspection visits, consultations with national authorities, and the evaluation of implementation statistics.

The Commission publishes periodic reports on the implementation of European legislation, which include information on the state of transposition of directives, the implementation measures adopted by the Member States, and the difficulties encountered in implementation. These reports are public and may be consulted by citizens and stakeholders. The Commission also organizes follow-up meetings with national authorities to discuss implementation issues and to seek solutions to the problems identified.

Monitoring the implementation of European legislation is a process that involves multiple actors, including the Commission, the Member States, the European Parliament, national courts, and stakeholders. Cooperation between these actors is fundamental to ensuring the effective implementation of European legislation and to protecting the rights of citizens.

### 18.3 The Role of the Court of Justice

The Court of Justice of the European Union plays a fundamental role in monitoring the application of European legislation, as it is the body competent to interpret European law and to resolve disputes arising from its application. The Court of Justice ensures the uniformity and coherence of the interpretation of European law in all the Member States, through the mechanism of preliminary rulings that allows national courts to request its interpretation.

The Court of Justice also resolves the infringement proceedings that the European Commission initiates against Member States that fail to comply with their European obligations. These proceedings include condemnation for infringement, the imposition of financial sanctions, and the enforcement of judgments. The Court of Justice ensures that the Member States comply with EU law and that citizens can exercise their rights effectively.

The role of the Court of Justice also extends to the protection of the fundamental rights of citizens, as the Court ensures that European legislation is compatible with the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union and with the common constitutional traditions of the Member States. The Court of Justice is therefore the guardian of EU law and the protector of the fundamental rights of European citizens.

## Chapter 19: The European Union Budget

### 19.1 The Budgetary Process

The budgetary process of the European Union is the set of procedures through which the European institutions draft, approve, and execute the Union's annual Budget. The budgetary process has a significant political dimension, as it determines how the Union's resources are allocated among the different policies and programs. The budgetary process also has a democratic dimension, as the Budget must be approved by the European Parliament, which is the direct representative of European citizens.

The budgetary process begins with the drafting of the budget draft by the European Commission, which submits its proposal to the European Council. The European Council examines the proposal and adopts a position on the multiannual financial framework, which establishes the maximum expenditure limits for each policy area. The European Council's position is submitted to the European Parliament, which examines it and adopts its own position. If Parliament and the Council fail to reach an agreement, a conciliation procedure is activated to reach an agreement on the financial framework.

Once the financial framework has been approved, the Commission drafts a detailed budget draft that includes specific allocations for each policy area and program. This draft is submitted to the Council, which adopts its position on the Budget. The Council's position is submitted to the European Parliament, which examines it and adopts its own position. If Parliament and the Council fail to reach an agreement, a conciliation procedure is activated to reach an agreement on the final Budget.

### 19.2 The Union's Own Resources

The European Union's own resources are the revenues that finance the Union Budget, and which come from different sources established in the Treaties and in Council decisions. Own resources include contributions based on Value Added Tax (VAT), contributions based on Gross Domestic Product (GDP), customs duties, sugar contributions, and other specific resources. These resources are collected by the Member States and transferred to the European Union to finance its activities.

VAT-based contributions are calculated by applying a harmonized rate to the VAT collected by each Member State. The harmonized rate is determined based on the Union's financing needs and the contributive capacity of each Member State. GDP-based contributions are calculated based on the wealth of each Member State, measured by its GDP, and have the objective of ensuring an equitable distribution of the financial burden among the Member States.

Customs duties are the revenues that the Union collects from the importation of goods from third countries, and are calculated based on the value of the imported goods and the applicable tariff rates. Customs duties are an important own resource for the Union, as they represent a significant portion of its total revenues. The collection of customs duties is managed by the national customs authorities, which transfer the revenues collected to the European Union.

### 19.3 Execution of the Budget

Execution of the Budget is the process by which the European institutions spend the resources allocated in the Budget to finance their activities and programs. Execution of the Budget is subject to strict financial management rules, which include planning, authorization, accounting, control, and evaluation. These rules have the objective of ensuring that resources are used effectively, efficiently, and in accordance with the law.

Budgetary planning is the first step of execution, and consists of establishing the expenditure objectives and priorities for the budgetary period. Authorization is the process by which it is verified that the proposed expenditure is in accordance with the law and that the funds are available. Accounting is the recording of the institution's financial operations, which allows the monitoring of the execution of the Budget. Control is the verification that the expenditure has been carried out in accordance with the law and the principles of sound financial management. Evaluation is the measurement of the effectiveness and efficiency of expenditure.

The European institutions are subject to an internal control system that ensures the regularity and efficiency of the execution of the Budget. This system includes the separation of functions between the authorizing officer, the accounting officer, and the internal auditor, which ensures that no person has control over all phases of the expenditure process. The internal control system also includes the carrying out of internal audits, performance evaluation, and risk management.

## Chapter 20: Parliamentary Oversight of the Commission

### 20.1 Oversight Instruments

The European Parliament has different oversight instruments over the European Commission, which allow it to supervise the Commission's activities and to hold it accountable for its actions. These instruments include parliamentary questions, motions of censure, non-binding resolutions, public hearings, and the budget approval procedure. These instruments have the objective of ensuring that the Commission acts in a transparent, effective, and lawful manner.

Parliamentary questions are an instrument that allows MEPs to request information from the Commission on any matter relating to its activities. Questions may be written or oral, and the Commission has the obligation to respond within a determined period. Parliamentary questions are an important oversight mechanism, as they allow MEPs to obtain information about the Commission's activities and to demand explanations for its decisions.

Motions of censure are a more severe oversight instrument, which allows the European Parliament to express its lack of confidence in the Commission. A motion of censure may only be submitted by one fourth of the MEPs, and requires a two-thirds majority of the votes cast to be approved. If the motion of censure is approved, the Commission must resign en bloc. This instrument is rarely used, but its existence has a deterrent effect on the Commission, as it reminds it that it is subject to Parliament's oversight.

### 20.2 The Committee of Inquiry

Committees of inquiry are oversight instruments that allow the European Parliament to investigate issues of public interest, including the activities of the European Commission. Committees of inquiry may be established at the request of one fourth of the MEPs, and have the competence to request information, hold public hearings, and formulate recommendations. Committees of inquiry are important oversight tools, as they allow Parliament to investigate issues that cannot be addressed with the ordinary oversight instruments.

Committees of inquiry may investigate any issue of public interest, including the activities of the Commission, the management of European funds, the protection of fundamental rights, and other issues that affect European citizens. Committees of inquiry may request documents, testimonies, and other evidence from the institutions, the Member States, and other organizations. The conclusions of committees of inquiry are published and submitted to the Commission and the other institutions for consideration.

Committees of inquiry do not have coercive powers, which means that they cannot impose sanctions or compel persons to testify. However, committees of inquiry may request the assistance of national authorities and European institutions to obtain the necessary information. Committees of inquiry are a valuable instrument of parliamentary oversight, as they allow Parliament to investigate issues of public interest and to hold institutions accountable.

### 20.3 The Budget Approval Procedure

The budget approval procedure is a fundamental instrument of European Parliament oversight of the Commission, as it allows Parliament to supervise the Commission's financial management and to ensure that funds are used effectively and efficiently. The European Parliament acts as budgetary authority together with the Council, and has the capacity to modify budget allocations, to reject expenditure proposals, and to request information on the execution of the Budget.

The European Parliament examines the budget draft submitted by the Commission and adopts its own positions on the budget allocations. Parliament may propose modifications to the allocations, may reject expenditure items, and may request additional information from the Commission on the justification of the allocations. The conciliation procedure between Parliament and the Council allows an agreement to be reached on the final Budget.

Parliamentary oversight over the execution of the Budget is exercised through the budgetary discharge procedure, in which Parliament examines the execution reports submitted by the Commission and approves or rejects the management. Parliament may also request additional audits from the European Court of Auditors, which is the body responsible for verifying the regularity and sound management of Union revenues and expenditures.

## Chapter 21: The Role of the Court of Justice in the Legislative Process

### 21.1 Constitutional Review of Legislation

The Court of Justice of the European Union plays a fundamental role in the constitutional review of European legislation, as it is the body competent to verify that European legislation is compatible with the Treaties and with the Charter of Fundamental Rights. Constitutional review is a mechanism that ensures that European legislation respects the fundamental principles of EU law and the fundamental rights of citizens.

Constitutional review is exercised through different mechanisms, including actions for annulment, preliminary rulings on validity, and conformity control with fundamental rights. Actions for annulment are procedures in which the Member States, the institutions, or citizens may challenge the validity of a Union legal act before the Court of Justice. Preliminary rulings on validity are mechanisms through which national courts request the Court of Justice to rule on the validity of a Union legal act in a specific case.

Conformity control with fundamental rights is a mechanism that ensures that European legislation respects the fundamental rights enshrined in the Charter of Fundamental Rights and in the common constitutional traditions of the Member States. The Court of Justice verifies the conformity of legislation with fundamental rights through a proportionality control, which evaluates whether the limitation of fundamental rights is appropriate, necessary, and proportionate to the pursued objective.

### 21.2 Judicial Dialogue

Judicial dialogue is a mechanism of cooperation between the Court of Justice of the European Union and national courts, which facilitates the coherent application of European law in all the Member States. Judicial dialogue manifests itself through the mechanism of preliminary rulings, which allows national courts to request the Court of Justice to interpret European law when a question arises about its interpretation or validity.

Judicial dialogue is a bidirectional process, in which both the Court of Justice and national courts contribute to the interpretation and application of European law. The Court of Justice interprets European law through its rulings, which are binding on national courts. National courts apply European law in the specific cases brought before them, and may request the interpretation of the Court of Justice when they have doubts about the interpretation of a European provision.

Judicial dialogue is fundamental to ensuring the uniformity of the interpretation of European law in all the Member States. Without judicial dialogue, each national court could interpret European law differently, which would generate legal uncertainty and affect the effectiveness of EU law. The Court of Justice has developed a rich and complex case law that provides guidance to national courts in the interpretation of European law.

### 21.3 Enforcement of Court of Justice Rulings

Enforcement of Court of Justice rulings is a fundamental aspect of the EU legal system, as it ensures that the Court's decisions are effectively complied with by the institutions, the Member States, and citizens. The Court of Justice does not have its own enforcement mechanisms, but the European Commission has the obligation to monitor the enforcement of rulings and to take the necessary measures to ensure their compliance.

In the event that a Member State fails to comply with a Court of Justice ruling, the Commission may initiate additional infringement proceedings to require compliance with the ruling. If the Member State continues to fail to comply, the Commission may bring the matter again before the Court of Justice, which may impose financial sanctions on the Member State for the infringement. Financial sanctions may be significant and have the objective of deterring Member States from failing to comply with the Court's rulings.

Enforcement of Court of Justice rulings also depends on the cooperation of national courts, which are responsible for applying European law in specific cases. National courts have the obligation to apply European law in accordance with the interpretation of the Court of Justice, and to ensure that stakeholders can exercise their rights effectively. Cooperation between the Court of Justice and national courts is fundamental to ensuring the effectiveness of EU law.

## Chapter 22: The Influence of Interests in the Legislative Process

### 22.1 Lobby Groups

Lobby groups are organizations that seek to influence the European legislative process to promote their interests. Lobby groups may represent businesses, economic sectors, civil society organizations, national governments, or other actors. The activities of lobby groups are regulated by European transparency legislation, which establishes requirements for registration, declaration of activities, and financial transparency.

Lobby groups may influence the legislative process through different mechanisms, including direct lobbying of MEPs and institutional officials, participation in public consultations, organization of events, and public communication. Direct lobbying is a common activity in Brussels, where thousands of lobby group representatives work to influence legislative decisions. Lobbying may be a valuable source of information for the institutions, but may also pose risks of regulatory capture and imbalance in the representation of interests.

European transparency legislation establishes strict requirements for lobby groups, including the obligation to register in the Union's Transparency Register, the obligation to declare their lobbying activities and their sources of financing, and the obligation to comply with codes of conduct. The Transparency Register is a public mechanism that allows citizens to access information about the lobby groups operating in Brussels and about their lobbying activities.

### 22.2 Stakeholders

Stakeholders are actors that have an interest in the European legislative process and that may be affected by the adopted legislation. Stakeholders include businesses, civil society organizations, trade unions, professional associations, national governments, local and regional authorities, and other relevant actors. The European institutions have the obligation to consult stakeholders during the process of drafting legislative proposals, to ensure that proposals take into account the opinions and concerns of all affected actors.

Stakeholder consultation is a fundamental process for the quality of the legislative process, as it provides the institutions with valuable information about the needs and concerns of the different actors. Stakeholders may contribute with technical knowledge, practical experience, and perspectives that enrich the legislative debate. Stakeholder consultation also contributes to the democratic legitimacy of the legislative process, as it ensures that all voices are heard before a decision is adopted.

The European institutions use different mechanisms to consult stakeholders, including online public consultations, public hearings, bilateral meetings, conferences, and seminars. The European Commission has adopted an Openness and Participation Policy that establishes the principles and procedures for stakeholder consultation in the process of drafting legislative proposals. This Policy has the objective of ensuring that consultation is transparent, inclusive, and effective.

### 22.3 Transparency of Lobbying

Transparency of lobbying is a fundamental principle that has the objective of ensuring that citizens can access information about the activities of lobby groups and about their influence in the legislative process. Transparency of lobbying is achieved through requirements for registration, declaration of activities, and financial transparency that apply to lobby groups operating in Brussels.

The Union's Transparency Register is a public mechanism that allows citizens to access information about registered lobby groups, including their identity, their objectives, their lobbying activities, and their sources of financing. Registration in the Register is voluntary, but the European institutions only maintain formal relations with lobby groups that are registered. This incentivizes lobby groups to register to ensure that their voices are heard by the institutions.

Transparency of lobbying is also achieved through the publication of periodic reports on meetings between institutional representatives and lobby groups. These reports are published on the institutions' Internet portals and allow citizens to know which lobby groups are interacting with the institutions and on which issues. Transparency of lobbying is fundamental to ensuring the integrity of the legislative process and to preventing regulatory capture by particular interest groups.
