Mesopotamia, whose name means "between rivers" in Greek, is one of the most important regions in human history, as it was the place where some of the most advanced, innovative, and influential civilizations of antiquity were born. This region, which stretches between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in present-day Iraq, Syria, Turkey, and Kuwait, was the setting for the development of agriculture, writing, law, architecture, astronomy, mathematics, and other disciplines that laid the foundations of Western civilization. The Sumerians, who settled in southern Mesopotamia around 4000 BC, were the first to develop a writing system (cuneiform writing, which used wedge-shaped symbols carved on clay tablets), a system of government (temples and palaces, which served as administrative, religious, and economic centers), a polytheistic religion (with gods such as Anu, Enlil, Enki, Inanna, and Marduk), monumental architecture (ziggurats, stepped temples that served as astronomical observatories and religious centers), and epic literature (the Epic of Gilgamesh, one of the oldest literary works in the world). The Babylonians, who dominated Mesopotamia around 1800 BC, created the Code of Hammurabi, one of the first sets of written laws in history, and developed monumental architecture that included the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. The Assyrians, who dominated Mesopotamia around 900 BC, created a militaristic and expansionist empire that stretched from Egypt to Iran, and developed monumental architecture that included palaces with sculpted reliefs depicting military campaigns, hunting scenes, and religious rituals.

Cuneiform writing is the first writing system in human history, and its invention in Mesopotamia around 3400 BC marked the beginning of a new era in the history of civilization, as it allowed humans to record information, communicate at a distance, preserve collective memory, and develop literature, science, philosophy, and public administration. Cuneiform writing was developed in the Sumerian city of Uruk (in present-day Iraq), and it was used by carving wedge-shaped symbols (from which it takes its name, from the Latin "cuneus" meaning "wedge") onto wet clay tablets that were dried in the sun or baked in kilns to preserve them. The cuneiform symbols originally represented concrete objects and concepts (such as the sun, the moon, water, bread, the house), but over time they became more abstract and complex, until they became a complete writing system capable of representing sounds, words, and complete phrases. Cuneiform writing was used for more than 3,000 years (from 3400 BC to the 1st century AD) and was used by various civilizations and languages, including Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian, Elamite, Hittite, Hurrian, and Old Persian. The cuneiform tablets, of which more than 300,000 have been found at archaeological sites in Mesopotamia, contain a variety of documents including literary texts (the Epic of Gilgamesh, Sumerian hymns, Akkadian prophecies), legal texts (the Code of Hammurabi, Assyrian laws), religious texts (hymns, prayers, rituals, myths), administrative texts (inventories, contracts, tax lists, correspondence), scientific texts (astronomical tables, mathematical texts, medical texts), and historical texts (royal chronicles, military annals, king lists). Cuneiform writing is not only a communication system; it is also a testament to the human capacity for abstraction, analysis, and the systematic organization of knowledge.

The Giza pyramid complex, located on the outskirts of Cairo (Egypt), includes three main pyramids: the Great Pyramid of Khufu (built around 2560 BC, with an original height of 146 meters and a weight of approximately 6 million tons of stone), the Pyramid of Khafre (built around 2530 BC, with a height of 143 meters), and the Pyramid of Menkaure (built around 2500 BC, with a height of 65 meters). Greek philosophy is one of the most important and lasting contributions of Greek civilization to humanity, and its influence on Western philosophy, science, politics, ethics, metaphysics, logic, and human thought is profound and impossible to exaggerate. The first Greek philosophers, known as the Pre-Socratics (because they lived before Socrates), were thinkers who sought rational explanations for natural phenomena without resorting to mythology or religion, laying the foundations of Western scientific and philosophical thought. Thales of Miletus (around 624-546 BC) was the first to propose that water was the fundamental element of the universe, a revolutionary idea that marked the beginning of natural philosophy. Heraclitus of Ephesus (around 535-475 BC) proposed that fire was the fundamental element and that everything was in constant change ("you cannot bathe twice in the same river"). Parmenides of Elea (around 515-450 BC) proposed that being was immutable, eternal, and indivisible, and that the senses deceived us about the nature of reality. Democritus of Abdera (around 460-370 BC) proposed the atomic theory of the universe, according to which everything was composed of indivisible atoms moving in a void, an idea confirmed by modern science more than 2,000 years later. Socrates (470-399 BC) was the father of Western philosophy, and his Socratic method (questions and answers that seek truth through dialogue and reflection) remains the foundation of Western education and philosophy. Plato (428-348 BC), a disciple of Socrates, was the founder of the Academy, the first university in the world, and developed an idealist philosophy that distinguished between the world of ideas (eternal, immutable, and perfect) and the sensible world (changing, imperfect, and transient). Aristotle (384-322 BC), a disciple of Plato, was the founder of the Lyceum and developed a philosophical system that covered logic, metaphysics, ethics, politics, biology, and poetics, and whose influence dominated Western thought for more than 2,000 years.

The numerical system of the quipus was decimal: knots represented units (1-9), tens (10-90), hundreds (100-900), and so on, and the position of the knots on the cord indicated their positional value. The most elaborate quipus contained hundreds of colored cords (each color representing a different category: red for warriors, yellow for gold, green for corn, etc.), and some quipus included subsidiary cords, annexed cords, and marker cords that provided additional information. The quipucamayocs, the officials responsible for creating, reading, and maintaining the quipus, were a specialized professional class who received rigorous training in the empire's schools (the yachayhuasis), and their role was so important that when the Spanish conquered the Inca Empire, they burned thousands of quipus as part of their policy of destroying Inca culture, which has enormously hindered the interpretation of these devices. Although the numerical system of the quipus has been partially deciphered, the question of whether the quipus also contained linguistic information (narrative, historical, literary) remains the subject of debate among researchers, and some experts have proposed that the quipus could contain information as complex as conventional writing, only in a three-dimensional and tactile format rather than a two-dimensional and visual one. Quipus have been found at more than 600 archaeological sites throughout the former Inca Empire, and their study continues to reveal new aspects of this unique recording system.

The Maya developed one of the most sophisticated and elegant numerical systems of antiquity, a vigesimal (base-20) system that used only three symbols: a dot for one (.), a bar for five (-), and a shell for zero, a system that included the invention of zero, more than a thousand years before it was used in Europe.
