The Hanseatic League, or Hansa, was a commercial and defensive alliance of merchant guilds and cities of northern Germany, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia that dominated the maritime trade of northern Europe between the 12th and 17th centuries. Its origins date back to 1158, when the city of Lübeck obtained commercial privileges from Duke Henry the Lion of Saxony and Bavaria, and became the nerve center of trade between the Baltic Sea and the North Sea. The Hansa was not a formal political organization, but a fluid network of cities that shared commercial interests and acted in concert to protect their trade routes, obtain merchant privileges in foreign ports, and eliminate pirate competition. At its peak, around 1400, the Hanseatic League had approximately 200 associated cities, from Novgorod in Russia to London in England, and from Bergen in Norway to Bruges in Flanders, making it the largest commercial network in medieval Europe.

Mercantilism was the dominant economic theory in Europe between the 16th and 18th centuries, and constituted the theoretical and institutional framework that guided colonial trade and the imperial expansion of European powers. The fundamental principles of mercantilism held that the wealth of a nation was measured by the quantity of precious metals it possessed, that international trade was a zero-sum game in which one nation could only win at the expense of another, that the state should actively intervene in the economy to promote exports and restrict imports, and that colonies should exist to benefit the mother country. Mercantilist policies included the imposition of high tariffs on imports, the granting of commercial monopolies to privileged companies, the prohibition of exporting precious metals, the encouragement of national industries through subsidies and privileges, and the creation of colonial systems that guaranteed the supply of cheap raw materials to the mother country.
