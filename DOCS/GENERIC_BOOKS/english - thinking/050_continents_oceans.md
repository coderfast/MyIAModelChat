A continent is a large landmass that extends over a wide geographical area, distinguished from islands by its size and by being connected to other landmasses or by constituting a recognized geographical unit. The definition of a continent is not purely geographical, but also involves cultural, historical, and political criteria that have determined the traditional classification of the great landmasses. The five traditionally recognized continents are Europe, Asia, Africa, America, and Oceania, although the classification varies according to the criteria used: some systems consider America as two continents (North and South), others include Antarctica as a sixth continent, and others recognize six or seven continents depending on how the landmasses are grouped. The total surface area of the continents covers approximately one hundred forty-nine million square kilometers, representing slightly more than twenty-nine percent of the Earth's total surface. The continents present a great diversity of landscapes, climates, ecosystems, and cultures, reflecting the geological, climatic, and human complexity of the planet. The study of the continents is fundamental to geography, geology, climatology, biology, and social sciences, as the continents are the stage where natural and human processes shape the world.

The marine food chain is the sequence of organisms that feed on each other in the marine ecosystem, from primary producers to top predators. Primary producers are photosynthetic organisms, such as phytoplankton, algae, and marine plants, that convert solar energy into organic matter through photosynthesis. Phytoplankton, composed of microscopic organisms such as diatoms, dinoflagellates, and cyanobacteria, is the base of most marine food chains and produces approximately fifty percent of the oxygen in the Earth's atmosphere. Primary consumers are organisms that feed on primary producers, such as zooplankton (copepods, fish larvae, jellyfish), mollusks (oysters, mussels, snails), and small fish (sardines, anchovies, herring). Secondary consumers are larger fish, seabirds, and mammals that feed on primary consumers, such as tuna, salmon, penguins, and seals. Top predators are organisms at the top of the food chain, which have no natural predators, such as sharks, orcas, and large cetaceans. The marine food chain is a complex and dynamic system, where changes in one species can affect all others through cascade effects. Overfishing of one species can cause an imbalance in the food chain, affecting populations of predators and prey and altering the ecosystem structure. The conservation of marine biodiversity requires the protection of key species, sustainable fisheries management, and the preservation of critical habitats for the maintenance of food chains.

Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that explains the movement of the Earth's lithosphere, the rigid outer layer of the planet that includes the crust and the upper part of the mantle. According to this theory, the lithosphere is divided into several tectonic plates, large rock fragments that move slowly over the asthenosphere, the lower layer of the mantle that behaves as a viscous fluid at high temperatures. The movement of the tectonic plates is driven by mantle convection currents, which transport heat from the Earth's interior toward the surface. The tectonic plates interact with each other at their boundaries, creating different types of geological phenomena. At divergent boundaries, the plates move apart from each other, creating new oceans (such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge) and volcanoes. At convergent boundaries, the plates approach and collide, creating mountains (such as the Himalaya, formed by the collision of the Indian plate with the Eurasian plate), ocean trenches (such as the Mariana Trench), and volcanic activity (such as the Pacific Ring of Fire). At transform boundaries, the plates slide past each other, creating geological faults (such as the San Andreas Fault in California) that can cause earthquakes. The theory of plate tectonics was developed in the first half of the twentieth century, based on the work of Alfred Wegener, Arthur Holmes, and Harry Hess, and has become one of the pillars of modern geology, explaining the distribution of continents, mountain formation, volcanic activity, and earthquakes.
