# Basic Oceanography

## Chapter 1: Introduction to the Oceans

### 1.1 The terrestrial hydrosphere

The terrestrial hydrosphere comprises all the water present on our planet, encompassing the oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, groundwater, and water contained in the atmosphere as vapor. The oceans occupy approximately 71 percent of the Earth's surface, corresponding to some 361 million square kilometers in extent. This immense mass of saline water contains nearly 97 percent of the planet's total water, with the remaining 3 percent being freshwater distributed in glaciers, polar ice caps, and continental waters. The hydrosphere plays a fundamental role in regulating the global climate, as it absorbs and releases heat gradually, moderating temperatures in coastal regions and influencing weather patterns worldwide.

The distribution of water on the Earth's surface is not uniform, but presents significant regional variations determined by the configuration of the continents, ocean currents, and climatic factors. The five major oceans — Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic, and Southern — are interconnected, forming a continuous body of water that covers most of the planet. Each one presents its own characteristics in terms of temperature, salinity, depth, and chemical composition, which generates an enormous diversity of marine environments. The oceans constantly receive freshwater from rivers, precipitation, and ice melt, which partially modifies their properties in coastal zones and near river mouths.

The study of the hydrosphere is fundamental for understanding the processes that regulate life on Earth, from the water cycle to climatic dynamics. Oceanography, as a multidisciplinary science, encompasses physical, chemical, biological, and geological aspects of the oceans, providing comprehensive knowledge of these ecosystems. Technological development in recent decades, including oceanographic satellites, monitoring buoys, and autonomous underwater vehicles, has revolutionized our ability to study and understand the oceans. This knowledge is essential for managing marine resources, predicting weather phenomena, and understanding the impact of climate change on oceanic ecosystems.

### 1.2 History of oceanography

Oceanography as a scientific discipline has its roots in the marine explorations of the 18th and 19th centuries, although human curiosity about the oceans is much older. The first systematic oceanographic expeditions were carried out by the HMS Challenger between 1872 and 1876, a British expedition that traveled over 127,000 kilometers and collected data on temperature, salinity, currents, and marine life in all the major oceans. This expedition is considered the birth of modern oceanography, as it laid the methodological foundations and compiled an unprecedented amount of information about the oceans. For nearly four years, a team of scientists aboard the Challenger investigated unknown aspects of the seas, discovering more than 4,700 new marine species.

Throughout the 20th century, oceanography experienced accelerated development driven by the military needs of the two world wars and the Cold War. Advances in sonar, radar, and submarine technology allowed the ocean floor to be mapped with precision never before achieved, revealing the existence of mid-ocean ridges, abyssal trenches, and other submarine features. The Vema expedition, carried out by Columbia University between 1952 and 1953, discovered the Mariana Trench and confirmed the existence of the greatest known depth in the oceans, the 10,994 meters of Challenger Deep. Subsequently, in the 1960s, the work of Harry Hess and other scientists provided key evidence for the theory of plate tectonics, based on the study of mid-ocean ridges.

In the contemporary era, oceanography has become an increasingly interdisciplinary and technological science. Satellites such as the Jason altimeter and the SMOS radiometer allow the ocean surface to be monitored on a global scale, measuring variations in sea level, surface temperature, and salinity. Argo buoys, deployed worldwide since 2000, provide vertical profiles of temperature and salinity down to 2,000 meters depth, creating a real-time monitoring network of the oceans. Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) and remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) allow exploration of deep and hostile zones that were previously inaccessible, opening new frontiers in the knowledge of life and geological processes in the ocean depths.

### 1.3 Importance of the oceans for the planet

The oceans play a crucial role in regulating the global climate, acting as the planet's largest heat reservoir and absorbing a significant portion of the carbon dioxide emitted by human activities. The thermal capacity of water is approximately four times that of air, which allows the oceans to store and redistribute enormous amounts of energy through thermohaline currents. These deep currents, driven by differences in temperature and salinity, transport heat from the tropics toward the polar regions, directly influencing the climate of the continents. Without the regulating action of the oceans, temperature variations would be much more extreme and life as we know it would be impossible.

In addition to their climatic role, the oceans are the planet's greatest source of biodiversity, harboring an estimated 500,000 to 10 million species, many of which have not yet been discovered or described by science. From the microscopic organisms of plankton to the largest whales, marine life represents a complex web of ecological relationships that sustain oceanic ecosystems. Corals, seagrass beds, mangroves, and kelp forests are coastal ecosystems of extraordinary productivity that harbor a great diversity of species and provide essential services such as coastal protection, carbon capture, and food provision. The pelagic zone, which comprises the water column away from the coast and the bottom, also harbors an enormous diversity of life, from the phytoplankton that performs photosynthesis at the surface to the organisms of the abyssal depths.

The oceans also constitute a fundamental economic resource for humanity, providing food, raw materials, energy, and commercial transport routes. Marine fishing and aquaculture supply protein to billions of people worldwide, representing an essential part of global food security. Mineral resources of the ocean floor include oil and natural gas on continental shelves, manganese nodules in the abyss, and hydrothermal sulfides at mid-ocean ridges. Similarly, the oceans offer possibilities for renewable energy generation through wave energy, offshore wind, tidal, and thermal energy. International maritime transport moves more than 80 percent of global trade by volume, connecting economies and facilitating the exchange of goods and services between all regions of the world.


## Chapter 2: Structure and Zones of the Ocean

### 2.1 Vertical zones of the ocean

The oceanic water column is conventionally divided into several vertical zones based on depth, light penetration, temperature, and environmental conditions. The epipelagic zone, also called the light zone, extends from the surface to approximately 200 meters depth and is the most productive zone of the ocean, as it receives sufficient sunlight to allow photosynthesis by phytoplankton. In this layer, most marine life is concentrated, from small planktonic organisms to large predators such as tuna, sharks, and cetaceans. The temperature in the epipelagic varies considerably depending on latitude, season, and currents, but generally remains between 15 and 30 degrees Celsius in tropical regions.

The mesopelagic zone, known as the twilight zone, extends from 200 to 1,000 meters depth and receives only a tiny percentage of the sunlight that penetrates the surface. In this zone, the phenomenon of the Deep Thermocline Discontinuity occurs, a layer where temperature drops rapidly with depth, acting as a barrier that separates warm surface water from cold deep water. Life in the mesopelagic is represented mainly by small and moderately large organisms, including many bioluminescent fish that use light production to communicate, attract prey, or evade predators. The bathyal zone, which extends from 1,000 to 4,000 meters, is completely dark and presents extreme conditions of pressure, darkness, and low temperature, yet still harbors a remarkable diversity of life adapted to these adverse conditions.

The abyssal zone, situated between 4,000 and 6,000 meters, represents the greatest volume of the ocean and is characterized by extreme environmental conditions: pressure up to 600 atmospheres, temperature near zero degrees, and complete darkness. Despite these hostile conditions, life exists even in the deepest zones, with species adapted to extreme pressure such as the snailfish, amphipod crustaceans, and various gelatinous organisms. The hadal zone, which comprises the oceanic trenches below 6,000 meters to the greatest known depth (10,994 meters in the Mariana Trench), harbors specialized communities of extremophilic life that defy our understanding of the limits of life. These hadal organisms, including fish, annelids, crustaceans, and microbes, have developed extraordinary biochemical and physiological adaptations to survive in an environment of immense pressure, total darkness, and scarce nutrients.

### 2.2 Horizontal zones and coastal regions

The horizontal zones of the ocean are defined based on distance from the coast, seafloor depth, and oceanographic characteristics. The neritic zone extends from the shoreline to the edge of the continental shelf, generally to about 200 meters depth, and is characterized by shallow waters, high biological productivity, and great influence of coastal processes. This zone is extraordinarily rich in life due to light penetration, the mixing of nutrients from the continent, and the existence of hard substrates that allow the development of sessile organism communities such as corals, sponges, and algae. The continental shelf is a submerged extension of the continent, with a gentle slope and depths that generally do not exceed 200 meters, constituting the habitat for a wide variety of benthic and pelagic species.

The coastal bathyal zone and the oceanic zone represent transitions toward deeper waters further from continental influence. The bathyal zone, from 200 to 4,000 meters, presents a gradient of environmental conditions including decreasing temperature, increasing pressure, and reduced biological productivity. In this zone, important oceanographic features are found such as continental slopes, submarine canyons, and deep muds, which harbor communities of organisms specialized in exploiting the scarce organic matter that arrives from the surface. The abyssal zone, from 4,000 meters to the ocean floor in the deep plains, represents the largest habitat by area on the planet and harbors benthic communities of low density but high diversity adapted to the extreme conditions of pressure, darkness, and low nutrient availability.

Coastal regions are interface zones between land and sea, characterized by high dynamics and habitat diversity. These include sand and gravel beaches, rocky cliffs, estuaries where rivers empty into the sea, coastal lagoons, tropical mangroves, and glacial fjords. Each of these coastal habitats harbors specific biological communities adapted to the particular conditions of salinity, waves, tides, and substrate. Rocky coasts, for example, present a vertical gradient of organisms that includes algae in the supralittoral zone, limpets and mussels in the intertidal, and sea urchins and starfish in the subtidal. Sandy coasts, on the other hand, are dynamic habitats where life is dominated by infaunal organisms that live buried in the sediment, such as bivalve mollusks, annelids, and crustaceans.

### 2.3 Oceanic lithosphere and submarine relief

The oceanic lithosphere, which constitutes the crust and upper mantle beneath the oceans, is significantly thinner and denser than continental lithosphere, with a thickness ranging between 5 and 10 kilometers in the deep basins. It is composed primarily of oceanic basalt, a dark and dense volcanic rock that forms at mid-ocean ridges when magma from the mantle rises to the surface and cools rapidly in contact with seawater. The oceanic lithosphere is continuously created at the ridges, moves away from them through seafloor spreading, and eventually subducts beneath continental lithosphere at subduction zones, completing a geological cycle that lasts approximately 200 million years. This cycle of creation and destruction of oceanic crust is a fundamental component of plate tectonics theory and explains the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, and abyssal trenches on Earth.

The submarine relief presents a diversity of geological forms that rival those of the continents, including mid-ocean ridges, abyssal trenches, seamounts, guyots, abyssal plains, submarine canyons, and erosion terraces. Mid-ocean ridges are submarine mountain chains that extend over more than 65,000 kilometers across all the oceans, representing the planet's largest mountain system. These ridges are formed by the separation of tectonic plates and the ascent of magma from the mantle, creating new oceanic crust that spreads symmetrically from both sides of the ridge. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge, which runs through the center of the Atlantic Ocean, is probably the most well-known ridge and has been extensively studied since the 1960s.

Abyssal trenches are linear depressions in the ocean floor that form at subduction zones, where one oceanic plate sinks beneath another plate. These trenches can reach depths exceeding 10,000 meters, such as the Mariana Trench in the Pacific Ocean, which at 10,994 meters represents the deepest point on Earth. Abyssal trenches are zones of intense tectonic activity, frequently associated with large-magnitude earthquakes and active volcanism. At the bottom of these trenches, extreme biological communities are found living under pressures exceeding 1,000 atmospheres, in total darkness and with scarce nutrient availability, representing one of the most hostile environments on the planet for life. Seamounts are isolated elevations of the ocean floor that rise at least 1,000 meters above the surrounding bottom, and they usually have volcanic origins, with some rising to the surface to form islands.


## Chapter 3: Ocean Currents

### 3.1 Thermohaline circulation

Thermohaline circulation is the system of deep ocean currents that extends across all the oceans of the planet and is driven by differences in temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline) of seawater. This circulation system, also known as the Global Conveyor Belt, transports water from the surface to the ocean depths in polar regions and brings it back to the surface in equatorial regions along cycles that can last more than 1,000 years. Warm, saline surface water from the North Atlantic cools near the pole, becomes denser, and sinks, initiating a deep flow that heads south along the Atlantic floor, continues through the Indian and Pacific Oceans, and eventually returns to the surface and the Atlantic after traveling thousands of kilometers. This system plays a crucial role in regulating global climate, as it redistributes heat and nutrients across all the oceans.

The sinking of water in the North Atlantic, near Greenland and the seas of Norway and Iceland, is the main engine of thermohaline circulation. In these regions, the surface water of the Atlantic, which has traveled from the tropics with high salinity and relatively high temperature, cools in contact with the polar atmosphere and becomes dense enough to sink to depths of 2,000 to 4,000 meters. This sinking process, called deep water formation, generates a deep current that flows southward along the Atlantic basin, known as the North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW). The NADW spreads across the entire South Atlantic, interacts with the currents of the Indian Ocean, and eventually contributes to the formation of the Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW), which surrounds Antarctica and extends across the deep oceans of the entire Southern Hemisphere.

Thermohaline circulation is directly linked to global climate and has experienced significant variations in the geological past, with possible implications for future climate patterns. Climate models predict that global warming could weaken thermohaline circulation by reducing deep water formation in the North Atlantic, due to rising sea surface temperatures and freshwater input from Greenland ice melt. A significant weakening of thermohaline circulation could have dramatic consequences for the climate of the Northern Hemisphere, including relative cooling of Western Europe, changes in precipitation patterns, and alterations in fisheries productivity. Continuous monitoring of this current is fundamental for understanding and predicting the effects of climate change on the global oceanic and climatic system.

### 3.2 Surface currents

Ocean surface currents are flows of water that move in the upper layers of the ocean, driven primarily by wind, the Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect), and the configuration of the continents. These currents form large circulatory systems called ocean gyres, which in the Northern Hemisphere rotate clockwise and in the Southern Hemisphere counterclockwise, under the influence of the Coriolis effect. The five major gyres worldwide — North Atlantic, South Atlantic, North Pacific, South Pacific, and Indian — organize the surface circulation of the oceans and determine the distribution of heat, nutrients, and marine organisms on a global scale. Surface currents are generally faster and wider than deep currents, with velocities that can reach several kilometers per hour in the most extreme cases.

The Gulf Stream is one of the most important and powerful surface currents in the world, transporting warm water from the Gulf of Mexico along the eastern coast of the United States and then toward the northeastern Atlantic, where it becomes the North Atlantic Current. This current transports more than 30 million cubic meters of water per second, an amount equivalent to all the world's rivers combined, and raises the temperatures of the coasts of Western Europe, making the climate of countries like Ireland, Norway, and Iceland significantly milder than other regions at the same latitude. The Gulf Stream is a fundamental component of North Atlantic circulation and its behavior has direct implications for the climate of Europe and the distribution of marine species in the Atlantic.

Other important currents include the Kuroshio Current in the North Pacific Ocean, which transports warm water from the tropics toward Japan and eastern Asia, and the Humboldt Current (or Peru Current), which transports cold, nutrient-rich water from Antarctica toward the western coast of South America. The Humboldt Current is responsible for the extraordinary fisheries productivity of the coasts of Peru and Chile, as trade winds drive an upwelling process that brings deep water rich in nitrates and phosphates to the surface, fueling a productive chain that begins with phytoplankton and ends with anchovy and other commercially important species. The Benguela Current, on the western coast of southern Africa, presents similar characteristics and is equally important for the region's fishery. These upwelling currents are high-productivity ecosystems but also highly vulnerable to climatic disturbances such as El Niño.

### 3.3 Tidal currents

Tidal currents are periodic movements of seawater caused by the gravitational force of the Moon and the Sun, which generate the phenomenon of tides. When the Moon is aligned with the Earth and the Sun (spring tides), their gravitational forces add up and produce the greatest tidal amplitudes, while when they are at a right angle (neap tides), their effects counteract each other and the amplitudes are minimal. Tidal currents occur when water moves horizontally to compensate for the level differences generated by tides, and their intensity depends on the tidal amplitude, the shape of the coast, and the depth of the seafloor. In some rivers and estuaries, tidal currents can be extremely strong, reaching velocities exceeding 10 kilometers per hour in places like the Severn River in England or the Amazon River in Brazil.

Macrotidalism is a phenomenon that occurs on those coasts where the tidal amplitude exceeds 4 meters, creating especially intense tidal currents. The Strait of Pas-de-Calais, between France and England, is a classic example of an extremely strong tidal current, where water flows at speeds up to 15 kilometers per hour, creating eddies, whirlpools, and very dangerous navigation conditions. The Bay of Fundy, in Canada, experiences the world's largest tides, with amplitudes that can reach 16 meters, generating tidal currents of great power. These currents play an important role in vertical water mixing, sediment and nutrient distribution, and coastal erosion, in addition to having implications for navigation, fishing, and tidal energy generation.

Tidal currents have important ecological implications, as they influence the distribution of marine organisms, sediment transport, and coastal ecosystem dynamics. In the intertidal zone, organisms must adapt to regular periods of immersion and emersion, which has selected a series of physiological and behavioral adaptations. Tidal currents can facilitate the transport of larvae of marine organisms, allowing the dispersal and colonization of new habitats. Similarly, the action of tidal currents on the seafloor creates habitats such as sandbanks, cockle beds, and sand flats, which harbor communities of benthic organisms. The kinetic energy of tidal currents can also be harnessed for electricity generation through tidal turbines, a renewable energy source with significant potential in regions with macrotidalism.


## Chapter 4: Waves and Tsunamis

### 4.1 Formation and types of waves

Waves are the set of surface disturbances that form on the ocean surface as a result of wind action on the water. Wave formation depends on three main factors: wind speed, duration of blowing, and the fetch distance over which the wind acts on the water. When wind blows over the ocean surface, it transfers energy to the water through friction, creating small capillaries that grow into progressive waves. If the wind continues to blow with sufficient intensity and duration, the waves grow until they reach a maximum size determined by the wind conditions, and then the wave state is said to be fully developed. Locally generated wind waves are called locally generated waves, while those that travel great distances from their generation zone are called remotely generated waves or swell.

Waves are classified based on their period, height, and wavelength, factors that determine their behavior and interaction with the coast. Short-period wind waves (less than 8 seconds) are generally irregular and low in energy, while long-period waves (more than 10 seconds) are more regular, with greater wavelength and greater energy transport capacity. Swell waves are long-period waves that have traveled thousands of kilometers from their generation zone in the North Atlantic or South Pacific, and can maintain their energy for days before reaching the coast. Significant wave height (Hs), defined as the average height of the highest third of waves in a time record, is the most commonly used parameter for describing sea state in oceanographic predictions.

Waves exert a decisive influence on the shape of coasts and the dynamics of littoral ecosystems. When waves reach shallow waters, they undergo a transformation process that includes refraction, diffraction, reflection, and breaking. Refraction is the change in propagation direction of the wave as it interacts with the seafloor, causing waves to bend and concentrate on coastal promontories and disperse in bays. Wave breaking on the coast releases energy that causes coastal erosion and sediment transport, creating beaches, cliffs, barrier islands, and other coastal morphological features. Wave energy also influences the distribution of marine organisms in the intertidal zone, selecting species with adaptations to withstand the mechanical action of waves.

### 4.2 Tsunamis: formation, propagation, and impact

Tsunamis are series of long-wavelength, long-period waves generated by a large-scale vertical disturbance of the water column, generally associated with large-magnitude submarine earthquakes. When an earthquake with a magnitude greater than 7.5 on the Richter scale produces a vertical displacement of the seafloor in a subduction zone, it displaces a water column that propagates in all directions at speeds up to 800 kilometers per hour in deep waters, similar to the speed of a commercial airplane. Unlike wind waves, tsunamis have extremely long wavelengths (up to 200 kilometers) and periods ranging from 10 minutes to one hour, allowing them to travel across entire oceans without losing significant energy. Tsunami height in deep waters is generally small (less than one meter), making their passage practically invisible to ships at sea, but their transformation at the coast can be catastrophic.

The propagation of a tsunami from its generation zone to the coast occurs in several well-defined phases. In deep waters, the tsunami travels as a non-dispersive deformation wave, with velocity determined by water depth according to the formula v = sqrt(g*h), where g is the acceleration of gravity and h is the depth. As the tsunami approaches the coast and the depth decreases, its speed decreases, its wavelength shortens, and its amplitude increases dramatically, a process known as shoaling. At the coast, the tsunami can manifest as a series of waves that cause coastal flooding, extremely strong rip currents, and the destruction of infrastructure and ecosystems. The maximum height of a tsunami at the coast depends on factors such as the magnitude and location of the earthquake, the geometry of the coast, the water depth, and the topography of the seafloor, potentially exceeding 30 meters in extreme cases.

Tsunamis represent one of the most devastating natural threats to coastal populations, having caused more than 250,000 deaths in the last 150 years. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, generated by a magnitude 9.1 earthquake off the coast of Sumatra, was one of the deadliest natural disasters in modern history, causing the deaths of approximately 230,000 people in 14 countries. The 2011 Japan tsunami, generated by a magnitude 9.0 earthquake off the coast of Tohoku, caused the deaths of nearly 20,000 people, destroyed coastal infrastructure, and triggered the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident. Tsunami warning systems, such as the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center and the Indian Ocean monitoring system, use networks of seismic sensors, DART buoys, and numerical models to detect and predict the arrival of tsunamis, providing evacuation time for coastal populations.

### 4.3 Wave energy

Wave energy is a renewable energy source that harnesses the movement of ocean waves to generate electricity. The ocean stores an enormous amount of energy in the form of waves, with an estimated theoretical potential of several terawatts on a global scale. Wave energy capture devices convert the kinetic and potential energy of waves into electrical energy through different mechanisms, including pressure oscillators, oscillating water columns, point absorbers, and overtopping devices. These devices can be installed nearshore or offshore, and their efficiency depends on the characteristics of the local wave climate, water depth, and coastal configuration.

The most developed wave energy devices include oscillating water columns (OWC), which consist of partially submerged chambers where waves compress and decompress trapped air, driving turbines that generate electricity. Oscillating water column devices are especially popular on the coasts of Portugal, Spain, and Australia, where pilot and commercial plants are in operation. Other devices include attenuator devices (such as the Pelamis), which use the relative motion between floating sections to drive hydraulics and generate electricity, and point absorbers, which capture wave energy from all directions through the movement of floating buoys. Wave energy offers advantages such as the high energy density of water, the predictability of waves, and low greenhouse gas emissions, but also technical and economic challenges such as marine corrosion, biofouling, extreme sea conditions, and high installation and maintenance costs.

The development of wave energy is advancing rapidly in several coastal countries, with projects in the research, development, and demonstration phases. Spain has the WaveCat in Galicia, an attenuator-type device that has demonstrated the technological viability of wave energy capture in the Atlantic. Portugal has been a pioneer in the installation of offshore floating devices, with the Aguçadoura project that operated briefly in 2008. Australia, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Japan are developing multiple wave energy projects on their coasts, with public and private investments aimed at overcoming technical and economic barriers. Despite the difficulties, wave energy has the potential to significantly contribute to the energy mix of coastal regions, providing a predictable, clean, and sustainable energy source complementary to other renewable sources such as wind and solar.


## Chapter 5: Tides

### 5.1 Tidal mechanics

Tides are the phenomenon of periodic rise and fall of sea level caused by gravitational interaction between the Earth, the Moon, and the Sun. The Moon, due to its proximity, exerts the main tidal force, while the Sun contributes approximately 46 percent of the lunar tidal force. When the Moon and the Sun are aligned with the Earth (new moon and full moon phases), their forces combine and produce high-amplitude tides called spring tides. When they are at a right angle (first quarter and last quarter), their effects partially counteract each other and low-amplitude tides called neap tides occur. The tidal cycle follows a semidiurnal pattern on most coasts, with two high tides and two low tides per day, separated by approximately 12 hours and 25 minutes.

Tidal amplitude varies enormously depending on local geography, water depth, and coastal configuration. In some bays and estuaries, the shape of the terrain can amplify the tidal amplitude, creating macrotides with amplitudes exceeding 10 meters. The Bay of Fundy in Canada experiences the largest tides in the world, with amplitudes that can reach 16 meters, equivalent to the height of a five-story building. In contrast, in some enclosed seas such as the Mediterranean Sea, tidal amplitudes are very small (less than 30 centimeters) due to their limited connection with the Atlantic Ocean and system resonance. The tidal currents that accompany water level variations can be extremely strong in straits and river mouths, creating dangerous conditions for navigation.

Tides play a crucial role in coastal ecosystem dynamics and have important ecological implications. The intertidal zone, which is the area exposed between high tide and low tide, is a unique habitat that experiences regular cycles of immersion and emersion. Organisms inhabiting this zone, such as limpets, mussels, barnacles, sea urchins, and algae, have developed physiological and behavioral adaptations to survive the extreme conditions of air exposure, temperature variations, and desiccation. Tides also influence sediment distribution, nutrient transport, and coastal biological productivity, creating a great diversity of habitats that include rocky shores, sand flats, seagrass meadows, and mudflats (estuaries where fine sediment predominates).

### 5.2 Tides and navigation

Tides have a decisive influence on maritime navigation, as they affect water depth in ports, channels, estuaries, and coastal zones. Deep-draft vessels, such as cargo ships and tankers, can be affected by tidal variations and need to plan their port entries and exits to coincide with high tide to take advantage of the greatest available depth. In some important ports, such as the one on the River Thames in London, deep-draft ships can only enter or exit during high tide hours, requiring precise planning based on tide tables and prediction systems. Navigation channels such as the Panama Canal and the Suez Canal are also affected by tides, though to a lesser extent than estuaries and rivers.

Tidal currents represent a significant challenge for navigation, especially in straits, channels, and coastal zones with large tidal amplitudes. These currents can reach velocities of several knots (kilometers per hour) and create dangerous conditions for small boats and people. The Strait of Pas-de-Calais, between France and England, is famous for its extremely strong tidal currents (up to 7.5 knots) that have caused numerous shipwrecks throughout history. Experienced sailors must know the characteristics of tidal currents in their navigation zones and use current tables, nautical charts, and global positioning systems (GPS) to plan safe routes. Modern maritime information systems integrate real-time tidal and current data to help mariners make informed decisions.

Tides also have implications for the construction and maintenance of port infrastructure. Wharves, breakwaters, jetties, and other coastal installations must be designed to withstand water level variations and the action of tidal currents. Ports in macrotidal zones require wharves with large vertical differences, floating access ramps, and mooring systems that adapt to level variations. Coastal erosion caused by tidal currents is another factor that must be considered in infrastructure design, especially in zones with fine sediments and soft coasts. Coastal engineers use numerical tidal and current models to predict the impact of water level variations on infrastructure and design appropriate solutions.

### 5.3 Tides and tidal energy

Tidal energy is a renewable energy source that harnesses the kinetic energy of tidal currents and the potential energy of water level variations to generate electricity. Tidal power plants can be classified into two main types: tidal barrages, which impound water during high tide and release it during low tide through turbines, and tidal stream generators, which capture the energy of tidal currents directly. The world's first commercial tidal power plant, the La Rance barrage in France, was inaugurated in 1966 and has an installed capacity of 240 megawatts, producing electricity continuously ever since. Other important installations include the Sihwa Lake plant in South Korea, the world's largest at 254 megawatts, and the experimental MeyGen plant in Scotland, which uses submerged turbines in the Pentland Firth strait.

Tidal energy offers significant advantages over other renewable energy sources, including its high predictability, energy density (water is 800 times denser than air), and continuous availability (unlike wind and solar, tides are completely predictable). However, it also has important challenges, such as the limited availability of locations with sufficiently large tidal amplitudes, the high cost of construction and maintenance of installations, and potential environmental impacts on coastal ecosystems. The construction of tidal barrages can alter intertidal habitats, modify sedimentation regimes, and affect marine fauna, including migratory fish, waterfowl, and marine mammals. For these reasons, new tidal energy installations must undergo rigorous environmental impact assessments and be designed to minimize negative effects on the environment.

The global potential for tidal energy is significant, with estimates suggesting a technical capacity of between 100 and 800 gigawatts worldwide. Countries with the greatest potential include Canada, the United Kingdom, France, South Korea, Argentina, Australia, and Russia, which possess coasts with large tidal amplitudes and favorable geography for installation. The International Energy Agency estimates that tidal energy could provide up to 2 percent of global electricity by 2050, a modest but significant contribution to the renewable energy mix. The development of new technologies, such as tidal stream turbines and oscillating devices, is reducing costs and improving the efficiency of tidal energy capture, opening new possibilities for its expansion in the coming decades.


## Chapter 6: Marine Life

### 6.1 Planktonic organisms and primary productivity

Marine plankton comprises the set of organisms that live in the water column and have limited horizontal displacement capability, depending heavily on currents for their distribution. Phytoplankton, which includes microscopic algae such as diatoms, dinoflagellates, and coccolithophores, is the photosynthetic component of plankton and represents the base of the marine food chain. These organisms perform photosynthesis in the surface layer of the ocean, converting light energy into organic matter and producing approximately 50 percent of atmospheric oxygen and capturing a significant portion of the carbon dioxide emitted by human activities. The primary productivity of oceanic phytoplankton is estimated at about 50 gigatons of carbon per year, an amount comparable to that of all terrestrial ecosystems combined.

Zooplankton, which includes microscopic animal organisms such as copepods, krill, fish larvae, and jellyfish, feeds on phytoplankton and other organic matter suspended in the water, transferring energy along the marine food chain. Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba) is one of the most abundant organisms on the planet, with an estimated biomass of hundreds of millions of tons, and constitutes the main food source for whales, seals, penguins, and fish in the Southern Ocean. The plankton cycle, known as the biological pump, transports carbon from the surface to the depths of the ocean through the production of sinking organic particles, including feces, carcasses of dead organisms, and aggregates of organic matter. This carbon export process is fundamental for regulating global climate and nutrient distribution in the water column.

The ocean's primary productivity is not uniform, but presents significant regional variations determined by nutrient availability, sunlight, and water temperature. The most productive oceanic zones are coastal upwelling areas, such as the coasts of Peru, California, and West Africa, where winds drive the sinking of surface water and the ascent of deep water rich in nutrients. Convergence zones, such as the Subantarctic Convergence and the South Atlantic Convergence Zone, are also areas of high productivity due to nutrient accumulation and water mass mixing. In contrast, the central ocean gyres, such as the North Pacific and North Atlantic gyres, are areas of low productivity (ocean deserts) where vertical stratification prevents the ascent of nutrients from the depths. Seasonal variations in primary productivity, known as phytoplankton blooms, are especially pronounced in temperate and polar regions, where the combination of available light and nutrients allows explosions of phytoplankton growth in spring and summer.

### 6.2 Fish and large predators

Fish represent the most diverse and numerous group of marine vertebrates, with more than 30,000 described species inhabiting all oceanic environments, from shallow coasts to abyssal trenches. Bony fish (Osteichthyes) constitute the majority of known species and are divided into two major groups: actinopterygians (ray-finned fish) and sarcopterygians (lobe-finned fish). Sharks and rays (Chondrichthyes), which possess cartilaginous rather than bony skeletons, are top predators in many marine ecosystems and play a crucial role in maintaining ecological balance. The great white shark (Carcharodon carcharias), the hammerhead shark (Sphyrna mokarran), and the whale shark (Rhincodon typus) are some of the most iconic species, although many of them are threatened by overfishing and habitat degradation.

Marine mammals include cetaceans (whales, dolphins, and porpoises), pinnipeds (seals, sea lions, and walruses), and sirenians (dugongs and manatees), and represent some of the largest and most charismatic predators of the oceans. Baleen whales, such as the blue whale (Balaenoptera musculus), the largest animal that has ever existed (up to 30 meters in length), feed on krill and other small crustaceans, filtering enormous quantities of water through their baleen plates. Toothed whales, such as the sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus), the largest delphinid, feed on squid and deep-water fish. Dolphins, belonging to the family Delphinidae, are highly intelligent and social marine mammals, with species such as the bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) inhabiting all the oceans and being the subject of extensive behavioral and cognitive studies.

Seabirds represent an important group of coastal and pelagic predators that depend on the oceans for food. The most representative families include petrels, shearwaters, albatrosses, cormorants, pelicans, and penguins. The wandering albatross (Diomedea exulans) has the largest wingspan of all birds (up to 3.5 meters) and can travel thousands of kilometers in search of food without landing. Penguins, which inhabit exclusively the Southern Hemisphere, are flightless birds adapted to swimming and hunting fish and krill in the cold waters of the Southern Ocean. Seabirds play an important role in the transfer of nutrients between the ocean and the land, as their nesting colonies on coastal islands deposit guano rich in nitrogen and phosphorus that fertilizes coastal soils.

### 6.3 Fragile marine ecosystems

Coral reefs are marine ecosystems of extraordinary biodiversity and productivity, built by colonies of coral polyps that secrete calcium carbonate to form reef structures. Corals have a symbiotic association with microscopic algae called zooxanthellae (Symbiodinium), which provide up to 90 percent of their energy through photosynthesis and give them their characteristic color. Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor but harbor more than 25 percent of all known marine species, making them the most biodiverse ecosystems on the planet. Australia's Great Barrier Reef, with 2,300 kilometers of length, is the largest biological structure in the world and was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981.

Kelp forests are marine ecosystems dominated by giant brown algae of the genus Macrocystis and other laminariales, which form dense and productive underwater structures extending from the seafloor to the surface. These forests, which can reach lengths of more than 45 meters, provide refuge, food, and breeding areas for a wide variety of marine organisms, including fish, invertebrates, marine mammals, and seabirds. Kelp forests are distributed along cold and temperate coasts worldwide, from California and Chile to southern Australia and South Africa, and are especially abundant on the coasts of California, where the combination of cold water and nutrients favors their development. These ecosystems are vulnerable to climate change, as rising sea temperatures can cause the disappearance of kelp forests and their replacement by less productive red algae forests.

Coastal wetlands, including mangroves, seagrass meadows, and salt marshes, are transitional ecosystems between land and sea that perform essential ecological functions. Mangroves, distributed along tropical and subtropical coasts, are forests of halophytic trees that grow on saline or brackish substrates, providing coastal protection against erosion and flooding, and harboring great biodiversity of birds, fish, and crustaceans. Seagrass meadows, such as Posidonia oceanica in the Mediterranean and Zostera marina in the Atlantic, are marine angiosperms that form highly productive underwater meadows, act as carbon sinks, and provide habitat for numerous species, including the juveniles of many commercially important species. Salt marshes are ecosystems dominated by salt-tolerant plants that cover the intertidal zones of temperate coasts, filtering sediments, absorbing nutrients, and providing feeding areas for migratory birds.


## Chapter 7: Exploration and Oceanographic Technology

### 7.1 Modern oceanographic instruments

Modern oceanography depends on a wide range of instruments and technologies to measure and monitor the physical, chemical, and biological properties of the oceans. High-precision oceanographic thermometers measure water temperature with resolutions of thousandths of a degree Celsius, allowing the detection of subtle variations that indicate changes in water masses and ocean circulation. Salinometers measure water salinity through the determination of electrical conductivity, a property directly related to the concentration of dissolved salts. Water column profilers, such as CTD (Conductivity, Temperature, Depth) instruments, are deployed from ships or buoys to measure vertical profiles of these properties from the surface to the ocean floor, providing essential data for understanding the structure of water masses.

Oceanographic buoys are monitoring platforms deployed in the oceans to collect real-time data on oceanic and meteorological conditions. Drift buoys move with currents and transmit temperature, pressure, and position data by satellite, providing information about the surface circulation of the oceans. Moored buoys are fixed to the ocean floor and measure variables at a fixed point over time, including temperature, salinity, current, and wave conditions. DART buoys (Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis) are underwater seismic sensors that detect tsunamis and transmit real-time alerts to monitoring centers. The Argo network, deployed since the year 2000, consists of more than 4,000 robot buoys distributed across all the oceans that measure temperature and salinity profiles down to 2,000 meters depth, providing oceanographic data of global scope.

Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUV) and remotely operated vehicles (ROV) have revolutionized the exploration of the ocean depths, allowing access to zones that were previously inaccessible to divers and manned submersibles. AUVs are robotic vehicles that can be programmed to conduct survey, data collection, and seafloor mapping missions autonomously, without human intervention. ROVs, on the other hand, are connected to a support ship through an umbilical cable that provides power, communications, and real-time control, allowing visual inspection, sample collection, and complex operations at depths up to 11,000 meters. These vehicles are equipped with high-definition cameras, sensors, hydraulic manipulators, and specialized tools that allow maintenance work, instrument installation, and biological sampling in the depths of the ocean.

### 7.2 Oceanographic satellites

Oceanographic satellites are a fundamental tool for global ocean monitoring, providing high-resolution data on the ocean surface that complement in situ measurements from buoys, ships, and seafloor sensors. Satellite altimeters, such as those on the Jason mission and the Copernicus Sentinel-6 mission, measure sea level height with millimeter precision, detecting variations caused by ocean currents, thermal expansion of water, tides, and other oceanographic phenomena. These data allow mapping of global ocean circulation, monitoring of sea level rise associated with climate change, and detection of phenomena such as El Niño and La Niña, which have a significant impact on global climate. Satellite altimetry has been essential for understanding ocean dynamics on a global scale and continues to provide crucial data for climate models and oceanographic predictions.

Satellite radiometers measure sea surface temperature (SST) through the detection of infrared radiation emitted by the water, providing global temperature maps with resolutions of several kilometers and daily frequencies. Missions such as MODIS aboard the Aqua and Terra satellites, and the radiometers on the Meteosat missions, allow monitoring of sea surface temperature variations in real time, detecting warming and cooling patterns that influence marine ecosystems and weather patterns. Ocean color sensors, such as MODIS OC3M and Sentinel-3 OLCI, measure the spectral reflectance of water to estimate chlorophyll-a concentration (an indicator of phytoplankton biomass), dissolved organic matter, and suspended particles. These data are essential for assessing the ocean's primary productivity, detecting harmful algal blooms, and monitoring the health of marine ecosystems.

Earth observation satellites also provide data on wind, waves, sea ice, and other oceanographic phenomena that are important for navigation, fishing, and coastal management. Satellite scatterometers, such as QuikSCAT and the scatterometers on MetOp missions, measure wind speed and direction over the ocean surface, providing essential data for weather and oceanographic forecasting. Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) image sensors, such as those on Sentinel-1 missions, provide high-resolution images of the ocean surface that allow detection of currents, waves, sea ice, and the presence of oil in the water. The integration of data from multiple satellites and in situ sources allows the creation of high-quality oceanographic products, such as ocean temperature, salinity, and current fields, which are used by weather agencies, research centers, and maritime industries worldwide.

### 7.3 Deep-sea exploration

The exploration of the ocean depths is one of the most challenging and exciting scientific frontiers of the present day, requiring technology capable of withstanding extreme conditions of pressure, darkness, and cold. The bathyscaphe Trieste, funded by the United States Navy, was the first vehicle to reach the bottom of the Mariana Trench in 1960, carrying Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh to a depth of 10,916 meters. For nearly 50 years, this depth record was not surpassed until 2012, when the Deepsea Challenger, a single-seat vehicle designed by filmmaker James Cameron, reached 10,908 meters in the same trench. In 2019, Victor Vescovo's submarine Limiting Factor set the current record at 10,928 meters, surpassing the deepest known point in the oceans.

Manned submersibles, such as the Alvin of the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution and the Shinkai 6500 of the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology, have played a fundamental role in the exploration of the ocean depths, allowing direct observation, sample collection, and installation of scientific instruments. These submarines, which can reach depths of up to 6,500 meters, are equipped with cameras, lights, manipulators, and sensors that allow scientists to study deep-sea ecosystems, including hydrothermal fields, abyssal trenches, and abyssal plains. ROVs, such as the Jason of the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution and the Victor 6000 of IFREMER, are unmanned vehicles connected to support ships through umbilical cables, providing a more versatile and safer platform for deep-sea exploration.

The exploration of the ocean depths has revealed a surprising and largely unknown world, including ecosystems that defy our understanding of the limits of life. Hydrothermal fields, discovered in 1977 on the East Pacific Rise, are cracks in the ocean floor where hot, mineral-rich water rises from inside the Earth, creating unique ecosystems dominated by chemosynthetic organisms that obtain energy from the oxidation of chemical compounds such as hydrogen sulfide. These ecosystems, which exist in total darkness and under extreme pressures, harbor communities of tubeworms, mollusks, crustaceans, and fish adapted to live without sunlight or oxygen. The existence of life in these extreme environments has had profound implications for understanding the origins of life on Earth and the search for extraterrestrial life on celestial bodies such as Jupiter's Europa and Saturn's Enceladus.


## Chapter 8: Climate Change and the Oceans

### 8.1 Ocean warming

Ocean warming is one of the most significant effects of anthropogenic climate change, as the oceans have absorbed more than 90 percent of the excess heat generated by greenhouse gas emissions since the preindustrial era. The average temperature of the oceans has increased by approximately 0.6 degrees Celsius since 1900, with most warming occurring in the surface layers (up to 700 meters) but with growing evidence of warming at greater depths. This ocean warming has multiple consequences: it contributes to sea level rise through thermal expansion of water, alters the distribution of water masses and ocean currents, affects biological productivity and the distribution of marine species, and can intensify tropical cyclones and other extreme weather phenomena.

Ocean warming is not uniform, but presents significant regional variations determined by ocean currents, continental configuration, and atmospheric circulation patterns. Tropical regions, especially the western Pacific Ocean, have experienced warming faster than the global average, with surface temperatures exceeding 30 degrees Celsius for prolonged periods, which has caused mass coral bleaching in reefs worldwide. Warming of the North Atlantic, on the other hand, has been uneven, with areas of accelerated warming (such as the Labrador Sea) and others where temperatures have remained stable or even decreased (such as the subpolar North Atlantic region), possibly due to changes in thermohaline circulation. Continuous monitoring of ocean temperature through Argo buoys, satellites, and ship measurements is fundamental for understanding the dynamics of ocean warming and its implications for global climate.

Ocean warming has cascading effects on marine ecosystems, altering the distribution, abundance, and behavior of marine species. Many species of fish, invertebrates, and marine mammals are shifting toward higher latitudes in response to rising temperatures, seeking suitable thermal conditions for survival and reproduction. Coral bleaching, caused by thermal stress associated with rising sea temperatures, has caused the loss of extensive areas of coral reefs worldwide, with devastating consequences for biodiversity and coastal communities that depend on the ecosystem services provided by reefs. Ocean warming also affects primary productivity by altering the vertical stratification of water, reducing the mixing of nutrients from the depths to the surface and decreasing phytoplankton productivity in many oceanic regions.

### 8.2 Ocean acidification

Ocean acidification is the decrease in ocean pH caused by the absorption of atmospheric carbon dioxide by seawater. The oceans have absorbed approximately one-third of the CO2 emitted by human activities since the preindustrial era, which has caused a reduction in ocean surface pH of about 0.1 units, equivalent to a 26 percent increase in acidity. The chemical reaction that causes acidification involves the dissolution of CO2 in seawater to form carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen ions and bicarbonate ions, reducing the availability of carbonate ions necessary for the formation of calcium carbonate structures by marine organisms. Ocean acidification is one of the least visible but most concerning aspects of climate change, with potentially devastating consequences for marine ecosystems and the industries that depend on them.

The organisms most affected by ocean acidification are those that build calcium carbonate structures, including corals, mollusks, crustaceans, echinoderms, and some phytoplankton. Reef corals, which build their skeletons from aragonite (a form of calcium carbonate), are especially vulnerable to acidification, as the reduction in carbonate ion availability hinders calcification and can cause the erosion of existing skeletons. Bivalve mollusks such as oysters, mussels, and clams, which are important for aquaculture and commercial fishing, can also be affected by acidification, with reductions in growth rate, larval viability, and disease resistance. The combination of acidification and ocean warming represents a double threat to coral reefs and other calcifying ecosystems, with potentially irreversible consequences for marine biodiversity and coastal economies.

The effects of ocean acidification on the marine food chain are potentially profound and far-reaching. Phytoplankton, which includes calcifying organisms such as coccolithophores and foraminifera, can be affected by acidification, with consequences for primary productivity and carbon export to the ocean depths. Antarctic krill, a key crustacean in the Southern Ocean food chain, uses calcium carbonate to build its exoskeleton and may be vulnerable to acidification, with potential cascading effects on the whales, penguins, and fish that depend on it as a food source. Ocean acidification can also affect the hearing and behavior of fish and marine mammals, as excess hydrogen ions can interfere with sound transmission in water. Research on the effects of ocean acidification is an active and urgent field, with direct implications for marine resource management and climate change policy.

### 8.3 Sea level rise

Sea level rise is a direct consequence of climate change that threatens billions of people living in low-lying coastal zones worldwide. Mean sea level has risen approximately 20 centimeters since 1900, with the rate of rise accelerating in recent decades, from 1.4 millimeters per year in the first half of the 20th century to 3.6 millimeters per year between 2006 and 2015. The main causes of sea level rise are thermal expansion of oceanic water (which contributes approximately one-third of the total rise) and the melting of glaciers and polar ice caps (which contributes the remainder). The contribution from the melting of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets is increasing and is expected to be the primary cause of sea level rise in the coming decades.

Projections for sea level rise by the end of the 21st century vary depending on greenhouse gas emission scenarios and climate system responses, but most models predict a rise of between 0.3 and 1 meter by 2100 under a moderate emission scenario. The most pessimistic scenarios, which include the potential collapse of the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, suggest that the rise could be significantly greater, with possibilities of several meters by the end of the century. A one-meter sea level rise would flood extensive areas of low-lying coasts, including river deltas such as the Nile and the Ganges-Brahmaputra, coastal cities such as Amsterdam, Venice, and Miami, and small Pacific and Caribbean islands that could disappear completely beneath the water.

The consequences of sea level rise for coastal populations and ecosystems are potentially devastating and include coastal flooding, beach erosion, saltwater intrusion into aquifers, loss of coastal habitats, and displacement of human communities. Saltwater intrusion into coastal aquifers is an especially concerning problem in regions where dependence on groundwater is high, such as in Bangladesh, Vietnam, and the Caribbean island nations. Coastal ecosystems such as mangroves, seagrass meadows, and salt marshes, which provide coastal protection, capture carbon, and harbor great biodiversity, can be severely affected by the rate of sea level rise if they do not have space to migrate inland. Adaptation to sea level rise requires measures such as the construction of dikes and coastal defenses, the restoration of natural ecosystems, coastal land-use planning, and, in some cases, the resettlement of vulnerable communities.


## Chapter 9: Ocean Resources and Sustainable Management

### 9.1 Fishing and aquaculture

Marine fishing is one of the oldest economic activities of humanity and continues to be an essential source of food for billions of people worldwide. Global fishing has reached unsustainable exploitation levels, with 34.2 percent of fish stocks exploited beyond their biological limits, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO). Overfishing has caused the collapse of numerous historical fisheries, such as the North Atlantic cod fishery, which collapsed in the 1990s due to overexploitation and has still not fully recovered. The effects of overfishing are not limited to the reduction of fish stocks, but include the degradation of marine ecosystems, the alteration of food chains, and the loss of biodiversity.

Aquaculture, or the cultivation of aquatic organisms, has grown rapidly in recent decades in response to the stagnation of wild fisheries and the growing demand for seafood. Aquaculture currently represents more than 50 percent of the total seafood consumed in the world, and is expected to continue growing to meet the demand of a growing global population. The main aquaculture products include salmon, tilapia, shrimp, oysters, mussels, and algae cultivated in coastal and inland facilities. Intensive aquaculture presents significant environmental challenges, including pollution from excess nutrients and chemicals, the spread of diseases to wild populations, escape of cultivated species, and degradation of coastal habitats such as mangroves.

The sustainable management of fisheries resources requires the implementation of policies based on scientific evidence, including catch quotas based on stock assessments, measures for the protection of threatened species, establishment of marine protected areas, and regulation of destructive fishing practices such as bottom trawling. Regional Fisheries Management Agreements (RFMAs) are international instruments that coordinate the management of transboundary and migratory fish stocks, establishing quotas, technical measures, and inspection and surveillance programs. Marine protected areas (MPAs) are ocean zones where fishing activities and other human activities are restricted or prohibited to conserve marine ecosystems and restore fish populations. Currently, less than 8 percent of the world's oceans are designated as marine protected areas, a figure far below the 30 percent target for 2030 established by the Convention on Biological Diversity.

### 9.2 Ocean mineral resources

The oceans harbor a variety of mineral resources that include oil and natural gas on continental shelves, manganese nodules on the abyssal plains, hydrothermal sulfides at mid-ocean ridges, and iron sands on coasts. Oil and natural gas from continental shelves represent a significant portion of global energy production, with key regions including the Gulf of Mexico, the North Sea, the coast of Brazil, and the South China Sea. The exploration and production of hydrocarbons in deep waters (below 500 meters) has advanced significantly in recent decades, with technology that allows operation under conditions of high pressure and temperature on the ocean floor. However, oil spills, such as the Deepwater Horizon in 2010, have highlighted the environmental risks associated with deep-water hydrocarbon production.

Polymetallic nodules are mineral concentrations that form slowly on the deep ocean floor, containing manganese, nickel, copper, and cobalt, metals that are essential for the manufacture of batteries and other technologies. These nodules, which are the size of a potato and grow at a rate of millimeters per million years, are found in large concentrations in the abyssal plains of the Pacific Ocean, particularly in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone between Mexico and Hawaii. Hydrothermal sulfides, which form in the chimneys of hydrothermal fields, contain precious metals such as gold, silver, copper, and zinc, and their composition makes them attractive targets for submarine mining. The mining of deep-sea mineral resources presents significant technical and environmental challenges, including impacts on deep-sea ecosystems, management of disturbed sediments, and regulation of activities in international areas.

The regulation of mining on the international seabed is the responsibility of the International Seabed Authority (ISA), an international organization established by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea to administer the resources of the seabed in international areas. The ISA has granted exploration permits for polymetallic nodules and hydrothermal sulfides in several areas of the ocean, and is developing a code of conduct for mining that will include environmental protection measures. However, environmental organizations and some governments have expressed concern about the potential environmental impacts of deep-sea mining, arguing that too little is known about deep-sea ecosystems to ensure that mining can be carried out sustainably. Proposed moratoria on deep-sea mining have generated intense debate about the balance between the need for mineral resources for the energy transition and the protection of deep marine ecosystems.

### 9.3 Marine conservation

Marine conservation is the set of strategies and actions aimed at protecting and restoring marine ecosystems, maintaining biodiversity, and ensuring the sustainable use of ocean resources. The creation of marine protected areas (MPAs) is one of the most effective tools for marine conservation, as by restricting or prohibiting fishing activities and other human activities, they allow the recovery of species populations, habitat restoration, and the maintenance of biodiversity. Examples of successful MPAs include the Papahanaumokuakea Biosphere Reserve in Hawaii, the Ascension Island National Marine Park in the South Atlantic, and the Cabo de Palos e Islas Hormigas Marine Reserve in Spain, which have demonstrated that effective protection of marine areas can lead to the recovery of fish populations and improved ecosystem health.

The restoration of degraded marine ecosystems is another essential component of marine conservation, including the restoration of coral reefs, kelp forests, mangroves, and seagrass meadows. Coral restoration projects involve the cultivation of coral colonies in underwater nurseries and their transplantation to degraded areas, a process known as underwater gardening. Kelp forest restoration involves the removal of predators such as the crown-of-thorns starfish (Acanthaster planci) and the seeding of new algae in areas where the forests have disappeared. Mangrove restoration includes the planting of mangrove seeds or seedlings in coastal areas where the forests have been destroyed by human activity, providing coastal protection and habitat for marine fauna. These restoration efforts are necessary but complex, requiring detailed knowledge of ecosystem ecology and careful planning to ensure their success.

The fight against marine pollution is fundamental for ocean conservation, as pollutants such as plastics, chemicals, agricultural nutrients, and hydrocarbons have negative effects on marine ecosystems and human health. Plastic pollution is one of the most urgent environmental problems, with more than 8 million tons of plastics reaching the oceans each year, forming plastic islands such as the Great Pacific Garbage Patch and affecting hundreds of marine species that ingest them or become entangled in them. Pollution from agricultural nutrients, from fertilizer runoff and wastewater, causes eutrophication of coastal areas, creating dead zones where lack of oxygen kills marine organisms. Hydrocarbon pollution, whether from accidental spills or regular operation of platforms and ships, has devastating effects on coastal ecosystems and marine fauna, including birds, marine mammals, and fish.


## Chapter 10: Oceanography and Society

### 10.1 Oceanography and climate change

Oceanography plays a fundamental role in understanding and predicting climate change, as the oceans are the planet's largest heat and carbon reservoir and largely regulate global climate. Global climate models incorporate ocean dynamics to simulate thermohaline circulation, heat and gas exchange between the ocean and the atmosphere, and the effects of ocean warming on sea level, precipitation patterns, and the frequency of extreme weather phenomena. Oceanographic data collected by buoys, satellites, and scientific expeditions are used to validate and improve these models, providing an empirical basis for climate predictions and mitigation and adaptation policies. International cooperation in the collection and sharing of oceanographic data, through organizations such as the International Hydrographic Organization and the Ocean Research Program, is essential for advancing climate science.

The oceans act as a carbon sink that absorbs a significant portion of CO2 emitted by human activities, partially alleviating global warming. However, the oceans' carbon absorption capacity is decreasing as water temperatures rise and ocean acidification reduces the efficiency of the biological pump. Oceanographic research seeks to quantify the current and future capacity of the oceans to absorb carbon, including the study of blue carbon (the capture and storage of carbon in marine ecosystems) and the potential of ocean energy to replace fossil fuels. Nature-based solutions for climate change, such as the restoration of mangroves, seagrass meadows, and kelp forests, can provide additional carbon capture while improving biodiversity and protecting coasts.

Adaptation to climate change in coastal zones requires detailed oceanographic knowledge to plan the protection of human communities and ecosystems. Coastal flood maps, based on sea level rise and wave models, are essential tools for urban planning and emergency management. Early warning systems for tsunamis, tropical cyclones, and coastal floods, which integrate oceanographic and meteorological data, are fundamental for protecting coastal populations from the effects of climate change. International cooperation in technology transfer and capacity building is crucial to help developing countries, which are the most vulnerable to the effects of climate change in coastal zones.

### 10.2 Oceanographic education

Oceanographic education is essential for creating an informed citizenry committed to ocean protection and sustainable management of marine resources. Oceanographic education programs range from basic teaching in schools to the training of specialized researchers in academic institutions and research centers. The dissemination of oceanographic science through science museums, aquariums, documentaries, and digital platforms is fundamental for raising awareness about the importance of the oceans and the challenges they face. World Oceans Day, celebrated on June 8, is an opportunity to raise public awareness about the need to protect the oceans and promote concrete actions to reduce pollution, overfishing, and the impact of climate change.

Oceanographic research centers, such as the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution in the United States, the Instituto de Ciencias del Mar in Spain, and the National Oceanography Centre in the United Kingdom, play a crucial role in training researchers and disseminating oceanographic science. These centers organize scientific expeditions, publish high-impact research, and collaborate with industry and governments to apply oceanographic knowledge to solving real-world problems. Marine education programs, such as summer camps, research scholarships, and international exchange programs, provide opportunities for young people to become interested in oceanography and contribute to advancing knowledge about the oceans. Investment in oceanographic education is an investment in the future of the planet, as the environmental challenges we face require a population with solid knowledge of the oceans and their role in the Earth system.

Oceanography is a young but extraordinarily dynamic science that has experienced extraordinary advances in recent decades thanks to technological development and international cooperation. Discoveries made in the ocean depths, including hydrothermal fields, extremophilic life, and the dynamics of abyssal trenches, have expanded our understanding of the limits of life and the planet's geological processes. Understanding ocean circulation and its role in regulating global climate is fundamental for predicting and mitigating the effects of climate change. The protection of marine ecosystems and the sustainable management of ocean resources are urgent challenges that require collaboration between scientists, governments, industries, and citizens. The future of oceanography will depend on our ability to invest in research, technology, and education, ensuring that the next generations have the knowledge and tools necessary to protect and manage the oceans sustainably.


## Chapter 11: Chemical Oceanography

### 11.1 Chemical composition of seawater

Seawater is a complex solution containing a wide variety of dissolved elements, with an average salinity of 35 grams per liter (35 parts per thousand). The most abundant ions in seawater are chloride (55 percent of dissolved salts), sodium (30.6 percent), sulfate (7.7 percent), magnesium (3.7 percent), calcium (1.2 percent), and potassium (1.1 percent). These six ions represent approximately 99.5 percent of all dissolved salts in seawater. The law of constant proportions establishes that the ratio of the major ions in seawater is practically constant in all water masses worldwide, which allows the estimation of total salinity by measuring the concentration of a single ion, such as chloride. This uniformity in ionic composition significantly facilitates oceanographic studies and allows global comparisons of salinity.

In addition to major ions, seawater contains a large number of trace elements in extremely low concentrations, including iron, zinc, copper, manganese, cobalt, and other transition metals that are essential for marine life. Iron, for example, is a limiting nutrient in vast areas of the ocean, especially in southern regions and upwelling zones, where its availability directly controls the primary productivity of phytoplankton. Iron fertilization experiments conducted in the South Pacific and Southern Oceans have demonstrated that the addition of minimal amounts of iron can trigger massive phytoplankton blooms, confirming the limiting role of this element. Halogenated organics, such as bromoform and iodoform, are produced by marine organisms and play an important role in the atmospheric chemistry of the ocean surface, influencing cloud formation and incoming solar radiation.

The distribution of nutrients in the oceanic water column follows well-defined patterns that reflect the interaction between biological, chemical, and physical processes. The main nutrients for marine life include nitrate, phosphate, silicate, and iron, which are used by phytoplankton in the surface layer and recycled in the depths through the decomposition of organic matter. In the epipelagic, nutrient concentrations are generally low due to uptake by phytoplankton, while in the depths concentrations are higher due to the mineralization of organic matter that sinks from the surface. This vertical nutrient gradient is a key factor that controls the ocean's primary productivity and the distribution of water masses in the vertical column.

### 11.2 The oceanic carbon cycle

The oceanic carbon cycle is one of the most important biogeochemical cycles on the planet, regulating the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere and, therefore, global climate. The oceans contain approximately 38,000 gigatons of carbon in the form of dissolved inorganic carbon, more than 50 times the amount of current atmospheric carbon. The exchange of CO2 between the ocean and the atmosphere occurs through the water surface, driven by the difference in partial pressure of CO2 between the two compartments. In polar regions, where the water is cold and can dissolve more CO2, there is a net absorption of atmospheric carbon, while in tropical regions, where the water is warmer and biological activity is high, there is a net release of CO2. This surface exchange is the first step of the so-called biological pump, which transports carbon from the surface to the depths of the ocean.

The biological pump consists of several mechanisms that contribute to the vertical transport of carbon in the water column. The most important mechanism is the production of organic matter by phytoplankton in the surface layer, which is consumed by zooplankton and then exported to the depths in the form of feces, carcasses of dead organisms, and aggregates of organic matter known as marine snow. This sinking organic matter is partially decomposed by microbes in the intermediate layers, releasing CO2 that re-dissolves in the water, and partially reaches the ocean floor where it may remain stored for thousands or millions of years. The efficiency of the biological pump determines what proportion of the carbon absorbed from the atmosphere is stored in the ocean depths rather than returning to the atmosphere, and varies significantly among oceanic regions depending on the composition of the phytoplankton community, water temperature, and nutrient availability.

Ocean acidification, caused by the absorption of anthropogenic CO2, is altering the oceanic carbon cycle with potentially significant consequences for marine ecosystems and global climate. When CO2 dissolves in seawater, it reduces the concentration of carbonate and aragonite ions, which are necessary for the formation of calcium carbonate structures by organisms such as corals, mollusks, and foraminifera. The reduction in the availability of these ions can hinder calcification, weaken existing skeletons, and alter the composition of marine communities. Additionally, acidification can affect the rate of primary production and the efficiency of the biological pump, with implications for the future capacity of the oceans to absorb CO2. Ocean models predict that, under high-emission scenarios, the oceans' carbon absorption capacity could be significantly reduced by the end of the 21st century, creating a positive feedback that would accelerate global warming.

### 11.3 Dissolved oxygen and dead zones

Dissolved oxygen in seawater is essential for the survival of most aquatic organisms, and its distribution in the water column reflects the balance between production processes (photosynthesis) and consumption (respiration and decomposition). Oxygen concentration in the surface layer is generally high due to exchange with the atmosphere and photosynthetic production by phytoplankton. However, as depth increases, oxygen concentration decreases due to organism respiration and the decomposition of organic matter that sinks from the surface. In many areas of the ocean, a minimum oxygen zone occurs at intermediate depths (between 200 and 1,000 meters), known as the oxygen minimum zone (OMZ), where conditions are critical for marine life. OMZs are especially pronounced in the eastern Pacific, the Indian Ocean, and coastal areas with high productivity and upwelling.

Dead zones are coastal or estuarine areas where oxygen concentration decreases to levels insufficient to sustain marine life, generally below 2 milligrams per liter. These zones form when excess nutrients, from agricultural runoff and wastewater, trigger accelerated eutrophication that generates large phytoplankton blooms. When these blooms die, bacterial decomposition of organic matter consumes enormous quantities of oxygen, creating hypoxic or anoxic conditions that kill or displace fish, crustaceans, and other organisms. Currently, more than 700 dead zones are documented worldwide, with a combined area of more than 245,000 square kilometers, and their number and size have increased significantly in recent decades due to increasing nutrient pollution and warming of seawater.

The recovery of dead zones is a complex environmental challenge that requires reducing nutrient inputs to coastal waters. Recovery strategies include improved wastewater treatment, implementation of more sustainable agricultural practices, reduction of fertilizer use, and creation of artificial wetlands that filter nutrients before they reach the sea. The Gulf of Mexico dead zone management program, established in 1997 by the Mississippi-Gulf of Mexico Watershed Task Force, coordinates the efforts of multiple federal and state agencies to reduce the nutrient load from the Mississippi River. However, despite these efforts, the Gulf of Mexico dead zone continues to be one of the largest in the world, reaching areas of more than 20,000 square kilometers in the worst years. The relationship between nutrient pollution, water warming, and dead zone formation underscores the need for an integrated approach to coastal management that addresses multiple sources of environmental stress simultaneously.


## Chapter 12: Marine Oceanography and Human Activity

### 12.1 Maritime transport

Maritime transport is the backbone of international trade, carrying more than 80 percent of global trade by volume and approximately 70 percent by value. The world's merchant fleet consists of more than 50,000 ships that regularly sail the major shipping routes, including containers, bulk carriers, tankers, gas carriers, and general cargo ships. The world's busiest ports include Shanghai, Singapore, Rotterdam, Busan, and Hong Kong, which handle hundreds of millions of tons of cargo per year. The main shipping routes connect the world's major production and consumption centers, including the Asia-Europe route through the Suez Canal, the transpacific route between Asia and North America, and the transatlantic route between Europe and the Americas.

The maritime transport industry faces significant challenges related to environmental pollution, including greenhouse gas emissions, oil pollution, and the introduction of exotic species through ballast water. Merchant ships emit approximately 1 billion tons of CO2 per year, representing about 2.5 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, an amount comparable to that of the entire aviation industry. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has established ambitious targets for reducing maritime transport emissions, including a 50 percent reduction by 2050 compared to 2008 and complete elimination of carbon emissions by the end of the century. To achieve these targets, the industry is exploring alternative fuels such as hydrogen, ammonia, biofuels, and electricity, in addition to improvements in ship energy efficiency and shipping route optimization.

Ballast water pollution is a significant environmental problem affecting marine ecosystems worldwide. When ships discharge their ballast tanks in a port different from where they were filled, they can release marine organisms, bacteria, and viruses that colonize the new environment, potentially causing biological invasions with devastating ecological and economic consequences. The International Convention for the Control and Management of Ships' Ballast Water and Sediments, adopted by the IMO in 2004 and entered into force in 2017, establishes international standards for ballast water treatment and sediment management. The implementation of this convention is significantly reducing the introduction of exotic species through maritime transport, protecting marine biodiversity and coastal ecosystems.

### 12.2 Ocean energy

The oceans represent an enormous and largely unexplored energy resource, with potential for generating renewable energy through multiple technologies, including wave energy, tidal energy, ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC), current energy, and offshore wind energy. Wave energy, which harnesses the movement of waves, has an estimated potential of 2,950 terawatt-hours per year on a global scale, concentrated mainly on the coasts of the North Atlantic and the Pacific. Tidal energy, which uses tidal currents, is especially promising in regions with large tidal amplitudes, such as northeastern Asia, western Europe, and Argentine Patagonia. OTEC, which exploits the temperature difference between the sea surface and the depths, is viable in tropical regions where this difference exceeds 20 degrees Celsius and can provide both electricity and freshwater through desalination.

Offshore wind energy is the most developed and commercially viable ocean energy technology currently, with more than 50 gigawatts installed worldwide, mainly in Europe. Offshore wind farms use turbines anchored to the seabed or floating turbines that harness the constant and strong winds of the open sea to generate electricity. Pioneers in this technology include Denmark, which hosts the world's first offshore wind farm (Horns Rev, 2002), the United Kingdom, Germany, China, and the Netherlands, which have ambitious plans for offshore wind expansion. Floating offshore wind, which uses turbines anchored with flexible cables instead of fixed structures, is opening new possibilities for energy generation in deep waters where conventional turbines are not viable.

The development of ocean energy presents significant technical, economic, and environmental challenges. Marine environments are extremely hostile for energy infrastructure, with saltwater corrosion, biofouling, wave and wind loads, and the risk of oil spills. The cost of installation and maintenance of ocean installations is generally higher than that of onshore installations, although the learning curve and economies of scale are narrowing this gap. The environmental impacts of ocean energy include the alteration of marine habitats, interference with the migration routes of birds and marine mammals, underwater noise during construction, and risks of oil spills. Marine spatial planning, which coordinates the sustainable use of ocean space, is essential for minimizing conflicts between ocean energy, fishing, navigation, and marine conservation.

### 12.3 Coastal tourism and recreation

Coastal and marine tourism is one of the fastest-growing industries in the world, generating trillions of dollars annually and employing millions of people in coastal communities. Beaches are the main attraction of coastal tourism, with billions of visitors per year seeking recreational activities such as swimming, surfing, diving, and sailing. Marine nature tourism, which includes whale watching, dolphin watching, and seabird observation, coral reef diving, and mangrove kayaking, is growing rapidly as a sustainable alternative to conventional sun-and-beach tourism. The most popular marine tourism destinations include the Maldives, the Caribbean, the Mediterranean, Australia, and Southeast Asia, which attract millions of international visitors each year.

Coastal tourism has important environmental implications that require careful management to ensure long-term sustainability. Negative impacts of tourism on coastal ecosystems include beach erosion from excessive trampling, pollution from wastewater and plastics, degradation of coral reefs from irresponsible diving practices, disturbance of marine fauna through noise and contact, and construction of tourist infrastructure in sensitive coastal areas. Sustainable management of coastal tourism requires the implementation of measures such as regulating access to sensitive areas, promoting ecological practices among visitors, investing in wastewater treatment, and creating protection areas where tourism is restricted or controlled.

Underwater tourism, including recreational diving, snorkeling, and visits to aquariums and marine museums, is emerging as a high-value form of nature tourism that can generate significant revenue for coastal communities without causing significant environmental impacts. Dive centers on coral reefs, such as those on Australia's Great Barrier Reef, the coasts of Egypt, and Caribbean countries, attract millions of divers annually who generate hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue. The growth of underwater tourism has driven the creation of underwater art museums, such as the Atlantic Museum of Lanzarote in Spain and the Museo Subacuático de Arte in Mexico, which combine art, education, and marine conservation. These projects demonstrate that underwater tourism can be a positive force for marine conservation when managed responsibly and coordinated with ecosystem protection efforts.


## Chapter 13: Marine Geology

### 13.1 Oceanic sediments

Oceanic sediments are materials that accumulate on the ocean floor over millions of years, forming layers of variable thickness that record the geological, climatic, and biological history of the planet. Sediments are classified based on their origin into four main categories: lithogenic (derived from continental erosion and transported by rivers, winds, and currents), biogenic (composed of remains of marine organisms such as shells, skeletons, and colonies), cosmogenic (derived from meteorites and cosmic dust), and hydrothermogenic (formed by the precipitation of minerals from hydrothermal fluids). Lithogenic sediments predominate near continents and in zones of high fluvial sediment input, such as deltas and continental shelves, while biogenic sediments dominate in oceanic zones far from continental influence, especially in areas of high biological productivity.

The composition of biogenic sediments reflects the composition of the communities of organisms living in the upper water column. Calcite sediments, composed of remains of foraminifera, coccolithophores, and bryozoans, predominate in temperate and tropical zones where calcite saturation is high. Siliceous sediments, composed of remains of diatoms and radiolarians, are more common in upwelling and high-latitude zones where the productivity of these organisms is high. Red clay sediments, composed mainly of eolian clay particles, predominate in the deep zones of the South Pacific, far from continental sources and with low biological productivity. The distribution of sediments on the ocean floor is an indicator of past and present oceanographic, climatic, and geological conditions, and their study provides valuable information about the planet's history.

Oceanic sediment cores are stratigraphic records that allow the reconstruction of the planet's climatic and oceanic history over millions of years. Scientists from the Ocean Drilling Program (ODP) and its successor, the International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP), have extracted sediment cores from oceans around the world, providing an unprecedented source of data on past climate, ocean circulation, and marine life. Oxygen isotope analyses in foraminifera remains allow the estimation of past water temperatures and glacial ice volume, while carbon isotope analyses provide information about the carbon cycle and atmospheric CO2 levels. Sediment cores also contain records of geological events such as volcanic eruptions, meteorite impacts, and changes in plate tectonics, providing a continuous archive of Earth's history.

### 13.2 Processes of oceanic crust formation

Oceanic crust is continuously formed at mid-ocean ridges, where magma from the mantle rises to the surface and cools rapidly in contact with seawater, creating oceanic basalt with pillow lava texture. The creation of oceanic crust at ridges is the main engine of seafloor spreading, a process that moves tectonic plates symmetrically away from both sides of the ridge at speeds of 1 to 16 centimeters per year, depending on the region. Spreading velocity varies significantly between ridges: the East Pacific Rise has one of the fastest spreading rates in the world (up to 16 cm/yr), while the Mid-Atlantic Ridge has a moderate rate (2.5 cm/yr). Differences in spreading velocity influence ridge morphology, with fast-spreading ridges presenting a smooth or nonexistent axial valley, while slow-spreading ridges show a pronounced valley.

The oceanic lithosphere cools and thickens as it moves away from the ridge, following a square-root relationship with age. This cooling causes the crust to subside gradually, creating a depth profile that is shallow near the ridge and deeper in the distant oceanic basins. When the oceanic lithosphere reaches an age of approximately 80 million years, it has reached its equilibrium thickness and temperature, and its depth stabilizes on the abyssal plains at about 5,500 meters below sea level. This relationship between seafloor depth and crustal age was one of the key pieces of evidence that supported the seafloor spreading theory in the 1960s.

Subduction is the process by which oceanic lithosphere sinks beneath another tectonic plate (usually continental) at plate convergence zones, completing the cycle of creation and destruction of oceanic crust. When an oceanic plate subducts, it drags with it marine sediments, trapped water, and volcanic material, which are transported toward the Earth's mantle. The release of water from the subducting plate at depth lowers the melting point of the surrounding mantle, causing the formation of magma that rises and feeds the volcanoes of island arcs and continental volcanic arcs. Island arcs, such as the Japanese Arc, the Mariana Arc, and the Sunda Arc, form at ocean-ocean subduction zones, while continental volcanic arcs, such as the Andes and the Pacific Cordillera, form at ocean-continent subduction zones. Subduction is also responsible for the planet's strongest earthquakes, including megathrust earthquakes that can reach magnitudes exceeding 9.0 and generate devastating tsunamis.

### 13.3 Submarine volcanism

Submarine volcanism is a fundamental geological process that creates new oceanic crust, forms islands, and generates unique ecosystems in the depths of the ocean. Submarine volcanism occurs mainly at mid-ocean ridges, where magma rises from the Earth's mantle through cracks and fractures in the crust, forming pillow lava eruptions that cool rapidly in contact with seawater. These eruptions, which occur at depths of 2,000 to 4,000 meters at the ridges, create new oceanic crust that moves laterally away from the ridge through seafloor spreading. Volcanism at the ridges is generally effusive, with low-risk eruptions that produce low-viscosity basaltic lava, but it can occasionally produce explosive eruptions when magma interacts with seawater.

Submarine volcanism also occurs at subduction zones, where the sinking oceanic plate generates magma that rises and feeds the volcanoes of island arcs. These submarine volcanoes, which form volcanic island chains such as the Mariana Islands, the Aleutian Islands, and the Sunda Arc, are some of the most active and dangerous volcanoes on the planet. Eruptions on submarine volcanoes of island arcs can be extremely explosive, producing submarine pyroclastic flows, ash columns that reach the surface, and localized tsunamis. Submarine volcanism at subduction zones also creates hydrothermal fields, where hot, mineral-rich water rises from the ocean floor, forming metallic sulfide chimneys that harbor unique communities of chemosynthetic organisms.

Submarine volcanic islands, known as guyots or seamounts when they do not reach the surface, are volcanic-origin elevations of the ocean floor that form when a submarine volcano grows tall enough to rise above sea level. These islands, which include Hawaii, the Canary Islands, Iceland, and many Pacific islands, form over hotspots in the Earth's mantle, where columns of magma rise from mantle depths and feed volcanoes on the surface of the tectonic plate moving over them. As the plate moves over the hotspot, a chain of islands and seamounts forms that moves away from the active volcano, such as the Hawaii-Emperor chain that extends more than 6,000 kilometers across the Pacific. The study of submarine volcanism is fundamental for understanding the geological processes that shape the ocean floor, the natural hazards associated with submarine volcanoes, and the unique ecosystems that form around hydrothermal vents.


## Chapter 14: Physical Oceanography

### 14.1 Thermal properties of seawater

Seawater temperature is one of the most important physical properties for understanding ocean dynamics and marine ecosystems. Ocean surface temperature ranges from minus 1.8 degrees Celsius in polar waters to more than 30 degrees Celsius in the tropics, with a latitudinal gradient that reflects the distribution of solar radiation received by the Earth's surface. Seawater temperature depends on latitude, season, depth, proximity to the coast, and the influence of ocean currents. In tropical regions, surface temperature remains relatively stable throughout the year, while in temperate regions it presents significant seasonal variations with summer maxima and winter minima. The vertical distribution of temperature in the water column shows a characteristic pattern with a warm, well-mixed surface layer, a thermocline where temperature drops rapidly with depth, and a cold, stable deep layer.

The thermocline is a transitional layer where temperature drops rapidly with depth, separating the warm surface layer from the cold deep water. The depth and intensity of the thermocline vary significantly depending on latitude, season, and local oceanographic conditions. In tropical regions, the thermocline is generally permanent and found at depths of 100 to 200 meters, while in temperate regions the thermocline develops in spring and summer and breaks down in autumn and winter due to vertical mixing caused by surface cooling and storms. The thermocline acts as a barrier that hinders the exchange of water masses between the surface and the depths, influencing the distribution of nutrients, oxygen, and marine organisms in the water column.

Seawater temperature variations have significant effects on marine ecosystems and global climate. Coral bleaching, caused by water temperature rising above normal thresholds, has caused the loss of extensive areas of coral reefs worldwide, with devastating consequences for marine biodiversity and coastal communities. Warming of seawater also alters the distribution of species, shifting them toward higher latitudes in search of suitable thermal conditions. On a global scale, seawater temperature variations influence ocean circulation, weather patterns, and energy distribution in the climatic system, with direct implications for continental climate and global food security.

### 14.2 Salinity and water masses

Seawater salinity, defined as the total amount of dissolved salts in water, is a fundamental property that influences density, circulation, and oceanic ecosystems. The average salinity of seawater is 35 grams per liter, but presents significant variations depending on latitude, season, depth, and proximity to continents. Zones with higher evaporation, such as the tropics and enclosed seas like the Red Sea and the Mediterranean Sea, present salinities above the average, while zones with high freshwater input, such as estuaries and areas near polar ice melt, present lower salinities. The distribution of salinity on the ocean surface reflects the balance between evaporation and precipitation, and is an important indicator of oceanic circulation patterns and climatic changes.

Water masses are volumes of oceanic water that present homogeneous properties of temperature, salinity, and density, and form on the ocean surface where water comes into contact with the atmosphere. Once formed, water masses sink and spread horizontally along constant-density planes, transporting their properties across the oceans. The main water masses of the world include Tropical Surface Water (TSW), which forms in the tropics with high temperature and salinity; Antarctic Intermediate Water (AAIW), which forms at the Antarctic Convergence with low salinity; and North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW), which forms in the polar seas of the North Atlantic with low temperature and high salinity. These water masses are distributed across the oceans following well-defined patterns determined by their relative densities.

The interaction between water masses is a key factor in ocean dynamics and the regulation of global climate. When two water masses with different properties come into contact, mixing and diffusion processes occur that transfer heat, salinity, and nutrients between them. These processes influence biological productivity, oxygen distribution, and the thermohaline circulation that regulates global climate. The formation and destruction of water masses is a continuous process influenced by climate, wind patterns, and freshwater input from rivers and ice melt. Changes in water mass formation, such as the weakening of the NADW due to global warming, can have significant consequences for ocean circulation and global climate, underscoring the importance of monitoring and understanding these processes.


## Chapter 15: Coastal Ecosystems

### 15.1 Mangroves and coastal wetlands

Mangroves are transitional coastal ecosystems between land and sea, dominated by halophytic trees and shrubs that grow on saline or brackish substrates on tropical and subtropical coasts. These coastal forests are distributed along the coasts of Africa, Asia, Central and South America, Australia, and the Pacific islands, covering approximately 150,000 square kilometers globally. Mangrove trees possess exceptional physiological adaptations to survive in saline environments, including the ability to excrete salt through their leaves, develop aerial roots (pneumatophores) that allow respiration in flooded substrates, and produce viable seeds while still attached to the tree (viviparity). These adaptations allow them to colonize and dominate coastal zones where other trees cannot survive, creating unique ecosystems of extraordinary productivity and biodiversity.

The ecosystem services provided by mangroves are of incalculable value to human communities and marine ecosystems. Coastal protection is perhaps the most important service, as the dense roots of mangrove trees absorb the energy of waves and currents, significantly reducing coastal erosion and protecting coastal communities from floods, storms, and tsunamis. Studies conducted after the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami demonstrated that areas with intact mangroves suffered significantly less damage than areas where mangroves had been destroyed. Mangroves also act as extremely efficient carbon sinks, storing between three and five times more carbon per unit area than tropical terrestrial forests, making them important allies in the fight against climate change.

Despite their ecological importance, mangroves are threatened globally by deforestation, intensive aquaculture (especially shrimp farming), coastal urbanization, pollution, and climate change. It is estimated that between 35 and 50 percent of global mangrove area has been lost in the last 50 years, with loss rates that have decreased in recent years thanks to conservation efforts but remain concerning. Mangrove restoration programs, which include planting seeds and seedlings, creating channels to improve drainage, and removing invasive species, are recovering degraded areas in countries such as India, the Philippines, Ecuador, and Madagascar. The protection and restoration of mangroves is a strategic investment that provides multiple benefits, including coastal protection, carbon capture, habitat provision for marine fauna, and income generation for local communities through sustainable fishing and ecotourism.

### 15.2 Seagrass meadows

Seagrass meadows are underwater ecosystems dominated by flowering plants (angiosperms) completely adapted to life in seawater, which form extensive green carpets on the sandy and muddy bottoms of shallow coasts worldwide. Unlike algae, seagrasses possess roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits, and perform photosynthesis to obtain their energy. There are approximately 72 species of seagrasses distributed across all temperate and tropical coasts of the world, with the best-known species including Posidonia oceanica in the Mediterranean, Zostera marina in the North Atlantic, Thalassia testudinum in the Caribbean, and Halodule uninervis in the Indo-Pacific. These meadows cover approximately 300,000 square kilometers of the ocean floor, an area comparable to that of temperate forests, but their ecological importance is disproportionately greater than their extent.

Seagrass meadows perform multiple ecological functions that make them high-priority ecosystems for marine conservation. Sediment stabilization is one of their most important functions, as the roots and rhizomes of the plants anchor the sediment and reduce coastal erosion, maintaining water clarity and preventing turbidity that can harm other organisms such as corals. Seagrass meadows also act as efficient carbon sinks, capturing atmospheric CO2 through photosynthesis and storing it in their tissues and the surrounding sediment for hundreds of years. This blue carbon is an increasingly recognized resource in the fight against climate change, and seagrass restoration projects are being promoted as nature-based solutions for climate change mitigation.

Seagrass meadows provide habitat, food, and breeding areas for a wide variety of marine organisms, from microscopic invertebrates to commercially important fish and marine mammals. The dugong (Dugong dugon) and the manatee (Trichechus manatus), two threatened sirenians, depend exclusively on seagrasses as a food source, and the loss of these meadows is one of the main threats to their survival. Juvenile fish of many commercial species, including shrimp, groupers, and salmon, use the meadows as refuge and feeding areas during the early stages of their life cycle, making them essential nurseries for sustainable fishing. Despite their importance, seagrass meadows are threatened by pollution, turbidity, coastal construction, boat anchoring, and climate change, and it is estimated that between 1.5 and 7 percent of their global area is lost annually.

### 15.3 Estuaries and intertidal zones

Estuaries are partially enclosed coastal bodies of water where freshwater from rivers mixes with saltwater from the sea, creating an environment of variable salinity that ranges from nearly fresh to nearly marine. These bodies of water include bays, rias, fjords, coastal lagoons, and deltas, and are characterized by their high biological productivity, their role as nurseries for many marine species, and their function as natural filters that retain sediments and nutrients before they reach the open sea. Estuaries form when a river valley is partially flooded by sea level rise or tectonic subsidence of the coast, creating a basin where freshwater and saltwater mixing occurs. The shape and dynamics of estuaries depend on factors such as coastal geology, tidal regimes, river discharge, and recent geological history.

Zonation within an estuary reflects the salinity gradient from the river mouth to the estuary mouth. In the fresh/oligohaline zone, near the river mouth, salinity is low (less than 5 parts per thousand) and the biological community is dominated by organisms tolerant of low salinity, such as freshwater fish, aquatic insects, and riparian plants. In the mesohaline zone, intermediate salinity (5-18 ppt) harbors a mixture of freshwater and marine species, creating a unique transitional community. In the polyhaline zone, near the estuary mouth, salinity is high (18-30 ppt) and the biological community approaches that of marine coasts, with marine fish, crustaceans, and mollusks. These variable salinity zones create a great diversity of habitats that support a disproportionately high biodiversity relative to their area.

Estuaries face multiple threats from human activity, including pollution from wastewater, increased sedimentation from deforestation and agriculture, destruction of habitats for coastal development, and the effects of climate change such as sea level rise and coastal flooding. Nutrient pollution from agriculture and wastewater is especially problematic in estuaries, as it triggers eutrophication, harmful algal blooms (red tide), dead zones, and water quality degradation. Restoration of degraded estuaries includes removing obsolete dams to restore river connectivity, restoring coastal wetlands to filter pollutants, and replanting riparian trees to stabilize banks and provide shade. Restored estuaries can rapidly recover their ecological functionality, providing valuable ecosystem services such as flood protection, water purification, and habitat for wildlife.


## Chapter 16: Marine Environment Protection

### 16.1 Plastic pollution

Plastic pollution in the oceans is one of the most urgent and visible environmental problems of our time, with devastating consequences for marine fauna, ecosystems, and potentially human health. It is estimated that between 8 and 12 million tons of plastic reach the oceans each year, coming from terrestrial sources such as urban runoff, rivers, illegal dumping, and inadequate waste management, as well as marine sources such as fishing and maritime transport. Non-biodegradable plastic gradually fragments into microplastics (particles smaller than 5 millimeters) that are distributed across all the oceans, from the surface to the abyssal trenches, and have been found in the blood, lungs, and stomach of humans, raising concerns about their potential health effects. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch, an accumulation of floating plastic of approximately 1.6 million square kilometers, is the most visible example of this problem, although plastic pollution is present in all the world's oceans and coasts.

The effects of plastic pollution on marine fauna include ingestion, entanglement, suffocation, and transfer of chemical contaminants. Hundreds of marine species, including sea turtles, seabirds, marine mammals, fish, and crustaceans, have been documented ingesting plastic or becoming entangled in nets, ropes, and other debris. Ingestion of plastic can cause obstruction of the digestive tract, false sensation of satiety, reduced food intake, malnutrition, and death. Microplastics, which are ingested by organisms of all sizes, from zooplankton to large predators, can transfer chemical contaminants to organism tissues, with potential toxicological effects. Additionally, microplastics can act as vectors for pathogenic organisms and exotic species, facilitating the spread of diseases and biological invasion.

Solutions to plastic pollution in the oceans require a comprehensive approach that addresses the production, use, and disposal of plastic throughout its entire lifecycle. Source reduction, including bans on single-use plastic products, promotion of biodegradable materials, and implementation of container deposit systems, is fundamental for reducing the amount of plastic reaching the oceans. Improved waste management, including selective collection, recycling, and proper treatment of waste, is essential for preventing plastic from reaching the environment. Cleanup initiatives, such as The Ocean Cleanup, are developing technologies to collect floating plastic in the oceans, but these efforts are insufficient without a significant reduction in the inflow of new plastic. Public awareness, environmental education, and legislation are key tools for changing the consumption and production patterns that generate plastic pollution.

### 16.2 Marine protected areas

Marine protected areas (MPAs) are ocean zones where human activities are regulated or restricted to protect marine ecosystems, maintain biodiversity, and ensure the sustainable use of marine resources. MPAs can vary in size, level of protection, and management objectives, from strict nature reserves where all extractive activities are prohibited to multiple-use areas where certain regulated fishing and recreational activities are permitted. Globally, there are more than 26,000 MPAs covering approximately 8 percent of the world's oceans, although the geographical distribution of MPAs is very uneven, with most protection concentrated in high seas waters and little protection on the coasts of developing countries where pressure on marine resources is greatest.

Scientific evidence demonstrates that well-designed and managed MPAs are effective for improving the health of marine ecosystems, increasing the abundance and diversity of species, improving the structure of biological communities, and providing economic benefits to coastal communities. The positive effects of MPAs include increased fish size and density within protected areas, the spread of larvae and juveniles to adjacent areas (spillover effect), and improved ecosystem resilience to disturbances such as coral bleaching and climate change. MPAs also provide economic benefits through sustainable fishing in buffer zones, ecotourism, and scientific research, generating income that exceeds management and protection costs.

The international goal of protecting 30 percent of the oceans by 2030, established in the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022), requires a significant expansion of the global MPA network, especially in high seas waters where currently only 1.2 percent is protected. The implementation of the UN High Seas Treaty (BBNJ), adopted in 2023, provides a legal framework for the creation of MPAs on the high seas, which until now lacked a legal mechanism for their establishment. Marine spatial planning, which coordinates the sustainable use of ocean space, is essential for ensuring that MPAs are located in the most ecologically important areas and integrated with other management measures such as fishing regulation, pollution management, and coastal planning. The success of MPAs depends on the participation of local communities, international cooperation, and the effective implementation of protection and management measures.

### 16.3 Integrated coastal zone management

Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) is a holistic approach to coastal planning and management that seeks to balance the objectives of economic development, environmental protection, and social well-being. ICZM recognizes that coastal zones are complex systems where multiple human activities (fishing, tourism, transport, urbanization, industry) interact with natural ecosystems (beaches, reefs, mangroves, estuaries), and that the management of these activities must be coordinated to avoid conflicts and maximize sustainable benefits. The principles of ICZM include the participation of all stakeholders, evidence-based planning, adaptive management, the precautionary principle, and intergenerational solidarity. This approach has been adopted by numerous countries and international organizations as a framework for sustainable coastal management.

Marine spatial planning (MSP) is a key tool of ICZM that allows the allocation of ocean space uses in a way that minimizes conflicts between activities and maximizes sustainable benefits. MSP involves identifying suitable areas for different uses (fishing, conservation, renewable energy, transport, tourism) based on ecological, economic, and social criteria. The implementation of MSP has been shown to be effective in reducing conflicts between fishing and conservation, facilitating the development of offshore renewable energy, and improving the protection of marine ecosystems. Successful MSP examples include those of Australia, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Norway, which have developed marine spatial plans that coordinate multiple activities in a sustainable manner.

Adaptation to climate change is an essential component of ICZM, as coasts are one of the zones most vulnerable to the effects of global warming, including sea level rise, coastal flooding, erosion, saltwater intrusion, and ecosystem loss. Coastal adaptation strategies include the protection of natural ecosystems (mangroves, seagrass meadows, salt marshes) that provide natural coastal protection, the construction of coastal defenses (dikes, breakwaters, seawalls) where natural ecosystems are insufficient, accommodation (modifying infrastructure and practices to reduce vulnerability), and, ultimately, the resettlement of communities in high-risk areas. Adaptive decision-making, which incorporates uncertainty about the future and allows strategies to be adjusted as new information becomes available, is fundamental for coastal management in the context of rapid and unpredictable climate change.


## Chapter 17: 21st Century Oceanographic Research

### 17.1 Observational oceanography

Observational oceanography is the set of activities and technologies dedicated to measuring, monitoring, and recording the physical, chemical, and biological properties of the oceans at local, regional, and global scales. Ocean observation has experienced an unprecedented revolution in recent decades thanks to the development of satellite technologies, autonomous buoys, underwater vehicles, and sensor networks that allow real-time and continuous data collection. The Argo network, consisting of more than 4,000 robot buoys distributed across all the oceans, provides vertical profiles of temperature and salinity down to 2,000 meters depth every 10 days, creating a three-dimensional map of the thermohaline structure of the oceans. Oceanographic satellites, including altimeters, radiometers, and scatterometers, provide high-resolution data on the ocean surface, including sea level height, surface temperature, wind speed, and chlorophyll concentration.

Drift buoys and moored buoys are essential components of the ocean observation network, providing in situ data that complement and validate satellite observations. Argo buoys, which are automatically deployed and recovered every 10 days, have transformed our understanding of ocean circulation, ocean warming, and climatic phenomena such as El Niño and La Niña. Drift buoys, which move with currents and transmit data by satellite, provide information about surface circulation and water properties. Moored buoys, which measure variables at a fixed point over time, are essential for detecting long-term variations and monitoring phenomena such as tides, tidal currents, and waves. The integration of data from multiple sources, including satellites, buoys, ships, and numerical models, is fundamental for creating high-quality oceanographic products that are used by weather agencies, research centers, and maritime industries.

Autonomous oceanography, which uses autonomous underwater vehicles (AUV) and autonomous surface vehicles (ASV), is emerging as a revolutionary tool for ocean exploration and monitoring. AUVs can be programmed to conduct survey, data collection, and biological sampling missions autonomously, covering extensive areas of the ocean floor with a resolution and efficiency that surpass those of crewed ships. ASVs, such as the robotic ships Saildrone and Liquid Robotics Wave Glider, can navigate for months without human intervention, collecting meteorological and oceanographic data in remote and dangerous areas. The combination of AUVs, ASVs, and fixed sensor networks is creating an increasingly dense and complete ocean observation infrastructure, enabling unprecedented understanding of oceanic processes across multiple temporal and spatial scales.

### 17.2 Ocean modeling

Numerical ocean modeling is a fundamental tool for understanding, predicting, and managing oceanic processes, allowing the simulation of circulation, temperature, salinity, nutrients, biological productivity, and other aspects of the oceans at global, regional, and local scales. Ocean models are based on the equations of fluid physics, thermodynamics, and biogeochemistry, and are solved numerically on supercomputers to simulate the evolution of the ocean over time. Global models, such as the Nucleus for European Modelling of the Ocean (NEMO) and the Modular Ocean Model (MOM), simulate global circulation and heat and mass exchanges between the ocean and the atmosphere, providing essential data for climate prediction. Regional models, such as the Regional Ocean Modeling System (ROMS), simulate processes at coastal and continental shelf scales with higher resolution, including tidal currents, upwelling, stratification, and vertical mixing.

Ocean models coupled with climate models are essential for predicting the effects of climate change on the oceans and vice versa. Ocean-atmosphere coupling allows the simulation of heat, moisture, and gas exchange between the ocean surface and the atmosphere, including CO2 absorption and heat release. Global climate models, such as those of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), use ocean components to simulate thermohaline circulation, sea level rise, ocean acidification, and changes in marine productivity under different greenhouse gas emission scenarios. The validation of ocean models with observational data is fundamental for ensuring their reliability and improving their predictive capacity.

Operational ocean models, which provide real-time predictions of oceanic conditions, are used by weather agencies and maritime industries for safe navigation, fisheries management, and environmental emergency response. Wave prediction systems, such as WAVEWATCH III and EGRR, provide wave forecasts up to 10 days, allowing mariners to plan safe routes and avoid dangerous storms. Tidal current models, such as OTIS (Oregon State University Tidal Inverse Solution), provide real-time predictions of current and tidal level, essential for navigation in estuaries and coastal zones. Pollutant dispersion models, which simulate the transport and transformation of pollutants in water, are used to assess the impact of oil spills, industrial discharges, and wastewater pollution. The integration of multiple models and data sources into oceanographic information systems, such as the NOAA Oceanographic Information System, is improving the capacity to understand, predict, and manage the oceans.

### 17.3 Molecular marine biology

Molecular marine biology has revolutionized our understanding of the biodiversity, ecology, and evolution of marine organisms, providing tools to identify species, study their genetic relationships, understand their adaptations, and assess the impact of human activities on marine populations. DNA and RNA sequencing techniques, including next-generation sequencing (NGS) and environmental DNA (eDNA) sequencing, allow the identification of species present in a water or sediment sample without the need to observe or capture organisms, a revolutionary tool for monitoring marine biodiversity. The use of eDNA to detect the presence of rare, threatened, or invasive species is transforming the way we monitor and manage marine ecosystems, enabling earlier and more comprehensive detections than traditional sampling methods.

Marine population genetics studies use molecular markers to understand the genetic structure, gene flow patterns, and connectivity between populations of marine organisms. These studies are fundamental for designing networks of marine protected areas that maintain genetic connectivity between populations, assessing the impact of habitat fragmentation, and guiding the management of transboundary fisheries. Population genetics studies have revealed that many marine species, apparently uniform on a global scale, actually comprise multiple cryptic species or genetically differentiated subpopulations, with important implications for conservation and management. The discovery of hidden biodiversity through molecular techniques is significantly expanding our knowledge of life's diversity in the oceans.

Molecular marine biology is also providing insights into the adaptations of marine organisms to extreme conditions, including life in the deep sea, tolerance to variable salinity, and resistance to ocean warming. Transcriptomic and proteomic studies, which analyze gene and protein expression in response to environmental changes, are revealing the molecular mechanisms that allow organisms to adapt to conditions of thermal stress, hypoxia, and acidification. This knowledge is essential for predicting the capacity of marine species to adapt to climate change and for developing conservation strategies that maintain ecosystem resilience. Marine synthetic biology, which applies genetic engineering principles to create organisms with new functions, is emerging as a promising field for marine biotechnology, including the production of biofuels, pharmaceuticals, and materials from marine organisms.


## Chapter 18: Oceanography and Sustainable Development

### 18.1 Sustainable Development Goals and the oceans

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), adopted in 2015 as part of the 2030 Agenda, include a specific goal dedicated to the conservation and sustainable use of the oceans, seas, and marine resources for sustainable development (SDG 14). This goal encompasses a wide range of targets, including the prevention and reduction of marine pollution, the protection of marine and coastal ecosystems, the regulation of fishing and aquaculture, the conservation of marine biodiversity, the reduction of ocean acidification, the elimination of fishing subsidies that contribute to overfishing, investment in the blue economy of developing countries, and the strengthening of international cooperation for ocean governance. Progress toward SDG 14 has been slow on many fronts, with plastic pollution, overfishing, and degradation of marine ecosystems continuing to increase in many regions.

The blue economy is a concept that promotes the sustainable use of ocean resources for economic development, job creation, and poverty reduction. The blue economy encompasses sectors such as sustainable fishing, responsible aquaculture, marine tourism, ocean energy, maritime transport, and marine biotechnology, with a focus on green job creation and reducing inequality. Developing coastal countries, which heavily depend on marine resources for food security and their economy, are the primary beneficiaries of a sustainable blue economy approach. Investment in the blue economy requires a balance between economic development and environmental protection, incorporating principles of sustainability, circularity, and social equity.

International cooperation is fundamental for the sustainable management of the oceans, as marine problems such as pollution, overfishing, climate change, and biodiversity protection transcend national borders and require global solutions. International legal frameworks, such as the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), and the Paris Agreement on climate change, provide the foundations for international cooperation in ocean management. The implementation of these agreements requires political will, financial resources, technology transfer, and capacity building, especially in developing countries. Citizen science, which involves citizens in the collection of oceanographic data, is emerging as a valuable tool to complement research and monitoring efforts, expanding the knowledge base available for decision-making on ocean management.

### 18.2 Citizen science and public participation

Oceanographic citizen science is a growing movement that involves non-specialized citizens in the collection of oceanographic data, environmental monitoring, and assessment of marine ecosystem health. Citizen science programs include the collection of data on water quality at beaches, monitoring of seabirds, identification of invasive species, documentation of coral bleaching, and collection of water samples for microplastic analysis. These programs provide valuable data at temporal and spatial scales that would be impossible to collect through traditional research methods, as they use the geographical distribution and temporal availability of thousands of volunteers to cover extensive areas of the coast and ocean. Platforms such as iNaturalist, eBird, and Reef Check are coordinating citizen science projects on a global scale, providing data that are used by researchers, environmental managers, and policymakers.

Public participation in oceanographic science not only provides valuable data but also raises awareness about the importance of the oceans and the challenges they face. Citizen science programs involve coastal communities in the protection of their marine environment, creating informed and engaged citizens who can advocate for more effective environmental protection policies. Citizen science projects in schools and universities introduce young people to oceanography and marine biology, inspiring the next generation of scientists and environmental managers. Citizen science can also be a powerful tool for monitoring the implementation of international agreements such as the Paris Agreement and the SDGs, providing independent data on the state of marine ecosystems and the effectiveness of protection policies.

Technology is facilitating the expansion of oceanographic citizen science, with the development of low-cost sensors, mobile applications for data collection, and digital platforms for image and data analysis. Low-cost water quality sensors, such as Arduino and Raspberry Pi devices, allow citizens to measure temperature, salinity, pH, and dissolved oxygen on their local coasts, providing real-time data on water quality. Mobile applications such as iNaturalist and Litterati allow citizens to document marine biodiversity and plastic pollution with smartphone cameras, creating global databases that are used by researchers and environmental managers. The integration of citizen science with professional science, including data validation, method standardization, and collaboration in interpreting results, is essential for maximizing the impact of citizen science in the understanding and protection of the oceans.

### 18.3 The future of oceanography

The future of oceanography is marked by technological advances, greater interdisciplinarity, and growing awareness of the importance of the oceans for humanity. Exploration of the ocean depths will continue to expand our understanding of life in extreme environments and the planet's geological processes, with new underwater vehicles and technologies that will allow access to zones still unexplored. Ocean monitoring will become increasingly complete and continuous, with sensor networks, next-generation satellites, and autonomous vehicles that will create a global real-time observation system. Ocean modeling will continue to improve, with more accurate models, higher spatial and temporal resolution, and better coupling with terrestrial, atmospheric, and biological systems. The integration of oceanography with other disciplines, such as biology, chemistry, geology, meteorology, and social science, will provide a more complete understanding of the oceans and their role in the Earth system.

The environmental challenges we face, including climate change, pollution, overfishing, and biodiversity loss, will require an increasingly applied oceanography oriented toward problem-solving. Oceanographic research will need to provide scientific evidence for decision-making on marine resource management, ecosystem protection, and adaptation to climate change. The communication of oceanographic science to the general public, politicians, and decision-makers will be increasingly important for raising awareness about the importance of the oceans and mobilizing public support for protection policies. Oceanographic education, from basic teaching to postgraduate training, will be fundamental for preparing the next generation of scientists, managers, and citizens who will protect and manage the oceans.

In short, oceanography is a science in full expansion that plays a fundamental role in the understanding and protection of the oceans, which cover 71 percent of the Earth's surface and are essential for life on Earth. Oceanographic knowledge is crucial for understanding the functioning of global climate, managing marine resources sustainably, protecting the biodiversity of oceanic ecosystems, and adapting to the effects of climate change. Investment in research, technology, education, and international cooperation will be decisive for ensuring that the oceans continue to provide the essential ecosystem services for humanity in the coming decades and centuries. The future of the oceans depends on our decisions today, and oceanography provides the tools and knowledge necessary to make those decisions in an informed and responsible manner.


## Chapter 19: Glossary of Oceanographic Terms

### 19.1 Fundamental terminology

Oceanography uses a wide range of technical terms that reflect the complexity and multidisciplinarity of the science. Some of the fundamental terms include: thermocline, which refers to the transitional layer where temperature drops rapidly with depth; halocline, the layer where salinity varies rapidly; and pycnocline, the layer where density increases rapidly. Upwelling is the process by which deep, nutrient-rich water rises toward the surface, usually driven by winds that push surface water away from the coast. Downwelling is the opposite process, where surface water sinks toward the depths, usually in convergence zones. The mesopelagic refers to the ocean zone between 200 and 1,000 meters depth, while the bathypelagic comprises depths between 1,000 and 4,000 meters.

Other important terms include: thermohaline circulation, the global system of deep currents driven by differences in temperature and salinity; ocean gyre, a circular system of surface currents that rotates clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere; El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a climatic pattern that alters water temperature and precipitation patterns in the tropical Pacific; ocean acidification, the decrease in ocean pH caused by the absorption of atmospheric CO2; and the biological pump, the process by which carbon is transported from the surface to the depths of the ocean. Plate tectonics is the theory that describes the movement of lithospheric plates over the asthenosphere, explaining the distribution of volcanoes, earthquakes, and abyssal trenches.

Oceanographic terminology continues to evolve as the science advances and new phenomena and processes are discovered. Recent terms include blue carbon, which refers to carbon captured and stored in marine ecosystems such as mangroves, seagrass meadows, and kelp forests; the blue economy, a concept that promotes the sustainable use of ocean resources for economic development; and marine spatial planning, the tool that coordinates the sustainable use of ocean space. Understanding oceanographic terminology is fundamental for researchers, environmental managers, and citizens who wish to communicate effectively about the oceans and contribute to their protection.
