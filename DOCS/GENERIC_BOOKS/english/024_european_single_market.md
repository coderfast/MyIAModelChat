# The European Single Market

## Chapter 1: Fundamentals of the Single Market

### The European Economic Area as the Foundation of the Single Market

The European single market, also known as the internal market, represents one of the most ambitious and successful achievements of economic integration in contemporary history. It is an economic space that guarantees the free movement of persons, goods, services, and capital between the Member States of the European Union, eliminating trade barriers and creating a common market of more than 450 million consumers with a combined Gross Domestic Product exceeding 15 trillion euros. The single market was formally established on 1 January 1993 with the entry into force of the Single European Act, although its construction dates back to the Treaties of Rome of 1957 which created the European Economic Community with the objective of establishing a common market between its Member States. The European Economic Area, which includes the EU Member States plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway, extends the scope of the single market to countries not belonging to the European Union that participate in the internal market through specific agreements.

The single market is based on four fundamental freedoms that constitute the pillars of economic integration: the free movement of persons, the free movement of goods, the free movement of services, and the free movement of capital. The free movement of persons allows citizens of the European Union to move, reside, and work in any Member State without the need for special authorization, and to exercise their right of establishment on equal terms with nationals of the host State. The free movement of goods prohibits customs duties and quantitative restrictions on imports and exports between the Member States, as well as all measures having equivalent effect that may hinder intra-community trade. The free movement of services allows service providers established in one Member State to provide services temporarily in another Member State without the need to establish themselves permanently there. The free movement of capital guarantees the absence of restrictions on capital movements between the Member States and between them and third countries.

### The Institutional Pillars of the Single Market

The institutional architecture of the single market rests on a comprehensive regulatory framework that includes directives, regulations, and the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union. Directives establish the objectives that must be achieved by the Member States, but leave them the freedom to choose the form and means to achieve them, which allows European standards to be adapted to the peculiarities of each national system. Regulations have direct application in all the Member States from the moment of their entry into force, without the need for transposition into national law, which guarantees uniform application throughout the internal market. The case law of the Court of Justice of the EU interprets the rules of the internal market and guarantees their effective application in all the Member States, establishing fundamental principles such as direct effect and the supremacy of European law.

The Internal Market Committee and the European Commission are primarily responsible for the development and implementation of single market legislation. The Internal Market Committee, composed of representatives of the Member States, advises the Commission in the preparation of legislative and implementing proposals related to the internal market. The European Commission has the legislative initiative to propose new rules or modify existing ones, and monitors the application of European law in the Member States, being able to initiate infringement proceedings against States that do not correctly apply the rules of the internal market. The European Parliament and the Council of the EU participate in the legislative process through the co-decision procedure, which has become the ordinary procedure for the adoption of internal market legislation.

### The Benefits of the Single Market for Citizens and Businesses

The single market has generated significant benefits for both citizens and European businesses. For citizens, the single market has meant greater freedom of choice, both with regard to the goods and services available and with regard to the possibilities of residence and work in other Member States. European consumers can access a wider range of products and services at competitive prices, as competition among suppliers from different countries has contributed to reducing prices and improving quality. Citizens can move freely throughout the European Union without internal border controls, reside and work in any Member State, and access public services such as education and healthcare on equal terms with nationals of the host State.

For businesses, the single market has meant an expansion of their markets and greater competitiveness. Companies can sell their products and services to more than 450 million consumers without customs or restrictive barriers, which allows them to achieve economies of scale and reduce costs. The single market has facilitated the creation of pan-European supply chains, in which companies can specialize in the production of specific components and sell their products to manufacturers of other Member States. Competition among companies from different countries has driven innovation and productivity, contributing to economic growth and job creation. In addition, the single market has facilitated the mobility of workers, who can seek employment in other Member States, and of capital, which flows freely toward the most profitable investment opportunities. The single market has generated a multiplier effect on the European economy, creating synergies between the different national markets and expanding business opportunities for companies of all Member States. SMEs, which represent the vast majority of European companies, have particularly benefited from the single market, as they can access wider markets without the need to establish subsidiaries in other countries, reducing entry costs and increasing their growth opportunities.

The benefits of the single market extend beyond the economic dimensions, also contributing to social cohesion and European integration. The mobility of workers has promoted cultural exchange and mutual understanding among citizens of different European countries, strengthening European identity and the awareness of belonging to a shared political community. The single market has facilitated access to products and services from across the continent, enriching the daily lives of European citizens and expanding their consumption options. The free movement of persons has allowed millions of Europeans to study, work, and live in other countries, acquiring linguistic and cultural skills that make them more competitive in the international labor market. The single market has demonstrated that economic integration can generate tangible benefits for citizens, reinforcing public support for European integration and creating a sense of belonging to a community of shared destiny.

## Chapter 2: Free Movement of Goods

### The Elimination of Tariff and Non-Tariff Barriers

The free movement of goods is one of the fundamental freedoms of the single market and one of the main sources of economic benefits of European integration. The elimination of customs duties and quantitative restrictions on imports and exports between the Member States has meant a significant reduction in trade costs and an expansion of the markets available to European companies. Customs duties, which represented a significant barrier to intra-community trade before the creation of the common market, were completely eliminated for trade between the Member States of the European Economic Community in 1968, more than twenty years before the formal creation of the single market. The elimination of quantitative restrictions, including import and export licenses, quotas, and prohibitions, completed the process of liberalizing intra-community trade in goods.

In addition to tariff barriers, the single market has meant the elimination of non-tariff barriers, which are those measures that, without taking the form of customs duties, may hinder intra-community trade. These barriers include differences in product technical and safety standards, provisions relating to sales and distribution conditions, public procurement rules, and health and environmental protection measures that may be discriminatory or disproportionate. The harmonization of product technical and safety standards has been a fundamental instrument for eliminating non-tariff barriers to intra-community trade, ensuring that products that comply with European standards can circulate freely throughout the internal market without the need for additional controls at borders.

### The Role of Technical Standards in the Free Movement of Goods

Technical standards play a fundamental role in the free movement of goods, as they ensure that products sold in the internal market meet minimum requirements for safety, health, and environmental protection. The General Product Safety Directive establishes the essential requirements that all products marketed in the internal market must meet, and manufacturers are responsible for ensuring that their products comply with these requirements before marketing them. The CE marking is the mark that certifies that a product complies with all applicable European directives, and its presence on the product allows it to circulate freely throughout the internal market without the need for additional controls at borders.

The European standardization system, coordinated by the European Committee for Standardization (CEN), the European Committee for Electrotechnical Standardization (CENELEC), and the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI), develops the technical standards applied in the internal market. These technical standards detail the technical specifications that products must meet to ensure their conformity with the essential requirements of European directives. European technical standards are harmonized, which means that compliance with them gives rise to a presumption of conformity with European directives, facilitating the free movement of products in the internal market. The European standardization system has contributed significantly to eliminating technical barriers to intra-community trade, allowing products manufactured in one Member State to be freely sold in the other Member States without the need for significant adaptations. European technical standards cover a wide range of products and sectors, including electronics, machinery, chemicals, food, toys, textile products, and construction materials. The European standardization system is based on the principle of mutual recognition, which establishes that a product that has been lawfully marketed in one Member State must be able to be marketed freely in the other Member States, unless there are overriding reasons of general interest that justify a restriction. The principle of mutual recognition has been a fundamental instrument for eliminating non-tariff barriers to intra-community trade, as it allows products that comply with the standards of one Member State to be sold freely in the other Member States without having to comply with additional standards.

The European standardization system has also facilitated innovation, as technical standards provide a reference framework for the development of new products and technologies, and facilitate the marketing of innovative products throughout the internal market. European technical standards are developed by European standardization organizations such as the European Committee for Standardization (CEN), the European Committee for Electrotechnical Standardization (CENELEC), and the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI), which bring together experts from the Member States to develop technical consensus that reflects best practices and the latest technological innovations. The European standardization process is open, transparent, and based on consensus, which ensures that technical standards reflect the needs of all stakeholders, including manufacturers, users, consumers, and public authorities.

### Public Procurement and the Free Movement of Goods

Public procurement represents a significant part of the economic activity of the Member States, as public administrations are major purchasers of goods and services. The Public Procurement Directive establishes rules of transparency, equal treatment, and non-discrimination for public procurement, ensuring that companies from all Member States have equal opportunities to access public contracts from other Member States. The liberalization of public procurement has meant an expansion of the markets available to European companies, as they can compete on equal terms for public contracts of any Member State, regardless of their nationality.

The Public Procurement Directive establishes value thresholds that determine the obligation to publish public contracts in the Official Journal of the European Union, ensuring transparency and effective competition in the awarding of contracts. The procedures for awarding public contracts must be transparent, non-discriminatory, and based on objective criteria, ensuring equal opportunities for all companies wishing to participate in the tender. The Directive also establishes rules for the publication of public contracts, including tender notices, contract documents, and award results, which must be published in the Official Journal of the European Union to ensure transparency and supervision by the European Commission and economic operators.

## Chapter 3: Free Movement of Services

### The Services Directive and Its Impact on the Single Market

The free movement of services is one of the fundamental freedoms of the single market, although it has historically been more difficult to achieve than the free movement of goods due to the diversity of national regulatory systems for services. The Services Directive, adopted in 2006 after a lengthy legislative process, establishes a general framework for the liberalization of services in the internal market, removing barriers to the free provision of services and facilitating the establishment of service providers in other Member States. The Services Directive applies to any activity consisting of providing services for remuneration, excluding services provided by the State or by public bodies in the exercise of their sovereign functions, financial services, transport services, audiovisual services, and gambling services.

The Services Directive establishes the principle of free provision of services, which allows service providers established in one Member State to provide services temporarily in another Member State without the need to establish themselves permanently there. This principle facilitates the cross-border mobility of service providers, who can provide services in other Member States without the need to set up a subsidiary or branch in the host country. The Directive also establishes the single contact point procedure, which simplifies administrative procedures for service providers by allowing them to comply with the administrative formalities required by the host State through a single contact point, avoiding the need to deal with multiple administrative authorities.

### Financial Services in the Single Market

Financial services are one of the most regulated sectors of the single market, as their liberalization has a significant impact on financial stability and consumer protection. The European Union has developed a comprehensive regulatory framework for the regulation of financial services, including banking supervision, securities market regulation, investor protection, and the prevention of money laundering. The Single Market for Financial Services, also known as the roadmap for creating a single market for financial services, aims to complete the integration of European financial markets, eliminating the remaining barriers to the free movement of financial services and ensuring the stability of the European financial system.

The Banking Union, established in 2012 in response to the financial crisis, represents a significant advance in the integration of the European financial market. The Banking Union includes a Single Supervisory Mechanism, which gives the European Central Bank the responsibility of directly supervising the most significant banks in the euro area, and a Single Resolution Mechanism, which establishes a common framework for the orderly resolution of failing banks. The Banking Union aims to break the vicious circle between banks and national governments, ensuring that the difficulties of a bank do not become a problem for the fiscal stability of a Member State. The Capital Markets Union, which complements the Banking Union, aims to eliminate the remaining barriers to the free movement of capital and create a truly integrated single market for financial services.

### Digital Services in the Single Market

Digital services represent a rapidly growing sector that presents specific challenges for free movement in the single market. The European Union has developed a regulatory framework to regulate digital services, including the Digital Markets Regulation, which establishes competition rules for digital platforms acting as intermediaries between users; the Digital Services Regulation, which establishes transparency obligations and content moderation for digital service providers; and the Directive on Copyright in the Single Digital Market, which modernizes intellectual property rules for the digital age. These legal instruments aim to create a safe, transparent, and competitive digital environment in the internal market, ensuring that digital services circulate freely throughout the internal market while protecting the fundamental rights of consumers and content creators.

The Digital Markets Regulation, also known as the Digital Markets Act, identifies large digital platforms as "gatekeepers" and imposes specific obligations on them to ensure fair and open practices in the digital market. Gatekeepers, which include platforms such as search engines, social networks, messaging services, and e-commerce platforms, are required to allow users to install third-party applications, facilitate access to data generated by their activity on the platform, and refrain from favoring their own products or services to the detriment of those of competitors. The Digital Services Regulation, for its part, establishes transparency obligations for digital service providers, including the obligation to inform users about the criteria used for content moderation, the publication of transparency reports, and cooperation with national and European authorities.

## Chapter 4: Free Movement of Persons and Capital

### Free Movement of Workers

The free movement of workers is one of the fundamental freedoms of the single market and one of the most valued rights by European citizens. It allows citizens of the European Union to seek employment, work, and reside in any Member State without the need for special authorization, and guarantees equal treatment with nationals of the host State with regard to employment conditions, remuneration, social advantages, and vocational training. The free movement of workers has meant greater geographical mobility of the labor force in Europe, allowing workers to seek better employment opportunities in other Member States and companies to find the qualified workers they need regardless of their nationality.

The free movement of workers applies to citizens of the European Union, citizens of the European Economic Area States (Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway), and Swiss citizens under bilateral agreements. Citizens of third countries who are nationals of a Member State may also benefit from the free movement of workers if they hold a right of permanent residence in a Member State. The free movement of workers includes the right to seek employment in another Member State, the right to work as an employee or self-employed person, the right to reside in the host State, and the right to equal treatment with nationals of the host State. Workers exercising their right to free movement may benefit from the social advantages of the host State on the same conditions as nationals, including access to healthcare, unemployment benefits, and pension benefits.

### Free Movement of Capital

The free movement of capital is one of the fundamental freedoms of the single market and an essential requirement for the efficient functioning of European financial markets. The free movement of capital guarantees the absence of restrictions on capital movements between the Member States and between them and third countries, allowing free cross-border investment and the mobilization of financial resources toward the most profitable investment opportunities. The liberalization of capital movements has meant greater integration of European financial markets, facilitating the financing of companies and investment projects through capital markets, and allowing investors to diversify their investment portfolios across national borders.

The free movement of capital includes long-term capital movements, such as investments in fixed-income and equity securities, direct investments in companies, and long-term loans, as well as short-term capital movements, such as bank deposits, short-term loans, and foreign exchange operations. The European Union has adopted a regulatory framework for the regulation of capital movements, including anti-money laundering rules, payment services regulation, and securities market supervision. The free movement of capital is an essential requirement for the functioning of the Economic and Monetary Union, as it allows the transmission of monetary policy through financial markets and facilitates the financial stability of the euro area. Cross-border capital movements have increased significantly since the creation of the single market, reflecting the greater integration of European financial markets and the greater freedom of investors to diversify their portfolios across national borders. The liberalization of capital movements has facilitated the financing of investments in the less developed regions, contributing to economic development and regional convergence.

The free movement of capital has also facilitated the creation of a single market for financial services, in which companies and investors can access a wider range of financial services at more competitive costs. Banks, insurance companies, and investment firms can provide cross-border services without the need to establish subsidiaries in each Member State, reducing operating costs and expanding the available markets. The integration of European financial markets has facilitated the diversification of financial risks, allowing investors to spread their investments across different countries and sectors, reducing market volatility and improving financial stability. However, the free movement of capital also presents risks, including the spread of financial crises across borders and the possibility of speculative bubbles, which requires effective financial supervision and close cooperation between national and European supervisors. The integration of European financial markets has created channels of transmission of financial crises that can spread financial shocks from one country to another, as demonstrated during the global financial crisis of 2008 and the euro area sovereign debt crisis. To address these risks, the European Union has strengthened its financial supervision framework, creating the European System of Financial Supervision, which comprises three European Supervisory Authorities (the European Banking Authority, the European Securities and Markets Authority, and the European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority) and the European Systemic Risk Board. These bodies have the responsibility of coordinating financial supervision at the European level, identifying systemic risks, and proposing measures to mitigate them, contributing to the stability of the European financial system and the protection of financial consumers...

### The Right of Establishment

The right of establishment allows citizens of the European Union to engage in self-employed activities or to set up and manage companies in any Member State on the same conditions as nationals of that State. The right of establishment is one of the fundamental freedoms of the single market and has meant greater mobility of entrepreneurs and independent professionals in Europe, allowing them to establish themselves in other Member States to carry out their economic activities. The right of establishment includes access to economic activities, the conditions for exercising them, and access to public services on equal terms with nationals of the host State.

The right of establishment applies to citizens of the European Union and of the European Economic Area States, as well as to companies formed in accordance with the legislation of a Member State and having their registered office, central administration, or principal establishment within the European Union. European companies may establish themselves in any Member State by setting up a subsidiary, opening a branch, or appointing a permanent representative. The right of establishment is subject to limitations justified by reasons of public policy, public security, or public health, but these limitations must be proportionate and non-discriminatory. The Court of Justice of the European Union has interpreted the right of establishment broadly, ensuring that European citizens and companies can carry out their economic activities on equal terms throughout the internal market. The right of establishment has facilitated the creation of pan-European business networks, in which companies establish subsidiaries, branches, and strategic alliances in different countries to expand their markets, access new technologies, and take advantage of the comparative advantages of each region.

The freedom of establishment has meant greater competition in national markets, as companies from other Member States can compete on equal terms with national companies, promoting efficiency, innovation, and price reduction. The right of establishment has facilitated regional specialization, allowing regions to specialize in economic activities in which they have comparative advantages, and to trade the resulting products and services with the rest of the internal market. The mobility of entrepreneurs and independent professionals has enriched the business fabric of receiving regions, introducing new ideas, new technologies, and new management practices that have contributed to the modernization and competitiveness of regional economies. The right of establishment has proven to be an effective instrument for promoting economic convergence between European regions, as it facilitates the transfer of knowledge, technology, and human capital between the different countries of the internal market.

## Chapter 5: Regulatory Harmonization of the Single Market

### Harmonization Directives and Their Transposition

Regulatory harmonization is one of the fundamental instruments for ensuring the efficient functioning of the single market, as it allows the elimination of regulatory differences between the Member States that may hinder the free movement of goods, services, and persons. Harmonization directives establish the objectives that must be achieved by the Member States, but leave them the freedom to choose the form and means to achieve them, which allows European standards to be adapted to the peculiarities of each national system. The transposition of directives into national law is a complex process that requires the preparation of national legislation implementing the requirements of the directive, the adaptation of administrative and procedural provisions, and the training of officials responsible for applying the new rules.

The process of transposing directives into national law is subject to a set time limit, generally of two years from the adoption of the directive, which may be extended in exceptional circumstances. The Member States are obliged to communicate to the European Commission the transposition measures they adopt, together with a correlation table indicating the provisions of the directive and the equivalent provisions of national legislation. The European Commission verifies the correct transposition of directives into national law and may initiate infringement proceedings against States that do not transpose them correctly or that do not transpose them within the established time limit. Infringement proceedings are an important instrument for ensuring that directives are transposed in a complete and uniform manner in all the Member States.

### European Regulations and Their Direct Application

European regulations are another fundamental regulatory instrument of the single market, as they have direct application in all the Member States from the moment of their entry into force, without the need for transposition into national law. Regulations are mandatory in all their elements and are directly applicable in all the Member States, which guarantees uniform application throughout the internal market. Regulations are used for matters that require uniform application in all the Member States, such as competition rules, consumer protection rules, intellectual property rules, and personal data protection rules.

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is an example of a European regulation that is directly applicable in all the Member States, guaranteeing a uniform level of personal data protection throughout the internal market. The GDPR establishes the principles for the processing of personal data, the rights of data subjects, the obligations of data controllers and processors, and the sanctions for non-compliance. The GDPR has meant the harmonization of personal data protection rules throughout the European Union, eliminating differences between national legislations and ensuring that the personal data of European citizens are protected uniformly throughout the internal market. The direct application of the GDPR has facilitated the free movement of personal data in the internal market, as companies can transfer personal data between the Member States without needing to adapt to different national legislations.

### The Case Law of the Court of Justice and Its Role in the Interpretation of the Single Market

The case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union plays a fundamental role in the interpretation and application of single market law, as the Court's decisions interpret European rules and guarantee their uniform application in all the Member States. The Court of Justice has developed a rich and complex case law on the fundamental freedoms of the single market, establishing fundamental principles such as direct effect, the supremacy of European law, and the proportionality of restrictions on fundamental freedoms. The case law of the Court of Justice has contributed significantly to the construction of the single market, interpreting European rules in a progressive manner and ensuring that fundamental freedoms are effectively applied in all the Member States.

Direct effect is a fundamental principle of the case law of the Court of Justice that allows citizens and companies to directly invoke the provisions of European directives and regulations before national courts, even if the Member States have not correctly transposed the directives into national law. Direct effect ensures that citizens and companies can assert their European rights before national courts, regardless of the attitude of the Member States toward the transposition of directives. The supremacy of European law is another fundamental principle that establishes that European law prevails over national law in case of conflict, which guarantees the uniform application of European law in all the Member States. The principle of proportionality ensures that restrictions on the fundamental freedoms of the single market are appropriate, necessary, and proportionate to the objective pursued, which protects citizens and companies against unjustified restrictions on their economic freedoms.

## Chapter 6: The Digital Single Market

### The Digital Services Regulation

The digital single market is an emerging dimension of the internal market that aims to ensure that digital services circulate freely throughout the internal market while protecting the fundamental rights of users and content creators. The European Union has adopted an ambitious regulatory framework to regulate digital services, including the Digital Markets Regulation and the Digital Services Regulation, which together constitute the digital services package. The Digital Services Regulation establishes transparency obligations, content moderation, and user protection obligations for digital service providers, including hosting service providers, search engines, and online platforms.

The Digital Services Regulation classifies digital service providers into different categories according to their size and function, imposing proportionate obligations based on their impact on the internal market. Very large online platform providers, which include online platforms with more than 45 million active monthly users in the European Union, are subject to the strictest obligations, including the obligation to carry out systematic risk assessments, publish transparency reports, and cooperate with national and European authorities. The Regulation also establishes content moderation rules that ensure transparency and impartiality in the removal of content, and protect the fundamental rights of users, including freedom of expression and the right to information.

### The Digital Markets Regulation

The Digital Markets Regulation, also known as the Digital Markets Act, aims to ensure fair and open practices in the digital market, preventing large digital platforms from abusing their dominant position to the detriment of competitors and users. The Regulation identifies digital platforms that act as intermediaries between users and that have a significant impact on the internal market, and imposes specific obligations on them to ensure fair competition and innovation in the digital market. Gatekeepers, which are the platforms designated as such, are required to allow users to install third-party applications, facilitate access to data generated by their activity on the platform, and refrain from favoring their own products or services to the detriment of those of competitors.

The Digital Markets Regulation establishes a designation procedure for gatekeepers, which is based on quantitative criteria related to the size of the platform, the number of active users, and the volume of business. Gatekeepers are subject to specific obligations in relation to the services they provide, including core platform services, search engines, operating systems, web browsers, virtual assistants, and online advertising services. The Regulation also establishes an enforcement and sanctions procedure that allows the European Commission to investigate the practices of gatekeepers and impose sanctions in case of non-compliance, including fines of up to 10 percent of the worldwide turnover of the infringer.

## Chapter 7: Free Movement of Workers in the Single Market

### The Rights of Intra-Community Workers

The free movement of workers is one of the most important rights of the single market and has meant greater geographical mobility of the labor force in Europe. Workers exercising their right to free movement have the right to reside in the host State, to work as an employee or self-employed person, and to benefit from the social advantages of the host State on the same conditions as nationals. Equal treatment is a fundamental principle that ensures that intra-community workers receive the same treatment as nationals of the host State with regard to employment conditions, remuneration, social advantages, and vocational training.

Intra-community workers have the right to reside in the host State without the need for special authorization, and may apply for a residence certificate attesting to their right to reside and work in the host State. Intra-community workers also have the right to equal treatment with regard to access to employment, working conditions, remuneration, unemployment benefits, healthcare, and education. Equal treatment ensures that intra-community workers are not discriminated against on the grounds of their nationality and that they can benefit from the same opportunities as nationals of the host State. Intra-community workers who return to their State of origin after having worked in another Member State have the right to retain the unemployment benefits they have acquired in the host State and to export them to their State of origin for a specified period.

### The Coordination of Social Security Systems

The coordination of social security systems is a fundamental aspect of the free movement of workers, as it ensures that intra-community workers can benefit from social security benefits on equal terms with nationals of the host State. Regulation (EEC) No 1408/71 and Regulation (EC) No 883/2004 establish the rules for the coordination of social security systems, including the rules for determining the applicable legislation, the export of benefits, the calculation of benefits, and equal treatment. The coordination rules ensure that intra-community workers do not lose their social security rights when they move to another Member State to work, and that they can benefit from the social security benefits of the host State on the same conditions as nationals.

Regulation (EC) No 883/2004 establishes the principle of unity of legislation, which ensures that intra-community workers are subject only to the social security legislation of a single Member State, generally that of the place of work, which avoids double contributions and guarantees equal treatment. The Regulation also establishes the rules for the export of benefits, which allow intra-community workers to export social security benefits acquired in one Member State to another Member State, including pension benefits, temporary incapacity benefits, and unemployment benefits. The coordination of social security systems is a fundamental instrument for ensuring the free movement of workers, as it eliminates the administrative and financial barriers that could hinder labor mobility in the internal market.

## Chapter 8: Current Challenges of the Single Market

### The Digital Divide and the Single Market

The digital divide is one of the main challenges facing the single market today, as differences in access to digital technologies and digital skills between European regions can hinder the efficient functioning of the internal market. The European Union has adopted an ambitious package of measures to close the digital divide, including the Strategy for the Digital Single Market, which aims to create a truly integrated digital market, and the Data Strategy, which aims to create a common data space in the European Union. These strategies seek to eliminate the remaining barriers to the free movement of digital services and ensure that all European citizens and businesses can benefit from the opportunities of the digital economy.

The digital divide between European regions is a significant challenge that the European Union must address through investments in high-speed digital infrastructure, digital skills training, and the promotion of digital adoption by businesses and public administrations. The EU's broadband strategy aims to ensure that all European households have access to high-speed broadband services by 2030, which is an essential requirement for the efficient functioning of the digital market. Digital skills training is another pillar of the EU's digital strategy, as citizens and workers need to have adequate digital skills to fully benefit from the opportunities of the digital economy.

### The Regulation of Digital Platforms

The regulation of digital platforms is a growing challenge for the single market, as large digital platforms play an increasingly important role as intermediaries between users and as gatekeepers of access to the digital market. The European Union has adopted an ambitious regulatory framework to regulate digital platforms, including the Digital Markets Regulation and the Digital Services Regulation, which aim to ensure fair and open practices in the digital market while protecting the fundamental rights of users. The regulation of digital platforms is a complex challenge that requires finding a balance between promoting innovation and competition, and protecting the fundamental rights of users.

Digital platforms such as Google, Apple, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft have a significant impact on the internal market, as they act as intermediaries between users and providers of goods and services, and can influence consumer purchasing decisions and business opportunities for companies. The Digital Markets Regulation addresses the anticompetitive practices of large digital platforms by imposing specific obligations on them to ensure fair competition and innovation in the digital market. The Digital Services Regulation, for its part, establishes transparency obligations, content moderation, and user protection obligations for digital service providers, ensuring that digital services circulate freely throughout the internal market while protecting the fundamental rights of users.

## Chapter 9: The Single Market and European Competitiveness

### Competition Policy in the Single Market

Competition policy is a fundamental instrument for ensuring the efficient functioning of the single market, as it promotes competition among companies and prevents anticompetitive practices that may hinder intra-community trade. The European Union's competition policy includes the control of mergers and acquisitions, the fight against collusive agreements and abuse of dominant position, and the control of state aid. The European Commission has the responsibility of applying competition rules in the internal market, and may initiate investigations, impose sanctions, and order the modification or prohibition of anticompetitive practices.

The control of mergers and acquisitions aims to prevent corporate concentration operations from creating or reinforcing a dominant position that may hinder effective competition in the internal market. The European Commission must be notified of all mergers and acquisitions that exceed certain turnover thresholds, and has the power to authorize them, impose conditions, or prohibit them if they create or reinforce a dominant position that may hinder effective competition in the internal market. The fight against collusive agreements and abuse of dominant position aims to ensure that companies compete fairly in the internal market, preventing restrictive agreements and abuse of dominant position from hindering intra-community trade and harming consumers. The control of state aid aims to prevent Member States from granting aid that may distort competition in the internal market, favoring certain companies or sectors to the detriment of competitors.

### The Capital Markets Union

The Capital Markets Union is an ambitious initiative of the European Union that aims to create a single capital market that allows European companies to access more diverse sources of financing at lower costs, and that allows European investors to diversify their investment portfolios across national borders. The Capital Markets Union complements the Banking Union, which has created a more integrated banking market, and aims to eliminate the remaining barriers to the free movement of capital in the internal market. The Capital Markets Union includes measures to harmonize securities issuance rules, facilitate cross-border investment, improve investor protection, and create a more efficient dispute resolution framework.

The Capital Markets Union aims to create a truly integrated capital market in which European companies can access financing through securities markets, without relying exclusively on bank credit. The diversification of financing sources is important for the resilience of the European financial system, as it reduces dependence on the banking sector and facilitates the efficient allocation of financial resources. The Capital Markets Union also benefits European investors, who can diversify their investment portfolios across national borders and access a wider range of investment opportunities. The creation of an integrated capital market is a long-term project that requires the harmonization of national rules, the elimination of fiscal barriers, and the improvement of cooperation between financial supervisors.

## Chapter 10: The Future of the Single Market

### The Strategy for the Single Market Post-2025

The European single market faces significant challenges in the coming decades, including the acceleration of digital transformation, the ecological transition, demographic changes, growing international competition, and geopolitical challenges. The European Union has adopted a strategy for the single market post-2025 that aims to modernize and strengthen the internal market to address these challenges and ensure that the single market continues to be an engine of growth, employment, and well-being for European citizens. The strategy includes measures to complete the single market for services, close the digital divide, promote innovation, improve European competitiveness, and ensure that the benefits of the single market are distributed equitably among citizens and regions.

## Chapter 5: History and Evolution of the Single Market

### The Origins of the Common Market (1957-1986)

The origins of the European single market date back to the Treaty of Rome of 1957, which created the European Economic Community with the objective of establishing a common market between its Member States. The Treaty of Rome established the foundations for the progressive elimination of customs duties and quantitative restrictions on trade between the Member States, and for the adoption of a common external customs tariff vis-a-vis third countries. The treaty also established the foundations for the free movement of persons, services, and capital, although the implementation of these freedoms was gradual and was completed with the Single European Act in 1986. The Treaty of Rome laid the legal foundations of the common market, but its implementation was slow and was hampered by differences between the Member States regarding the pace and scope of economic integration.

The elimination of customs duties between the Member States was completed in 1968, when the customs union was established, eliminating all tariffs for intra-community trade and establishing a common external customs tariff for imports from third countries. The customs union was an important milestone in the construction of the common market, as it eliminated one of the most significant barriers to intra-community trade and created a unified economic space in which products could circulate freely without being subject to customs controls at borders. However, the elimination of customs duties was not sufficient to create a truly integrated market, as numerous non-tariff barriers persisted that hindered intra-community trade, such as differences in technical standards, national regulations, and fiscal barriers.

The period between 1968 and 1986 was one of relative stagnation in the construction of the common market, with few significant advances in the elimination of non-tariff barriers. Differences between the Member States regarding the pace and scope of integration, the lack of political will to reform national regulations, and the technical complexity of non-tariff barriers hindered progress in the construction of the internal market. This stagnation led to a growing recognition of the need for an ambitious reform that would accelerate the completion of the internal market, which resulted in the Single European Act in 1986.

### The Single European Act and the Completion of the Internal Market (1986-1993)

The Single European Act, signed in 1986 and entered into force in 1987, represented the first major revision of the Treaty of Rome and marked a turning point in the construction of the single market. The Single Act established the objective of completing the internal market before 31 December 1992, defining it as an area without internal frontiers in which the free movement of persons, goods, services, and capital was guaranteed. The Single Act also profoundly reformed the decision-making process in the Council of Ministers, extending the use of qualified majority voting to new policy areas and creating mechanisms to accelerate the legislative process. These reforms were crucial to overcoming the institutional deadlock that had characterized Europe in the 1970s and to allowing the Council to adopt decisions more quickly.

The Single Act represented a series of fundamental innovations that profoundly transformed the institutional and political architecture of the European Communities. The treaty established the objective of completing the internal market before 1992, setting a timetable with 279 measures necessary to eliminate the remaining barriers to free movement. The treaty also introduced the cooperation procedure, which gave the European Parliament a more active role in decision-making, and created European Political Cooperation, which was a mechanism for coordinating the positions of the Member States in matters of foreign policy. The Single Act was the result of complex negotiations between the governments of the Member States, the European Commission, and representatives of the business sector, and laid the foundations for the completion of the internal market in 1992.

The completion of the internal market before 1992 meant the elimination of thousands of technical, fiscal, border, and political barriers that prevented the free movement of persons, goods, services, and capital. Technical barriers included differences in national standards and standards that prevented the marketing of products from one country in another. These barriers were eliminated through the adoption of common European standards, the mutual recognition of national standards, and the harmonization of regulations in areas such as safety, health, and the environment. Fiscal barriers included VAT differentials between the Member States, which created distortions in trade and competition. These barriers were reduced through the harmonization of VAT rates and the adoption of common rules on the tax base.

### Subsequent Reforms and the Europe 2020 Strategy (1993-2010)

The period between 1993 and 2010 saw subsequent reforms that consolidated and expanded the internal market, adapting it to new economic and technological challenges. The creation of the European Union in 1993, through the Treaty of Maastricht, extended the competences of the EU to new areas such as economic and monetary policy, foreign and security policy, and cooperation in justice and home affairs. The Treaty of Maastricht also introduced European citizenship, which granted citizens of the Member States a series of rights, including the right to reside and work in any Member State. The Treaty of Amsterdam in 1997 strengthened the powers of the European Parliament and extended the scope of qualified majority voting in the Council, improving the effectiveness and legitimacy of European institutions.

The Treaty of Nice in 2001 prepared the EU for the enlargement to Central and Eastern Europe, reforming the institutions to make them capable of functioning effectively with a much larger number of Member States. The Treaty of Lisbon in 2007 simplified the institutional framework, created the offices of President of the European Council and High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy, and strengthened the powers of the European Parliament. These institutional reforms improved the effectiveness and legitimacy of European institutions, allowing better management of the internal market with an increasing number of Member States. The Treaty of Lisbon also incorporated the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, which sets out the civil, political, economic, and social rights of European citizens and persons residing in the EU.

The Europe 2020 Strategy for smart, sustainable, and inclusive growth, adopted in 2010, established strategic priorities for the EU that included promoting a knowledge-based and innovation-driven economy, promoting an economy with a high level of employment and social cohesion, and promoting an economy that is efficient in the use of resources and respectful of the environment. The Europe 2020 Strategy recognized that the internal market was a fundamental driver of economic growth and employment, and established measures to strengthen the internal market in areas such as digitalization, research and innovation, and territorial cohesion. The Europe 2020 Strategy also set the objective of allocating 3 percent of the EU's gross national product to research and development, and of increasing the employment rate of the working-age population to 75 percent.

### Recent Reforms and the Single Market Strategy Post-2025 (2010-2027)

The period between 2010 and 2027 saw recent reforms that adapted the internal market to new economic, technological, and social challenges. The global financial crisis of 2008 and the euro crisis of 2010-2012 exposed the vulnerabilities of the internal market and the need for structural reforms to improve economic resilience. The refugee crisis of 2015 highlighted the importance of the free movement of persons and the need for more effective mechanisms for managing migration flows. The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 highlighted the importance of European supply chains and the need to ensure the provision of essential goods in crisis situations. These challenges led to reforms of the internal market that included the creation of crisis response mechanisms, the improvement of supply chain resilience, and the adaptation of the internal market to digital transformation and the ecological transition.

The Single Market Strategy Post-2025, presented by the European Commission in 2020, sets out an ambitious vision for the future of the internal market. The strategy recognizes that the internal market must adapt to the economic, technological, and social changes that are transforming the European economy, and establishes measures to strengthen the internal market in areas such as digital transformation, the ecological transition, international competitiveness, and social cohesion. The strategy also recognizes the importance of protecting the internal market from unfair trade practices, including distorting foreign subsidies and dumping, and establishes mechanisms to ensure that the internal market is a space of fair and open competition for all companies operating within it. The single market strategy post-2025 is an ambitious framework that will guide internal market policy in the coming decades, adapting it to the challenges of the 21st century.

## Chapter 6: Free Movement of Persons

### The Rights of European Citizens

The free movement of persons is one of the fundamental freedoms of the single market and one of the most important rights of European citizens. The Treaty on European Union establishes that every EU citizen has the right to move and reside freely within the territory of the Member States, a right that may be invoked directly before national courts. This right includes the right to reside in any Member State for a period exceeding three months, the right to work or engage in an economic activity, the right to access education and training, and the right to access social services and healthcare on equal terms with nationals of the host State. The free movement of persons is a fundamental right that has transformed the lives of millions of European citizens, allowing them to study, work, and live in any EU country.

European citizens who move to another Member State for a period exceeding three months must register with the authorities of the host State and provide documents proving that they have sufficient economic resources, health insurance, and accommodation. European citizens who work in another Member State have the right to equal treatment in employment, including equal working conditions, equal remuneration, and access to social security. European citizens who reside in another Member State have the right to vote and stand as candidates in European and municipal elections of the host State, which allows them to participate in the democratic life of the country where they reside. The free movement of persons is a right that has promoted the social and cultural integration of European citizens, strengthening European identity and the awareness of belonging to a shared political community.

The free movement of persons also includes the right to non-discrimination on grounds of nationality in access to employment, education, social services, and healthcare. The principle of equal treatment prevents Member States from discriminating against citizens of other Member States in access to the rights and benefits established by national legislation. This principle has been developed by the case law of the Court of Justice of the EU, which has established that European citizens have the right to equal treatment in all areas of economic and social life, including access to employment, working conditions, social security, education, and healthcare. Non-discrimination is a fundamental principle of the internal market that ensures that European citizens can fully benefit from the free movement of persons.

### The Coordination of Social Security Systems

The coordination of social security systems is a fundamental aspect of the free movement of persons, as it ensures that European citizens who move to another Member State maintain their social security rights and do not lose benefits as a result of changing country. The Regulation on the Coordination of Social Security Systems establishes the rules for the coordination of social security systems of the Member States, including the aggregation of insurance periods, the export of benefits, and equal treatment between nationals and citizens of other Member States. The coordination of social security systems is a fundamental instrument for ensuring that the free movement of persons is not hindered by differences between national social security systems.

The aggregation of insurance periods allows European citizens who have worked in different Member States to aggregate the insurance periods for the calculation of social security benefits, such as pension, disability, and unemployment benefits. This mechanism ensures that European citizens do not lose social security rights as a result of having worked in different countries, and that they can access benefits corresponding to the entirety of their insurance periods, regardless of the country in which they worked. The export of benefits allows European citizens who reside in a Member State other than that in which they acquired social security rights to receive the corresponding benefits, which ensures that the free movement of persons is not hindered by the loss of benefits.

Equal treatment prevents Member States from discriminating against citizens of other Member States in access to social security benefits. This principle has been developed by the case law of the Court of Justice of the EU, which has established that European citizens have the right to equal treatment in access to social security benefits, including family benefits, health benefits, and pension benefits. The coordination of social security systems is a fundamental instrument for ensuring that the free movement of persons is effective and that European citizens can fully benefit from their right to move and reside freely within the territory of the Member States.

### The Schengen Area and the Abolition of Border Controls

The Schengen Agreement, signed in 1985 and supplemented by the Convention of Implementation in 1990, established the abolition of border controls at internal borders between participating Member States, creating an area of free movement without border controls. The Schengen Area was incorporated into the community acquis by the Treaty of Amsterdam in 1997, and currently includes 26 countries: 22 EU Member States plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and Switzerland. The abolition of internal border controls has meant a profound transformation of the lives of European citizens, who can move freely throughout the Schengen Area without being subject to identity checks at borders, facilitating travel for work, studies, tourism, or family reasons.

The Schengen Area is complemented by compensatory measures to ensure security at external borders, including systematic identity checks at external borders, police and judicial cooperation between the Member States, and the Schengen Information System (SIS), which is a database of wanted persons and objects that enables national authorities to exchange information on persons who pose a threat to public security. The Schengen compensatory measures are fundamental to ensuring that the abolition of internal border controls does not compromise public security, and to enabling Member States to address cross-border threats such as organized crime, terrorism, and illegal immigration.

The Schengen Area has faced significant challenges in recent years, including the refugee crisis of 2015, terrorist attacks, and the COVID-19 pandemic, which led some Member States to temporarily reintroduce internal border controls. These challenges have highlighted the need to strengthen the management of external borders and to improve cooperation between Member States to ensure the security and functioning of the Schengen Area. The European Border and Coast Guard Agency (Frontex) has been strengthened to improve the management of external borders, and new rules have been adopted to ensure that temporary reintroductions of border controls are carried out in an exceptional and proportionate manner. The Schengen Area remains a fundamental achievement of the internal market that enables the free movement of persons and has transformed the lives of European citizens.

### The Challenges of Free Movement of Persons

The free movement of persons faces significant challenges that require innovative solutions and cooperation between the Member States. One of the main challenges is the management of migration flows, which has generated tensions between the Member States regarding the equitable distribution of refugees and asylum policies. The refugee crisis of 2015 highlighted the limitations of the European asylum system and the need for greater solidarity between the Member States in the management of migration flows. The common asylum policy seeks to harmonize national asylum rules and ensure dignified and equitable treatment for asylum seekers, but its implementation has faced difficulties due to differences between national asylum policies.

Another important challenge is the fight against cross-border crime, which requires greater police and judicial cooperation between the Member States. Organized crime, terrorism, and cybercrime are cross-border threats that require coordinated responses from the authorities of different countries. Police and judicial cooperation has been strengthened through instruments such as Europol, Eurojust, and the European Arrest Warrant, but challenges remain in the coordination between national authorities. The free movement of persons requires an appropriate balance between the freedom of movement and public security, and the EU must continue to work to find this balance.

The free movement of persons also faces social challenges, such as the discrimination of citizens of other Member States in access to employment, housing, and public services. Despite legal provisions prohibiting discrimination, European citizens who move to another Member State may face practical barriers such as linguistic differences, cultural differences, and a lack of information about their rights. The EU must continue to work to eliminate these practical barriers and to ensure that all European citizens can fully benefit from the free movement of persons. The free movement of persons is a fundamental right that has transformed the lives of European citizens and continues to be a fundamental pillar of the internal market.

The European Union has also adopted rules for the protection of personal data in the digital market, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which establishes strict rules for the collection, use, and protection of personal data by companies and public administrations. The GDPR establishes citizens' rights over their personal data, including the right of access, the right to rectification, the right to erasure, and the right to data portability, and establishes obligations for companies that collect and process personal data, including obtaining consent, limiting processing, and notifying security breaches. ## Chapter 9: The Enforcement and Implementation of Internal Market Law

### Infringement Procedures

Infringement procedures are the principal instrument for ensuring the effective application of internal market law in the Member States. The European Commission, as guardian of the Treaties, has the responsibility of monitoring the application of European law in the Member States and of initiating infringement proceedings against States that do not correctly apply the rules of the internal market. Infringement proceedings are initiated when the Commission has grounds to consider that a Member State has not fulfilled its obligations under the Treaties or European legislation, and develop in several phases, including the pre-contentious phase, the contentious phase, and the enforcement phase.

The pre-contentious phase of the infringement procedure begins with a letter of formal notice, which the Commission sends to the Member State informing it of the reasons why it considers that it has not fulfilled its obligations. The Member State has a time limit to respond and, if the Commission is not satisfied with the response, it issues a reasoned opinion in which it sets out the measures that the Member State must adopt to fulfill its obligations. If the Member State does not comply with the reasoned opinion, the Commission may refer the matter to the Court of Justice of the EU, which may impose financial sanctions on the non-complying Member State. Infringement procedures are a fundamental instrument for ensuring that Member States correctly apply the rules of the internal market and for protecting the rights of citizens and companies.

The European Commission also has the competence to initiate infringement proceedings against Member States for the incorrect transposition of European directives into national law. The transposition of directives is a process by which Member States adapt their national legislation to comply with the objectives set out in European directives, and incorrect transposition may lead to distortions in the internal market and the loss of rights for citizens and companies. The Commission monitors the transposition of directives and, when it detects errors or deficiencies, initiates infringement proceedings to ensure correct transposition. Infringement proceedings for incorrect transposition are a fundamental instrument for ensuring the harmonization of national legislations and the effective functioning of the internal market.

### The Principles of Competition Policy

Competition policy is one of the most important instruments for ensuring the effective functioning of the internal market, as it seeks to prevent and sanction practices that distort competition in the internal market and that may harm consumers, companies, and economic integration. The fundamental principles of European competition policy include the prohibition of collusive agreements, the prohibition of abuse of dominant position, the control of mergers and acquisitions, and the control of state aid. These principles are set out in Articles 101 and 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union and in Regulation 1/2003, which establishes the rules for the application of Articles 101 and 102.

The prohibition of collusive agreements establishes that are incompatible with the internal market all agreements between companies which have as their object or effect the restriction or distortion of competition in the internal market. Collusive agreements include price-fixing agreements, market sharing, limitation of production, and the fixing of common commercial conditions. The European Commission has the competence to investigate and sanction collusive agreements, and may impose fines that may reach up to 10 percent of the worldwide annual turnover of the companies involved. The prohibition of collusive agreements is fundamental to ensuring that competition in the internal market is fair and open, and to protecting the interests of consumers and companies operating in the internal market.

The prohibition of abuse of dominant position establishes that is incompatible with the internal market the abuse by one or more companies of a dominant position in the internal market or in a substantial part of it. Abuse of dominant position includes practices such as imposing unfair prices, limiting production, discrimination between customers, and refusing to provide services. The European Commission has the competence to investigate and sanction abuses of dominant position, and may impose fines that may reach up to 10 percent of the worldwide annual turnover of the companies involved. The prohibition of abuse of dominant position is fundamental to ensuring that companies with a dominant position do not use their market power to harm consumers and competitors.

### The Control of Mergers and Acquisitions

The control of mergers and acquisitions is a fundamental instrument of competition policy that seeks to prevent corporate concentrations that may significantly restrict competition in the internal market. The European Commission has the competence to investigate mergers and acquisitions that exceed the established thresholds, and may authorize them, impose conditions, or prohibit them if it considers that they significantly restrict competition in the internal market.

The control of mergers and acquisitions is a preventive instrument that seeks to prevent corporate concentrations from creating dominant positions that may be used to restrict competition in the internal market. The European Commission assesses mergers and acquisitions based on their impact on competition, taking into account factors such as the market share of the companies involved, the concentration of the market, the existence of potential competitors, and barriers to market entry. The Commission may authorize mergers without conditions, impose conditions to ensure that competition is not significantly restricted, or prohibit mergers if it considers that they significantly restrict competition in the internal market. The control of mergers and acquisitions is fundamental to ensuring that the internal market is a space of fair and open competition for all companies operating within it.

The control of mergers and acquisitions also has a cross-border dimension, as many mergers and acquisitions affect markets in several Member States. The European Commission has the competence to investigate mergers and acquisitions that affect the internal market, regardless of the nationality of the companies involved, which ensures uniform control of corporate concentrations throughout the internal market. The Commission also cooperates with national competition authorities in the investigation of mergers and acquisitions affecting national or regional markets, ensuring the coordinated application of competition rules throughout the internal market. The control of mergers and acquisitions is a fundamental instrument for ensuring that the internal market is a space of fair and open competition for all companies operating within it.

### The Control of State Aid

The control of state aid is a fundamental instrument of competition policy that seeks to prevent national governments from distorting competition in the internal market through subsidies, tax exemptions, favorable loans, or other economic advantages in favor of specific companies. Article 107 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union establishes that are incompatible with the internal market state aid which distorts or may distort competition by favoring certain companies or certain sectors of production. The European Commission has the competence to investigate and sanction incompatible state aid, and may order the recovery of aid that has been unduly granted.

The control of state aid is fundamental to ensuring that companies compete on equal terms in the internal market, regardless of the nationality or location of the companies. State aid can significantly distort competition in the internal market, as it allows companies that receive aid to offer lower prices, invest more in research and development, or expand their markets more quickly than their competitors that do not receive aid. The European Commission assesses state aid based on its impact on competition, taking into account factors such as the size of the aid, the size of the affected market, the position of the beneficiary companies, and the existence of competitors. The Commission may authorize aid if it considers that it is compatible with the internal market, or prohibit it if it considers that it significantly distorts competition.

The control of state aid also has a regional dimension, as regional and local governments may grant aid that distorts competition in the internal market. The European Commission has established guidelines on regional state aid, which allow Member States to grant aid to less developed regions to promote economic development, but which establish limits to prevent aid from significantly distorting competition. The control of regional state aid is fundamental to ensuring that cohesion policy and regional aid are compatible with competition policy and with the effective functioning of the internal market.

## Chapter 11: The Common Policy of the Internal Market

### The Common Customs Tariff and the Customs Union

The common customs tariff is a fundamental instrument of the internal market that establishes a uniform tariff for imports from third countries, ensuring that all Member States apply the same tariffs to foreign imports. The customs union was established in 1968, when all tariffs for intra-community trade were eliminated and a common customs tariff was established for imports from third countries. The customs union is a prerequisite for the effective functioning of the internal market, as it ensures that products from third countries cannot enter the internal market through a Member State with lower tariffs, which would create distortions in competition and intra-community trade.

The European customs policy is managed by the European Commission, which has the competence to negotiate customs tariffs with third countries and to administer the common customs tariff. The Commission also has the competence to apply trade defense measures, such as anti-dumping duties and countervailing duties, to protect the internal market from unfair imports. The European customs policy is a fundamental instrument for ensuring that the internal market is protected from unfair trade practices and for promoting fair trade with third countries. The European customs policy also contributes to the financing of the European budget, as customs duties are one of the main sources of revenue for the EU.

The European customs union is one of the largest and most complex customs unions in the world, managing the trade of millions of tonnes of goods each year. European customs administration is carried out through national customs authorities, which apply European customs rules at the external borders of the EU. Cooperation between national customs authorities is fundamental to ensuring the uniform application of customs rules and to preventing customs fraud. The European Commission has established cooperation mechanisms between national customs authorities, including the Automated Information System (AIS) and the Anti-Fraud Network, which enable the exchange of information and operational cooperation between the customs authorities of the different Member States.

### The Common Commercial Policy

The common commercial policy is the instrument through which the EU acts as a single actor in international trade, negotiating trade agreements with third countries and international organizations. The common commercial policy is established in the European Treaties, which attribute to the EU exclusive competence over commercial policy, including the negotiation of free trade agreements, the imposition of customs tariffs, and the adoption of trade defense measures. The common commercial policy is a fundamental instrument for promoting the EU's trade interests in international trade and for ensuring that European companies can compete on equal terms with companies from other countries.

The EU has negotiated numerous trade agreements with countries around the world, including free trade agreements with Canada, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, and Vietnam, among others. These agreements eliminate or reduce customs tariffs and other trade barriers between the EU and partner countries, facilitating trade in goods and services and foreign investment. The EU's trade agreements also include provisions on the protection of human rights, the environment, and labor rights, ensuring that international trade is carried out in a sustainable and responsible manner. The common commercial policy is a fundamental instrument for promoting free trade and market opening within the framework of the World Trade Organization.

The common commercial policy also includes the adoption of trade defense measures to protect the internal market from unfair imports. Anti-dumping duties are imposed when it is demonstrated that products are being imported at prices lower than their normal value and that they cause significant injury to the European industry. Countervailing duties are imposed when it is demonstrated that products are being imported with the support of foreign subsidies that distort competition. Safeguard measures are imposed when a sudden increase in imports causes significant injury to the European industry. Trade defense policy is a fundamental instrument for protecting the internal market from unfair trade practices and for ensuring that European companies compete on equal terms with companies from other countries.

## Chapter 12: The Future of the Single Market

### The Geopolitical Challenges of the Internal Market

The internal market faces significant geopolitical challenges that require a coordinated response from the EU and the Member States. Growing economic competition from China, trade tensions with the United States, instability in the Middle East, and threats to global security are factors that affect the functioning of the internal market and that require a coherent and effective European response. The EU must ensure that the internal market is resilient to geopolitical disruptions and that it can function effectively in a context of growing international uncertainty.

The war in Ukraine has highlighted the importance of energy security and the diversification of supply sources for the functioning of the internal market. The EU's dependence on Russian gas has created significant vulnerabilities that have affected energy prices and the competitiveness of European companies. The EU has adopted measures to diversify energy supply sources, including investment in renewable energy, the construction of liquefied natural gas infrastructure, and the improvement of energy efficiency. These measures seek to reduce the EU's energy dependence and ensure the security of energy supply for the functioning of the internal market.

Economic competition from China poses significant challenges for the internal market, as Chinese companies may benefit from state subsidies, less stringent environmental and labor standards, and preferential access to the Chinese market, which may distort competition in the internal market. The EU has adopted measures to address these challenges, including the Anti-Foreign Subsidies Regulation, which allows the European Commission to investigate investment operations and public tenders in which companies receiving foreign subsidies participate, and the Investment Screening Mechanism, which allows Member States to review foreign investments on security grounds. These measures seek to protect the internal market from unfair trade practices and ensure that European companies compete on equal terms with companies from other countries.

### The Ecological Transition and the Internal Market

The ecological transition is one of the most important challenges facing the internal market in the coming decades. The EU has committed to achieving climate neutrality by 2050, and the internal market must adapt to this transition by adopting stricter environmental standards, promoting the circular economy, and incentivizing companies to adopt more sustainable practices. The ecological transition will require significant investments in renewable energy, energy efficiency, sustainable mobility, and waste management, and the internal market must facilitate these investments by removing barriers to trade in sustainable products and services.

The European Green Deal, presented by the Commission in 2019, establishes an ambitious framework for the transition to a climate-neutral economy by 2050. The Green Deal includes measures to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, protect biodiversity, promote the circular economy, and ensure a just transition for all regions and citizens. The internal market must adapt to these measures by adopting stricter environmental standards for products sold in the internal market, promoting eco-labeling and environmental information for consumers, and removing barriers to trade in sustainable products and services. The ecological transition is a challenge that requires a coordinated response from all European policies, including the internal market, competition policy, commercial policy, and cohesion policy.

The circular economy is an economic model that seeks to reduce waste and the overexploitation of resources through the reuse, repair, refurbishment, and recycling of products. The internal market must promote the circular economy by adopting rules that facilitate the reuse and recycling of products, removing barriers to trade in reused and recycled products, and incentivizing companies to adopt more sustainable production practices. The circular economy is a fundamental instrument for reducing the environmental impact of production and consumption, and for promoting more sustainable and resilient economic growth.

### Innovation and Competitiveness of the Internal Market

Innovation is a fundamental driver of the competitiveness of the internal market, and the EU must promote innovation through investment in research and development, the training of qualified human capital, and support for innovative companies. International competition demands that the internal market be sufficiently competitive to face the growing competition from emerging economies such as China, India, and Brazil, and that it promote innovation and productivity to maintain its position in the global economy.

The EU has adopted measures to promote innovation and the competitiveness of the internal market, including the Europe 2020 Strategy for smart, sustainable, and inclusive growth, the Smart Specialization Strategy, and the Research and Development Framework Programme. These strategies set ambitious objectives for investment in research and development, the training of qualified human capital, and support for innovative companies, and recognize that the internal market is a fundamental driver of innovation and competitiveness. The EU has also adopted measures to simplify regulatory rules, reduce administrative costs, and promote entrepreneurship, with the aim of making the internal market more attractive to innovative companies and foreign investment.

Artificial intelligence is one of the emerging technologies with the greatest potential to transform the European economy, and the EU has adopted a regulatory framework for the development and use of artificial intelligence systems in the internal market. The Regulation on Artificial Intelligence establishes rules for the development and use of artificial intelligence systems, ensuring safety, transparency, and respect for fundamental rights. The regulation classifies artificial intelligence systems according to their level of risk, and establishes stricter obligations for high-risk systems, including conformity assessment, algorithmic transparency, and human supervision. The artificial intelligence regulatory framework is fundamental to ensuring that technological innovation develops in a safe and rights-respecting manner in the internal market.

### Social Inclusion and Cohesion of the Internal Market

Social inclusion and cohesion are fundamental principles of the internal market that ensure that the benefits of economic integration are distributed equitably among all citizens and regions of the EU. The internal market must promote social inclusion by ensuring equal treatment, protecting workers' rights, promoting employment, and combating discrimination. Territorial cohesion is a fundamental instrument for reducing disparities between regions and for ensuring that all European regions can benefit from the opportunities offered by the internal market.

The internal market has generated significant benefits for European citizens, but has also created disparities between the most prosperous and the less developed regions. Less developed regions may have difficulty competing in the internal market due to a lack of infrastructure, qualified human capital, and investment. Cohesion policy is the principal instrument for reducing these territorial disparities, through investment in infrastructure, innovation, employment, and social development in less developed regions. Cohesion policy and the internal market are complementary and mutually reinforcing, as cohesion policy reduces territorial disparities and the internal market promotes economic growth and job creation.

Social inclusion of the internal market also requires the protection of workers' rights and the guarantee of fair working conditions throughout the internal market. The EU has adopted a broad regulatory framework for the protection of workers' rights, including directives on working time, equal treatment, health and safety at work, and the mobility of workers. These directives establish minimum standards for the protection of workers' rights throughout the internal market, ensuring that workers can benefit from the free movement of persons without losing their labor rights. The protection of workers' rights is fundamental to ensuring that the internal market is fair and equitable for all European citizens.

## Chapter 13: Free Movement of Capital and Financial Services

### The Liberalization of Capital Movements

The free movement of capital is one of the fundamental freedoms of the single market that guarantees the absence of restrictions on capital movements between the Member States and between them and third countries. The free movement of capital allows European investors to invest freely in any Member State, companies to obtain financing from foreign sources, and banks and other financial institutions to provide cross-border services. The free movement of capital is fundamental to the effective functioning of the internal market, as it allows the efficient allocation of financial resources throughout the European economy and facilitates investment and economic growth.

The Directive on the Liberalisation of Capital Movements, adopted in 1988, completed the liberalization of capital movements in the internal market, eliminating restrictions on capital movements between the Member States. The directive established the free movement of capital for all types of transactions, including investments in securities, company acquisitions, interbank loans, and personal capital transfers. The directive also established exceptions to the free movement of capital for reasons of public policy, public security, or economic policy, which could be invoked by the Member States in exceptional situations. The liberalization of capital movements was a decisive step toward the creation of an integrated financial market in the internal market.

The free movement of capital has been strengthened by the creation of the European single currency, which has eliminated exchange rate risks and significantly reduced the costs of cross-border financial transactions. The single currency has facilitated the integration of European financial markets, allowing companies and investors to access a wider range of financial instruments and more diverse sources of financing. The free movement of capital and the single currency are complementary and mutually reinforcing, contributing to the effective functioning of the internal market and the economic integration of the EU.

### The Regulatory Framework for Financial Services

The regulatory framework for financial services in the internal market is one of the most comprehensive and sophisticated in the world, and includes rules for the regulation of financial markets, investor protection, the prevention of money laundering, and the stability of the financial system. The European Union has adopted a broad regulatory framework for financial services, including the Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II), the Markets in Crypto-assets Directive (MiCA), the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive, the UCITS Directive, and the General Data Protection Regulation. This regulatory framework seeks to ensure the transparency, efficiency, and stability of European financial markets, and to protect investors and consumers of financial services.

The Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II), adopted in 2014, establishes rules for the regulation of financial markets, including securities markets, derivatives markets, and alternative trading systems. MiFID II establishes transparency rules for financial markets, including the publication of information on transactions, the obligation to record all transactions, and the prohibition of uncontrolled high-frequency trading. MiFID II also establishes rules for the protection of investors, including the obligation of financial service providers to act in the best interests of their clients, the obligation to provide clear and transparent information on financial products, and the obligation to classify clients according to their level of experience and financial knowledge.

The Markets in Crypto-assets Directive (MiCA), adopted in 2023, establishes rules for the regulation of crypto-assets in the internal market, including stablecoins, utility tokens, and investment tokens. MiCA establishes authorization and supervision requirements for crypto-asset service providers, including crypto-asset issuers, exchange service providers, and custody service providers. MiCA also establishes transparency rules for crypto-assets, including the obligation to provide clear and complete information on crypto-assets and associated risks, and the obligation to protect customer funds. MiCA is the first comprehensive regulatory framework for crypto-assets in the world, and makes the EU a benchmark in crypto-asset regulation.

### Financial Stability and the Internal Market

Financial stability is a fundamental requirement for the effective functioning of the internal market, as financial crises can have significant effects on the real economy and on the lives of European citizens. The European Union has developed a financial stability framework that includes macroprudential supervision, bank resolution, deposit insurance, and cooperation between supervisory authorities. This framework seeks to prevent financial crises, limit their impact when they occur, and protect depositors and investors.

The Banking Union, established in 2014, is a banking supervision framework that seeks to ensure the stability of the European banking system and protect depositors. The Banking Union includes the Single Supervisory Mechanism, which gives the European Central Bank the responsibility of supervising the most important banks in the euro area, and the Single Resolution Mechanism, which establishes a framework for the orderly resolution of failing banks without recourse to public funds. The Banking Union is a fundamental instrument for ensuring the stability of the European banking system and for protecting depositors and taxpayers in the event of banking crises.

The European Deposit Insurance Scheme establishes a deposit guarantee framework that protects depositors in the event of a bank failure. The scheme establishes a minimum level of deposit protection of 100,000 euros per depositor per bank, and guarantees the return of deposited funds within seven working days in the event of a bank failure. Deposit insurance is a fundamental instrument for maintaining depositors' confidence in the banking system and for preventing bank runs. The European Union has adopted measures to harmonize the deposit guarantee systems of the Member States and to ensure that all European depositors have the same level of protection.

### The Challenges of Financial Services in the Internal Market

Financial services face significant challenges in the internal market, including the need to adapt to digital transformation, to address cybersecurity risks, to protect consumers of financial services, and to ensure the stability of the financial system in a context of growing complexity and interconnection. Digital transformation is changing the way financial services are provided, with the rise of fintech, cryptocurrencies, and digital payment services, which requires new rules to regulate these new technologies and protect consumers.

Cybersecurity is a growing challenge for financial services, as cyberattacks can compromise the security of financial data, disrupt financial services, and cause significant economic losses. The European Union has adopted the Network and Information Security Directive (NIS2), which establishes cybersecurity rules for digital service providers and critical entities, including financial entities. The directive establishes obligations for the notification of security incidents, risk assessment, and the implementation of security measures. Cybersecurity is a fundamental challenge for financial services in the internal market, and the EU must continue to work to strengthen the resilience of the financial system against cyber threats.

The protection of consumers of financial services is another important challenge for the internal market. Consumers of financial services may be vulnerable to unfair practices, insufficient information, and complex financial products that they do not fully understand. The European Union has adopted a broad regulatory framework for the protection of consumers of financial services, including the Consumer Rights Directive, the Consumer Credit Directive, and the Payment Services Directive. These directives establish obligations for transparent information, creditworthiness assessment, and protection against unfair practices, ensuring that consumers can make informed decisions and are protected against financial risks. The protection of consumers of financial services is fundamental to maintaining confidence in the financial system and to ensuring the effective functioning of the internal market.

## Chapter 14: The Internal Market and Fundamental Rights

### The Protection of Fundamental Rights in the Internal Market

The protection of fundamental rights is a fundamental principle of the internal market that ensures that economic integration does not come at the expense of the civil, political, economic, and social rights of citizens. The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, incorporated into European law by the Treaty of Lisbon, establishes the fundamental rights that must be respected by EU institutions and by the Member States in the implementation of European law. The Charter establishes the rights of dignity, the rights of freedom, the rights of equality, the rights of solidarity, the rights of citizenship, and the rights of justice, and has the same legal value as the European Treaties.

The protection of fundamental rights in the internal market extends to all areas of economic integration, including the free movement of persons, goods, services, and capital, competition policy, commercial policy, and cohesion policy. The Court of Justice of the EU has the responsibility of ensuring that the rules of the internal market are applied in a manner respectful of fundamental rights, and has developed important case law on the protection of fundamental rights in the internal market. The Court has established that restrictions on the fundamental freedoms of the internal market must be proportionate and necessary to ensure the protection of fundamental rights, and that fundamental rights may justify restrictions on free movement when they are necessary to protect fundamental values such as human dignity, freedom, equality, and solidarity.

The protection of fundamental rights in the internal market also includes the protection of labor rights, which are fundamental to ensuring that economic integration benefits workers and not just companies. The European Union has adopted a broad regulatory framework for the protection of labor rights, including directives on working time, equal treatment, health and safety at work, and the mobility of workers. These directives establish minimum standards for the protection of labor rights throughout the internal market, ensuring that workers can benefit from the free movement of persons without losing their labor rights. The protection of labor rights is fundamental to ensuring that the internal market is fair and equitable for all European citizens.

### Equal Treatment and Non-Discrimination

Equal treatment and non-discrimination are fundamental principles of the internal market that ensure that all European citizens have the same opportunities and the same rights regardless of their nationality, gender, race, religion, disability, age, or sexual orientation. Equal treatment prevents Member States from discriminating against citizens of other Member States in access to employment, education, social services, and healthcare. Non-discrimination prevents companies from discriminating against their employees or customers on the grounds of nationality, gender, race, religion, disability, age, or sexual orientation.

The Equal Treatment in Employment Directive establishes rules for equal treatment between men and women in employment, including equal access to employment, equal working conditions, equal remuneration, and protection against dismissal on grounds of pregnancy or maternity. The directive also establishes rules for the prevention and combating of sexual harassment and gender-based discrimination in employment. Equal treatment between men and women is a fundamental principle of the internal market that contributes to social cohesion and economic growth by fully utilizing the potential of the entire working population.

The Framework Equal Treatment in Employment and Occupation Directive establishes rules for non-discrimination on grounds of disability, age, sexual orientation, religion, or beliefs in employment. The directive establishes the principle of equal treatment, which prohibits direct and indirect discrimination, harassment, and retaliation on any of the grounds mentioned. The directive also establishes reasonable accommodation obligations for employers, who must take the necessary measures to accommodate workers with disabilities in the workplace. Non-discrimination in employment is fundamental to ensuring that all European citizens have the same opportunities for access to employment and professional development in the internal market.

### Personal Data Protection in the Internal Market

Personal data protection is a fundamental right that has acquired growing importance in the internal market, due to digital transformation and the massive collection of data by companies and public administrations. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), adopted in 2016 and applicable since 2018, is the world's most important regulatory framework for personal data protection, and establishes strict rules for the collection, use, and protection of personal data by companies and public administrations of the EU.

The GDPR establishes citizens' rights over their personal data, including the right of access, which allows citizens to know what personal data is collected about them and how it is used; the right to rectification, which allows citizens to correct inaccurate personal data; the right to erasure, which allows citizens to request the deletion of their personal data; the right to data portability, which allows citizens to transfer their personal data from one provider to another; and the right to object, which allows citizens to object to the processing of their personal data for direct marketing purposes or for legitimate reasons. The GDPR also establishes obligations for companies that collect and process personal data, including obtaining explicit consent from data subjects, limiting processing to the specific purposes for which the data was collected, and notifying security breaches to supervisory authorities and affected data subjects.

The GDPR has made the EU a global benchmark for personal data protection, and has influenced the legislation of other countries around the world. The GDPR has demonstrated that it is possible to protect citizens' fundamental rights in the digital economy while promoting innovation and the competitiveness of European companies. The GDPR has also created a single market for personal data, ensuring that personal data can circulate freely between the Member States with a uniform level of protection, which facilitates e-commerce, digital services, and scientific research. Personal data protection is a fundamental right that must be respected in all areas of the internal market, and the EU must continue to work to ensure that the data protection regulatory framework adapts to technological changes and new threats to privacy.

## Chapter 15: The Internal Market and Public Services

### The Free Provision of Public Services in the Internal Market

Public services are services of general economic interest that are provided by public entities or by private entities under public control, and that are fundamental to ensuring social cohesion and equal opportunities for European citizens. Public services include education, health, public transport, energy, water, telecommunications, and postal services, which are essential for the quality of life of citizens and for the functioning of the economy. The European Union has recognized the importance of public services for social cohesion and has established a regulatory framework that ensures the provision of quality public services throughout the internal market.

The Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union recognizes the role of services of general economic interest in promoting social and territorial cohesion, and establishes that free competition and the rules of the internal market must not hinder the provision of services of general economic interest. Protocol 26, annexed to the Treaties, establishes that services of general economic interest contribute to the promotion of social and territorial cohesion in the Union, and that Member States have the responsibility of ensuring the provision of services of general economic interest under conditions that guarantee their access, quality, and continuity. The regulatory framework for services of general economic interest seeks to find a balance between competition in the internal market and the provision of quality public services.

The European Commission has adopted guidelines on services of general economic interest, which establish the principles for the provision of public services in the internal market. These guidelines establish that services of general economic interest must be provided under conditions that guarantee their universal access, quality, continuity, and affordability, and that Member States have the responsibility of defining services of general economic interest, of financing their provision, and of supervising their quality. The guidelines also establish that competition and internal market rules apply to services of general economic interest, but that state aid intended for the provision of services of general economic interest may be compatible with the internal market if it meets certain conditions.

### The Privatization and Liberalization of Public Services

The privatization and liberalization of public services are processes by which Member States transfer the provision of public services from public entities to private entities, or open public service markets to competition. The privatization and liberalization of public services are controversial processes that have generated debates about the balance between economic efficiency and social cohesion. Proponents of privatization argue that competition in public service markets can improve efficiency, reduce prices, and improve the quality of services. Critics argue that privatization may compromise universal access, continuity, and affordability of public services, and that public services should be provided by public entities to ensure that they serve citizens and not business profits.

The European Union has adopted a regulatory framework for the liberalization of public service markets, including directives on the liberalization of energy, telecommunications, transport, and postal service markets. These directives establish rules for the opening of markets to competition, the separation of production and distribution activities, and the regulation of prices and service quality. The liberalization of public service markets has meant a profound transformation of the provision of public services in the EU, allowing consumers to choose between different service providers and benefit from lower prices and a wider variety of services. However, the liberalization of public services has also generated concerns about universal access and service quality, especially in rural areas and less developed regions.

The European Union has adopted measures to ensure that the liberalization of public services is compatible with social and territorial cohesion. The Block Exemption Regulation for Services of General Economic Interest establishes that state aid intended for the provision of services of general economic interest may be compatible with the internal market if it meets certain conditions, including the clear definition of the service of general economic interest, adequate compensation for the service provider, and supervision of service quality. The de minimis Regulation establishes that state aid of lower amounts is not subject to the control of the European Commission, which allows Member States to grant aid to providers of services of general economic interest without prior authorization from the Commission. These measures seek to ensure that the liberalization of public services is compatible with social and territorial cohesion, and that public services remain accessible, quality, and affordable for all European citizens.

### Universal Access to Public Services

Universal access to public services is a fundamental principle of the internal market that ensures that all European citizens, regardless of where they live, have access to quality public services at affordable prices. Universal access to public services is essential for social and territorial cohesion, as it ensures that citizens of rural areas and less developed regions do not suffer significant disadvantages in access to essential services such as education, health, public transport, and energy. The European Union has adopted measures to promote universal access to public services, including the funding of investments in public service infrastructure in rural areas and less developed regions, and the adoption of rules that guarantee the quality and continuity of public services.

Universal access to public services includes the guarantee that public services are available throughout the European territory, that they are affordable for all citizens, that they are of quality, and that they are provided on a continuous basis. Universal access to public services requires investment in public service infrastructure, including roads, railways, power lines, telecommunications networks, and health care centers, which are necessary to ensure that public services reach all citizens. Cohesion policy is the principal instrument for funding these investments, transferring resources from the most prosperous regions to the less developed ones to ensure that all European regions have adequate public service infrastructure.

Universal access to public services also includes the guarantee that public services are affordable for all citizens, including citizens with limited economic resources. Member States have adopted measures to ensure the affordability of public services, including social tariffs, subsidies, and tax exemptions for low-income households. The European Union has adopted guidelines on services of general economic interest that establish that public services must be provided under conditions that guarantee their accessibility and affordability, and that Member States have the responsibility of ensuring that public services are accessible and affordable for all citizens. Universal access to public services is a fundamental principle of the internal market that contributes to social cohesion and equal opportunities for European citizens.

### The Future of Public Services in the Internal Market

The future of public services in the internal market is marked by digital transformation, the ecological transition, and the growing demand for quality public services by European citizens. Digital transformation is changing the way public services are provided, with the development of digital public services that allow citizens to access public services through the internet and mobile devices. The ecological transition requires investment in sustainable public services, including electric public transport, renewable energy, and sustainable water and waste management. The growing demand for quality public services by European citizens requires investment in the improvement of public service quality, the training of public service professionals, and the participation of citizens in the design and evaluation of public services.

The digital transformation of public services is an important challenge that requires significant investments in digital infrastructure, in the training of public officials, and in the adaptation of administrative procedures to new technologies. The European Union has adopted measures to promote the digital transformation of public services, including the EU Digital Strategy for 2030, which sets ambitious objectives for the digitalization of public services, and the eGovernment Action Plan, which promotes the provision of digital public services through the European digital services platform. The digital transformation of public services is a fundamental instrument for improving the efficiency, transparency, and accessibility of public services, and for ensuring that citizens can access public services easily and securely.

The ecological transition of public services is another important challenge that requires investment in sustainable public services and the adoption of more environmentally friendly management practices. Public services play a fundamental role in the ecological transition, as they are responsible for a significant share of greenhouse gas emissions and energy and natural resource consumption. The European Union has adopted measures to promote the ecological transition of public services, including the funding of investments in electric public transport, renewable energy, energy efficiency of public buildings, and sustainable water and waste management. The ecological transition of public services is a fundamental instrument for reducing the environmental impact of public administration and for promoting sustainability throughout the internal market.

## Chapter 16: The Internal Market and Business Innovation

### Entrepreneurship in the Internal Market

Entrepreneurship is a fundamental driver of job creation, innovation, and economic growth in the internal market. The European Union has adopted measures to promote entrepreneurship, including the simplification of company formation procedures, the reduction of administrative costs, access to financing, and business training. The Entrepreneurship Strategy, adopted by the European Commission, establishes measures to create a more favorable business environment in the internal market, including the simplification of regulatory rules, the reduction of barriers to market entry, and the promotion of business culture.

The free movement of companies is a fundamental principle of the internal market that allows entrepreneurs to establish companies in any Member State and to run their businesses from anywhere in the internal market. The free movement of companies includes the right to set up a company, to transfer the registered office to another Member State, and to create branches or subsidiaries in other Member States. The free movement of companies facilitates the creation of pan-European companies operating in several Member States, and promotes competition and innovation in the internal market. The European Union has adopted measures to facilitate the free movement of companies, including the simplification of company formation procedures, the reduction of formation costs, and the elimination of administrative barriers to the transfer of the registered office.

Access to financing is one of the main challenges facing entrepreneurs in the internal market, especially small and medium-sized enterprises that have difficulty accessing traditional financial markets. The European Union has adopted measures to improve companies' access to financing, including the European Investment Fund, which provides guarantees and capital to innovative companies; the Small Business Act, which funds entrepreneurship support programs; and the European Financing Platform, which connects entrepreneurs with investors. Access to financing is fundamental to entrepreneurship and job creation in the internal market, and the EU must continue to work to improve the financing conditions of companies, especially small and medium-sized enterprises and innovative companies.

### Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises in the Internal Market

Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are the predominant business fabric in the European economy, representing more than 99 percent of all companies and employing more than 60 percent of the workforce. SMEs are fundamental to job creation, innovation, and economic growth in the internal market, and the European Union has adopted a broad regulatory framework to support SMEs, including the Small Business Act, which establishes principles for creating a more favorable business environment for SMEs. The Small Business Act establishes measures to simplify regulatory rules, reduce administrative costs, improve access to financing, and promote innovation in SMEs.

The European Union has adopted specific measures to support SMEs in the internal market, including the Late Payment in Commercial Transactions Directive, which establishes that companies must pay their suppliers within a maximum period of 60 days, ensuring that SMEs receive their payments on time and do not suffer liquidity problems from late payments by their customers. The European Union has also adopted measures to facilitate SME access to public contracts, establishing value thresholds below which public contracts are not subject to European public procurement rules, and promoting the subcontracting of SMEs in larger public contracts. These measures seek to ensure that SMEs can compete on equal terms with large companies in the internal market and in public contracts.

Innovation is fundamental to the competitiveness of SMEs in the internal market, and the European Union has adopted measures to promote innovation in SMEs, including the Innovation Support Programme, which funds innovation projects in SMEs; the European Network of Innovative Enterprises, which promotes cooperation among innovative SMEs; and the European Innovation Award, which recognizes the most innovative SMEs. Innovation in SMEs is fundamental to job creation, economic growth, and the competitiveness of the internal market, and the EU must continue to work to create an environment favorable to innovation in SMEs, including the simplification of regulatory rules, the improvement of access to financing, and the promotion of cooperation between SMEs and research centers.

The single market will continue to be an engine of growth, employment, and well-being for European citizens, and the European Union will continue to work to strengthen and modernize the internal market to address the challenges of the future and ensure that the benefits of the single market are distributed equitably among all citizens and regions of the European Union.
