
# Major Cities of Spain

## Chapter 1: Madrid - The Capital

### 1.1 History of Madrid

Madrid is the capital of Spain and one of the largest and most populous cities in Europe, with over 3.2 million inhabitants in the municipality and more than 6 million in the metropolitan area. Its history dates back to the Roman era, when there was a settlement called Matritense, but the modern city was founded in the 9th century by Emir Muhammad I of Cordoba, who built a fortress on the site where the Royal Palace now stands. The name Madrid comes from the Arabic Mayrit, meaning "place of abundant waters," a reference to the multiple streams and springs that bubbled up in the area. Madrid became the capital of Spain in 1561, when Philip II moved the court from Toledo to Madrid, a decision that radically changed the history of the city and the country. During the 17th and 18th centuries, Madrid experienced significant urban development, with the construction of palaces, churches, plazas, and promenades that turned the city into a top-tier European cultural and political center.

### 1.2 Monuments and Museums of Madrid

Madrid is home to some of the most important monuments and museums in Spain and the world. The Royal Palace is the largest royal residence in Western Europe, with over 3,400 rooms, although it is currently only used for official ceremonies. The Palace was built in the 18th century on the ruins of an ancient Arab Alcazar, and its interior features decoration of extraordinary beauty with marbles, frescoes, tapestries, and porcelain. The Puerta del Sol is kilometer zero of Spain's roads and the geographic center of the city, with the Bear and the Strawberry Tree, the statue that symbolizes Madrid. The Plaza Mayor is one of the most beautiful squares in Spain, built in the 17th century with arches, balconies, and frescoes by Juan de Villanueva. The Prado Museum is one of the finest art galleries in the world, with works by Velázquez, Goya, El Greco, Bosch, Titian, and Rubens, among others. The Reina Sofía Museum houses Picasso's Guernica, one of the most important works of 20th-century art. The Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum complements the museum offering with a collection spanning from Trecento painting to contemporary art.

### 1.3 Modern Madrid and Its Cultural Life

Madrid is a vibrant and cosmopolitan city with one of the best cultural offerings in Europe. The Malasaña neighborhood is an alternative area with vintage shops, trendy bars, and an underground music scene. La Latina is the neighborhood of bars and tapas, with the Plaza de la Cava Baja and its many taverns. Chueca is Madrid's LGBTQ+ neighborhood, with lively nightlife and varied gastronomy. Salamanca is the upscale neighborhood, with luxury shops, fine-dining restaurants, and art galleries. El Retiro, Madrid's largest park, is an oasis of tranquility in the heart of the city, with the Crystal Palace, the Great Lake, and the Paseo de la Argentina. The festivals of San Isidro (May 15), Madrid's patron saint, include street parties, concerts, bullfighting events, and the famous celebration at the Pradera de San Isidro. Madrid's gastronomy includes cocido madrileño (Madrid-style chickpea stew), callos a la madrileña (Madrid-style tripe), the squid sandwich, churros con chocolate (churros with chocolate), and huevos rotos (broken eggs).

### 1.4 Economy and Transportation of Madrid

Madrid is Spain's most important economic center, with a GDP per capita above the national average and a diversified economy that includes services (finance, telecommunications, professional services), industry (pharmaceutical, food, technology), and commerce. The Madrid Stock Exchange is one of the most important in Europe, and the headquarters of the Bank of Spain is located in the city. Madrid's public transportation system is one of the most comprehensive in Europe, with 13 Metro lines (302 kilometers of network), 10 Cercanías commuter rail lines (370 kilometers), and numerous bus routes connecting the capital with all towns in the community. Adolfo Suárez Madrid-Barajas Airport is the busiest in Spain and one of the most important in Europe, with connections worldwide. The AVE high-speed train connects Madrid with Spain's major cities (Barcelona, Seville, Valencia, Malaga, Zaragoza) in competitive travel times.

## Chapter 2: Barcelona - The Condal City

### 2.1 History of Barcelona

Barcelona is the second most populous city in Spain, with over 1.6 million inhabitants in the municipality and more than 5 million in the metropolitan area. Its history spans over 2,000 years, when the Romans founded the colony of Barcino in 15 BC on a previous Iberian settlement. Barcelona was the capital of the County of Barcelona and, subsequently, of the Crown of Aragon, a Mediterranean empire that stretched from Valencia to Greece and Sicily. During the Middle Ages, Barcelona was one of Europe's most important cities, with a powerful economy based on maritime trade, the textile industry, and banking. The city experienced a cultural renaissance in the 19th century with the Renaixença movement, which revived the Catalan language and culture, and with the 1888 Universal Exposition, which drove the city's urban modernization. In the 20th century, Barcelona lived through the Civil War (1936-1939), the Franco dictatorship (1939-1975), and the democratic transition, which restored Catalonia's political and cultural autonomy.

### 2.2 Modernist Architecture of Barcelona

Barcelona is world-renowned for its modernist architecture, an artistic and architectural movement that developed in Catalonia at the end of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th. Antoni Gaudí is the most well-known modernist architect, with works such as the Sagrada Familia (Spain's most visited temple, with over 4.5 million visitors annually), Park Güell (an urban park with organic forms and trencadís mosaics), Casa Milà (La Pedrera, a residential building with an undulating façade), Casa Vicens (his first major work), and the Crypt of the Colònia Güell. Other important modernist architects include Lluís Domènech i Montaner (Palace of Catalan Music, Hospital de la Santa Cruz y San Pablo) and Josep Puig i Cadafalch (Casa Amatller, Casa de les Punxes). Modernisme is a movement that combines architecture with decorative arts, sculpture, and painting, characterized by the use of organic forms, vivid colors, trencadís mosaics, and a strong connection to nature and Catalan tradition.

### 2.3 Contemporary Barcelona

Barcelona is a cosmopolitan and modern city with vibrant cultural life, a top-tier gastronomic scene, and a diversified economy. La Rambla is the city's most famous avenue, a 1.2-kilometer pedestrian walkway connecting the Plaza de Cataluña with the Old Port, lined with shops, restaurants, florists, and street performers. The Gothic Quarter is the historic center of the city, with narrow streets, medieval squares, and historic buildings such as Barcelona Cathedral and the Palau de la Generalitat. El Born is a trendy neighborhood with designer shops, art galleries, and fashionable restaurants. La Barceloneta is the coastal neighborhood, with La Barceloneta beach and seafood and fish restaurants. Montjuïc is a hill overlooking the city, home to the Montjuïc Castle, the National Art Museum of Catalonia, the Pueblo Español, and the Laribal Gardens. Camp Nou, FC Barcelona's stadium, is Europe's largest stadium, with a capacity of 99,354 spectators.

### 2.4 Gastronomy and Culture of Barcelona

Catalan cuisine is one of the most varied in Spain, with dishes such as escudella i carn d'olla (a meat and vegetable stew), calcot (a roasted onion eaten with romesco sauce), mar i muntanya (combined seafood and meat), pa amb tomàquet (bread with tomato and olive oil), crema catalana (a custard dessert), and Cava wines from the Penedés region. Barcelona has a vibrant cultural scene, with museums such as MACBA (Barcelona Museum of Contemporary Art), CCCB (Barcelona Centre of Contemporary Culture), the Picasso Museum, and the Joan Miró Foundation. Music festivals include Primavera Sound, Sónar, and the Grec Festival. Barcelona's festivals include La Mercè (the city's main festival in September), Sant Jordi (the day of books and roses in April), and the Castellers (human towers, declared Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity).

## Chapter 3: Valencia - The City of the Turia

### 3.1 History of Valencia

Valencia is the third most populous city in Spain, with over 800,000 inhabitants in the municipality and more than 1.5 million in the metropolitan area. Founded in 138 BC by the Romans as Valentia Edetanorum, the city was conquered by the Visigoths in the 5th century, by the Arabs in the 8th century, and by El Cid Campeador in the 11th century, before being definitively conquered by James I of Aragon in 1238. During the Middle Ages, Valencia was one of the most important cities in the Crown of Aragon, with a powerful economy based on silk, maritime trade, and irrigated agriculture. The city was the birthplace of artists such as El Micalet, the sculptor of the famous cathedral bell tower, and writers such as Ausiàs March and Joanot Martorell. In the 19th century, Valencia experienced significant industrial development, with the silk, furniture, and food industries. In the 20th century, the city lived through the Civil War, the Franco dictatorship, and the democratic transition, which restored Valencia's political and cultural autonomy.

### 3.2 Monuments of Valencia

Valencia is home to monuments of extraordinary historical and artistic importance. Valencia Cathedral, built between the 13th and 15th centuries, combines Gothic, Romanesque, and Baroque styles, and houses the Santo Cáliz (Holy Grail), the cup that according to tradition was used by Jesus at the Last Supper. La Lonja de la Seda (Silk Exchange), declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996, is a 15th-century Gothic building that served as the city's Stock Exchange, with a Hall of Columns 17 meters high and an Orange Tree Patio. The Miguelete (Micalet) is the cathedral bell tower, a 63-meter Gothic tower that is the city's icon. The Plaza de la Virgen is one of Spain's most beautiful squares, with the Basilica of the Virgen de los Desamparados, the Cathedral, and the Palau de la Generalitat. The Santos Inocentes is a 15th-century Gothic church with a Valencian ceramic altarpiece. The City of Arts and Sciences, designed by Santiago Calatrava, is a futuristic architectural cultural and scientific complex that includes the Oceanographic (Europe's largest aquarium), the Hemisferic (an IMAX cinema), the Prince Felipe Science Museum, and the Palau de las Artes Reina Sofía (a performing arts center).

### 3.3 Fallas of Valencia

The Fallas of Valencia are the city's most famous festival, declared Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO in 2016. The Fallas are celebrated from March 15 to 19, in honor of San José, and consist of the construction and burning of ninots (papier-mâché figures up to 20 meters tall) that represent social and political satire. The ninots are built by fallero artists throughout the year, and each neighborhood in Valencia has its own fallera commission that constructs its own ninot. The burning of the fallas, on the night of March 19, is a fireworks spectacle that lights up the city and attracts millions of visitors. The Fallas also include the Ofrenda a la Virgen de los Desamparados (a procession with thousands of Valencians dressed in traditional costumes), the Cavalcada del Foc (a fireworks parade), and the Mascarota (a comedy show). The Fallas are a festival that unites the entire city and represents Valencia's cultural identity.

### 3.4 Valencian Gastronomy

Valencian cuisine is one of the most varied and recognized in Spain, with dishes that reflect the region's agriculture and Mediterranean influence. Paella is the most famous dish, a rice dish with chicken, rabbit, green beans, saffron, and rosemary cooked in a wide, shallow pan. Fideuà is a dish similar to paella but with noodles instead of rice, and is especially popular on the coast. Arroz al horno is a rice dish baked in the oven with chickpeas, chorizo, and tomato. All i pebre is an eel stew with garlic, paprika, and potatoes. Horchata de chufa is a sweet drink made from chufas (a tuber) that is especially popular in summer. The wines of the D.O. Valencia and Utiel-Requena are recognized for their quality. Valencian desserts include fartón (a fried sweet eaten with horchata), coca de llanda (a fruit cake), and bunyols de vent (cream-filled pastries).

## Chapter 4: Seville - The City of the Alcazar

### 4.1 History of Seville

Seville is the capital of Andalusia and the fourth most populous city in Spain, with over 690,000 inhabitants in the municipality and more than 1.5 million in the metropolitan area. Founded by the Tartessians as Himba in the 8th century BC, the city was later colonized by the Phoenicians, the Romans (who called it Hispalis), the Visigoths, and the Arabs (who called it Isbiliyya). The Romans turned Seville into an important city, with an amphitheater, a theater, aqueducts, and temples. During the Muslim period (8th-13th centuries), Seville was one of the most important cities of Al-Andalus, with monumental architecture including the Mezquita de la Giralda, the Alcázar, and the city walls. The Reconquista of Seville by Ferdinand III in 1248 marked the beginning of a new historical era, with the construction of the Cathedral and the Giralda, and the development of trade with the Americas. Seville was the center of trade with the Indies during the 16th and 17th centuries, and the departure point for the conquistadors who conquered America.

### 4.2 Monuments of Seville

Seville is home to some of Spain's most important monuments. Seville Cathedral is the largest Gothic cathedral in the world, measuring 116 meters in length and 76 meters in width, and houses the tomb of Christopher Columbus, the Giralda (a 104-meter tower that was the minaret of the former mosque), and the Retablo Mayor, a masterpiece of Gothic art. The Alcázar of Seville is a unique palatial fortress in Spain, built by the Christian kings on the ruins of a Muslim palace, combining Gothic, Renaissance, and Mudéjar styles. The Archivo de Indias, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site, houses the documentation of the discovery and colonization of America, with over 80 million pages. The Plaza de España, built for the 1929 Ibero-American Exposition, is one of Spain's most beautiful squares, with a diameter of 174 meters, canals, bridges, and benches dedicated to the Spanish provinces. The Santa Cruz neighborhood is Seville's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. The Metropol Parasol (Las Setas) is a 26-meter wooden structure that dominates the Plaza de la Encarnación.

### 4.3 Flamenco and Holy Week in Seville

Seville is the capital of flamenco, an art form that combines cante (singing), baile (dance), and toque (guitar) and was born in the city's Roma neighborhoods in the 19th century. Seville's tablaos flamencos, such as La Casa del Flamenco, Los Gallos, and El Arenal, offer pure flamenco shows that attract visitors from around the world. Seville's Holy Week is one of Spain's most important religious celebrations, with processions of pasos (religious images carried by costaleros) that traverse the city streets throughout the week. Seville's Feria de Abril is Spain's most important fair, a week-long celebration where Sevillanos wear traditional costumes, eat tapas, drink rebujito, and dance sevillanas. The Feria de Abril includes casetas (private tents), portadas (entrance decorations), alumbrado (lighting), and flamenco music.

### 4.4 Sevillian Gastronomy

Sevillian cuisine is one of the most varied in Andalusia, with dishes that reflect the influence of Andalusian, Castilian, and American cooking. Tapas are the most common way to eat in Seville, with dishes such as spinach with chickpeas, salmorejo, Russian salad, fried small fish, ham croquettes, and snails. Iberian acorn-fed ham (jamón ibérico de bellota) is one of the most prized products, and Seville is home to some of Spain's best tapas bars. The wines of the D.O. Jerez are Andalusia's most important wines, including fino, manzanilla, amontillado, oloroso, and sweet sherry. Gazpacho andaluz is Spain's most famous cold soup, made with tomato, cucumber, pepper, garlic, olive oil, and vinegar. Fried small fish (pescaito frito) is a traditional dish from the Andalusian coast, with anchovies, shrimp, octopus, and squid. Sevillian desserts include pestiños (fried dough with honey), torrijas (bread with honey), and bienmesabe (almond and egg yolk cake).

## Chapter 5: Bilbao - The City of Euskal Herria

### 5.1 History of Bilbao

Bilbao is the most populous city in the Basque Country, with over 350,000 inhabitants in the municipality and more than 1 million in the metropolitan area. Founded in 1300 by Diego López de Haro, Lord of Biscay, Bilbao developed as a commercial port on the Bilbao estuary, connecting the interior of Biscay with the Cantabrian Sea. During the 15th and 16th centuries, Bilbao was an important commercial center, with a steel industry and significant port activity. The Industrial Revolution of the 19th century turned Bilbao into one of Spain's most industrialized cities, with shipyards, steelworks, and chemical and textile industries. The Spanish Civil War (1936-1939) deeply affected Bilbao, which was bombed by Franco's aviation and suffered significant destruction. In the following decades, Bilbao experienced a significant urban transformation, with the construction of new infrastructure, the renewal of neighborhoods, and the creation of cultural and commercial spaces.

### 5.2 The Guggenheim and Bilbao's Transformation

The Guggenheim Museum Bilbao, inaugurated in 1997, is a masterpiece of contemporary architecture, designed by Frank Gehry with titanium, glass, and limestone. The museum has transformed Bilbao's image and turned the city into a top-tier tourist destination, with millions of annual visitors. The building, with its organic forms and titanium façade that changes color with the sunlight, is one of the most recognizable in the world. The Guggenheim houses a top-tier contemporary art collection, with works by artists such as Richard Serra, Jeff Koons, Louise Bourgeois, and Eduardo Chillida. The Guggenheim Effect has been studied by urban planners worldwide as an example of how a cultural project can transform a city. Other cultural spaces in Bilbao include the Bilbao Fine Arts Museum, the Azkuna Zentroa (a cultural center designed by Philippe Starck in a former public bathhouse), and the Alhóndiga.

### 5.3 Neighborhoods and Life in Bilbao

Bilbao is a city with great neighborhood diversity, each with its own character and personality. Casco Viejo (Old Town) is the city's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with pintxo bars, and traditional shops. Abando is the upscale neighborhood, with fashion shops, fine-dining restaurants, and art galleries. Deusto is a university neighborhood with a growing cultural and leisure offering. Santutxu is a residential neighborhood with views of the city and vibrant neighborhood life. Los Pueblos is an area of chalets and single-family houses with views of the estuary. Bilbao's nightlife is lively, with numerous bars, restaurants, and nightclubs offering a variety of leisure options. Pintxos are the most common way to eat in Bilbao, with specialized bars offering a variety of cold and hot pintxos. Cider is the traditional drink, served by pouring it from a height in cider houses (sidrerías).

### 5.4 Basque Gastronomy

Basque cuisine is one of the most recognized in the world, with a gastronomic tradition that includes pintxos (Basque tapas), cider, bacalao al pil-pil (pil-pil cod), marmitako (tuna stew), kokotxa (cod cheek stew), and desserts such as tarta de queso (cheesecake), Txantxangorros, and intxaurrak. The Basque Country has the highest concentration of Michelin stars per square meter in the world, with restaurants such as Arzak, Mugaritz, Azurmendi, and Martín Berasategui. The pintxo bars of Bilbao, such as those along Calle Licenciado Poza and the Plaza Nueva, offer a variety of pintxos ranging from traditional (cod, tortilla, ham) to innovative (foie gras, shrimp, cheese). Cider is the traditional drink of the Basque Country, produced from local apples, and is served in cider houses where it is poured from a height of one meter to oxygenate it. The wines of the D.O. Txakoli are fresh, fruity white wines that are especially popular on the coast.

## Chapter 6: Malaga - The Costa del Sol

### 6.1 History of Malaga

Malaga is a city located on the Costa del Sol, with over 570,000 inhabitants in the municipality and one of Spain's most visited cities. Founded by the Phoenicians in the 8th century BC as Malaka, the city was later colonized by the Romans, who turned it into a commercial center and important port. During the Muslim period (8th-15th centuries), Malaga was one of the most important cities of Al-Andalus, with monumental architecture including the Alcazaba, the Gibralfaro, and the Cathedral. The Reconquista of Malaga by the Catholic Monarchs in 1487 marked the beginning of a new historical era, with the construction of the Cathedral and the development of trade with the Americas. Malaga was an important commercial center during the 16th and 17th centuries, and a departure point for the conquest of America. In the 19th century, Malaga experienced significant industrial development, with the textile, mining, and agricultural industries. In the 20th century, the city lived through the Civil War, the Franco dictatorship, and the democratic transition, which restored Malaga's political and cultural autonomy.

### 6.2 Museums and Monuments of Malaga

Malaga is home to museums and monuments of extraordinary historical and artistic importance. The Museo Picasso Malaga, located in the Palacio de los Conde de Buenavista, houses a collection of over 200 works by Pablo Picasso, who was born in Malaga in 1881. The Museo Carmen Thyssen Malaga, located in the Palacio de Villalón, houses a collection of 19th-century Spanish painting. The Centro de Arte Contemporáneo de Malaga (CAC Malaga) is a contemporary art museum located in a former bullring. The Alcazaba is an 11th-century palatial fortress, declared a Property of Cultural Interest, that houses gardens, courtyards, and museums. The Gibralfaro is a 14th-century castle that overlooks the city and houses the City Museum. Malaga Cathedral, built between the 16th and 18th centuries, is a Renaissance and Baroque building with a three-part façade and an 87-meter tower. The Roman Theater of Malaga, located on the hill of Gibralfaro, is a 1st-century BC theater that houses a museum and cultural space.

### 6.3 Costa del Sol and the Beach

Malaga is the center of the Costa del Sol, one of Spain's most important coastal areas, with over 100 kilometers of beaches stretching from Nerja to Estepona. The Costa del Sol is a top-tier tourist destination, with sandy beaches, cliffs, coves, and picturesque villages such as Nerja, Frigiliana, Torremolinos, Benalmádena, Fuengirola, and Marbella. Marbella is a luxury city, with five-star hotels, fine-dining restaurants, golf courses, and lively nightlife. Puerto Banús is a luxury marina with yachts, designer shops, and restaurants. The Costa del Sol has a mild Mediterranean climate, with over 300 days of sunshine per year, making it an attractive destination year-round. Tourism is the economic engine of the region, with millions of annual visitors seeking sun, beach, culture, and gastronomy.

### 6.4 Malagan Gastronomy

Malagan cuisine is one of the most varied in Andalusia, with dishes that reflect the influence of Andalusian, Castilian, and American cooking. The espeto de sardinas (sardine skewer) is the most typical dish, made with fresh sardines roasted on a wooden stick, and is especially popular at beachside chiringuitos. Fried small fish (pescaito frito) is a traditional dish from the Malagan coast, with anchovies, shrimp, octopus, and squid. Malagan gazpacho is a cold soup made with tomato, cucumber, pepper, garlic, olive oil, and vinegar, and is especially popular in summer. Malaga wine is a sweet wine produced in the region, with designations such as Malaga, Sierras de Malaga, and Pasas de Malaga. The wines of the D.O. Sierras de Malaga include reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as garnacha, monastrell, and Pedro Ximénez. Malagan desserts include borrachuelos (fried dough with honey), pestiños (fried dough with honey), and bienmesabe (almond and egg yolk cake).

## Chapter 7: Granada - The City of the Alhambra

### 7.1 History of Granada

Granada is a city located at the foot of the Sierra Nevada, with over 230,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded in the 8th century BC by the Phoenicians, the city was later colonized by the Romans (who called it Illiberis), the Visigoths, and the Arabs. Granada became the capital of the Kingdom of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom on the Iberian Peninsula, in the 13th century, and was conquered by the Catholic Monarchs in 1492, an event that marked the end of the Reconquista and the beginning of Spanish expansion toward the Americas. The Alhambra, the most important Muslim palace in Spain, was built during the 13th and 14th centuries by the Nasrid sultans, and is considered one of the masterpieces of Islamic architecture and art. Granada was an important cultural and artistic center during the Middle Ages, with a university founded in 1531 and a vibrant cultural life including music, poetry, and philosophy.

### 7.2 The Alhambra and the Generalife

The Alhambra is Spain's most visited monument, with over 2.7 million visitors annually, and is considered one of the masterpieces of Islamic architecture and art. The palace includes the Plaza de los Arrayanes (Court of the Myrtles), the Patio de los Leones (Court of the Lions), the Sala de los Abencerrajes, the Sala de las Dos Hermanas, and the Cuarto Dorado (Golden Room), all with decoration of stucco, mosaics, and woodwork of extraordinary beauty. The Generalife, the palaces and gardens of the Nasrid sultans, is a complex of extraordinary beauty spread over a hill with views of the city of Granada and the Sierra Nevada. The gardens of the Generalife include the Patio de la Acequia (Court of the Water Channel), the Escalera del Agua (Water Stairway), and the Jardines de los Naranjos (Orange Tree Gardens). The Alcazaba is the fortress of the Alhambra, with the Plaza de Armas, the Torres de Comares, and the Torre de la Vela. The Palace of Charles V is a 16th-century Renaissance building that houses the Alhambra Museum and the Granada Fine Arts Museum.

### 7.3 Neighborhoods of Granada

Granada is a city with great neighborhood diversity, each with its own character and personality. The Albaycín is Granada's historic quarter, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers, and spectacular views of the Alhambra. El Sacromonte is the cave neighborhood, where Granada's Roma community lives, and is known for its flamenco shows. Realejo is a neighborhood with a history dating back to the Muslim period, with the Puerta de Elvira and the Plaza de la Trinidad. Plaza Nueva is Granada's main square, with the City Hall, the Palacio de la Madraza, and the Carrera del Darro. Calle Navas is Granada's commercial street, with shops, restaurants, and bars. The Center (Plaza Bib-Rambla) is the city center, with the Cathedral, the Royal Chapel, and the Monastery of San Jerónimo.

### 7.4 Granadan Gastronomy

Granadan cuisine is one of the most varied in Andalusia, with dishes that reflect the influence of Andalusian, Castilian, and American cooking. Tapas are the most common way to eat in Granada, and the particularity is that they are served free with each drink. Granadan tapas include habas con jamón (beans with ham), tortillas a la montillana, bread with tomato, Iberian ham, ham croquettes, and berenjenas con miel (eggplant with honey). Granadan gazpacho is a cold soup made with tomato, cucumber, pepper, garlic, olive oil, and vinegar, and is especially popular in summer. The pionono is a typical Granadan dessert, a small cake covered in sugar and cinnamon. The wines of the D.O. Granada are reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as garnacha, tempranilla, and macabeo. Granadan desserts include roscos de vino (wine-flavored rings), flores de Alhambra (Alhambra flowers), and bienmesabe.

## Chapter 8: Zaragoza - The City of the Ebro

### 8.1 History of Zaragoza

Zaragoza is the capital of Aragon and the fifth most populous city in Spain, with over 680,000 inhabitants in the municipality and more than 1 million in the metropolitan area. Founded in 24 BC by the Romans as Caesaraugusta, the city was an important commercial and administrative center of Roman Hispania. During the Visigothic period, Zaragoza was the seat of an important bishopric, and during the Muslim period (8th-11th centuries) it was the capital of a border marches. The Reconquista of Zaragoza by Alfonso I the Battler in 1118 marked the beginning of a new historical era, with the construction of the Cathedral of the Savior (La Seo) and the Basilica del Pilar. During the 12th and 16th centuries, Zaragoza was one of the most important cities in the Crown of Aragon, with a powerful economy based on commerce, the textile industry, and agriculture. In the 19th century, Zaragoza experienced the Carlist sieges (1808-1809), heroic episodes of the War of Independence against the French that turned the city into a symbol of national resistance.

### 8.2 Monuments of Zaragoza

Zaragoza is home to monuments of extraordinary historical and artistic importance. The Basilica del Pilar is Zaragoza's most important building and one of the most important in Spain, with interior decoration of frescoes by Francisco de Goya and a Virgin of the Pilar who is the patroness of the Hispanic world. The basilica's façade is one of the largest in Spain, with 108 meters in length and a dome 47 meters high. The Cathedral of the Savior (La Seo) is a building that combines Romanesque, Gothic, Baroque, and Mudéjar styles, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Palacio de la Aljafería is a unique palatial fortress in Spain, built in the 11th century by the Taifa kings of Zaragoza, combining Islamic, Gothic, Mudéjar, and Renaissance styles. The Puente de Piedra (Stone Bridge) is a 15th-century bridge that crosses the Ebro River and connects the city with the Magdalena neighborhood. The Roman Theater of Caesaraugusta, located on the Plaza del Pilar, is a 1st-century BC theater that houses a museum and cultural space.

### 8.3 Life in Zaragoza

Zaragoza is a vibrant city with a great offering of culture, leisure, and gastronomy. The Plaza del Pilar is one of Spain's largest squares, with 40,000 square meters, and houses the Basilica del Pilar, the Cathedral of the Savior, the Palacio de la Aljafería, and the City Hall. Calle Alfonso I is Zaragoza's commercial street, with shops, restaurants, and bars. The Magdalena neighborhood is a traditional area with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. The Ebro is the river that crosses the city, with riverside promenades, parks, and green spaces. Zaragoza's festivities include the Fiestas del Pilar (October 12), the city's most important celebration, with processions, street parties, concerts, and fireworks; Holy Week, with processions of pasos; and the Feria de San Jorge, a medieval fair held in April.

### 8.4 Aragonese Gastronomy

Aragonese cuisine is one of the most varied in Spain, with dishes that reflect the influence of Castilian, Catalan, and Aragonese cooking. Chilindrón is a lamb or chicken stew with peppers, tomato, and onion, and is Aragon's most representative dish. Ternasco is a roast lamb baked with potatoes, a traditional dish from the region. Migas are a dish of fried bread with garlic, pepper, and olive oil, especially popular during popular festivals. Frutas de Aragón is an almond, egg yolk, and sugar dessert especially popular at Christmas. The wines of the D.O. Cariñena, Calatayud, and Somontano are reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as garnacha, monastrell, and tempranilla. Zaragozan gastronomy also includes borraja con patatas (burdock and potato stew), ternasco de Aragón, and huevos a la aragonesa (fried eggs with ham and peppers).

## Chapter 9: Alicante and the Costa Blanca

### 9.1 History of Alicante

Alicante is a coastal city located in the south of the Valencian Community, with over 330,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Greeks as Akra Leuke in the 4th century BC, the city was later colonized by the Romans, the Visigoths, and the Arabs. During the Muslim period, Alicante was an important commercial port, and was conquered by James I of Aragon in the 13th century. The city experienced significant development in the 18th and 19th centuries, with the wine, oil, and citrus industries. In the 20th century, Alicante became an important tourist destination, with the Costa Blanca as its main attraction. The Castle of Santa Bárbara, located on the Benacantil hill, is a fortress that overlooks the city and houses a museum and cultural space. The Explanada de España is a 500-meter-long maritime promenade with a marble mosaic floor representing ocean waves, and is one of Spain's most beautiful walkways.

### 9.2 Costa Blanca

The Costa Blanca is one of Spain's most important coastal areas, with over 200 kilometers of beaches stretching from Dénia to Pilar de la Horadada. The Costa Blanca is a top-tier tourist destination, with sandy beaches, cliffs, coves, and picturesque villages such as Benidorm, Altea, Calpe, Denia, Javea, and Elche. Benidorm is a mass tourism city, with skyscrapers, beaches, and lively nightlife. Altea is a white village with a picturesque old town and a church with the most famous blue-tiled dome in Spain. Calpe is known for the Peñón de Ifach, a 332-meter limestone monolith that is the symbol of the Costa Blanca. Elche is known for its Palmeral, a palm grove of 200,000 palm trees declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Costa Blanca has a mild Mediterranean climate, with over 300 days of sunshine per year, making it an attractive destination year-round.

### 9.3 Alicantine Gastronomy

Alicantine cuisine is one of the most varied in the Valencian Community, with dishes that reflect the influence of Valencian, Mediterranean, and huerta (garden) cooking. Alicantine paella is a rice dish with chicken, rabbit, green beans, and saffron cooked in a wide, shallow pan. Arroz a banda is rice cooked in fish and seafood broth, served with alioli (a garlic and olive oil sauce). Arroz al horno is a rice dish baked in the oven with chickpeas, chorizo, and tomato. All i pebre is an eel stew with garlic, paprika, and potatoes. The wines of the D.O. Alicante include reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as monastrell, garnacha, and muscatel. Alicantine desserts include turrones (a nougat of almonds and honey especially popular at Christmas), panellets (an almond sweet eaten on All Saints' Day), and blosseres (fried dough with honey).

## Chapter 10: Cordoba - The City of the Mosques

### 10.1 History of Cordoba

Cordoba is a city located in the Guadalquivir valley, with over 320,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Romans as Corduba in the 2nd century BC, the city was an important administrative and cultural center of Roman Hispania. During the Visigothic period, Cordoba was the seat of an important bishopric, and during the Muslim period (8th-13th centuries) it was the capital of the Emirate of Cordoba and, subsequently, the Caliphate of Cordoba, becoming one of the world's most important cities, with a population of over 500,000. The Caliphate of Cordoba was a top-tier cultural and scientific center, with universities, libraries, hospitals, and a vibrant cultural life including philosophy, medicine, astronomy, and poetry. The Reconquista of Cordoba by Ferdinand III in 1236 marked the beginning of a new historical era, with the conversion of the mosque into a church and the construction of the Cathedral. During the 15th and 16th centuries, Cordoba was an important city, but subsequently experienced an economic and demographic decline.

### 10.2 The Mosque of Cordoba

The Mosque of Cordoba is one of the world's most important religious buildings, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. Built between the 8th and 10th centuries, the mosque covers 23,400 square meters, with 856 columns of marble, granite, and jasper, and an interior forest of columns that creates an impressive visual effect. The mihrab (prayer niche) is a masterpiece of Islamic art, with golden Byzantine mosaics. The Patio de los Naranjos (Court of the Orange Trees) is an ablution courtyard with orange trees and fountains. Cordoba Cathedral, located in the center of the mosque, is a Gothic and Renaissance cathedral built from the 13th century onward, with a gilded wooden main altarpiece and a sacristy with paintings by Valdés Leal.

### 10.3 Neighborhoods and Life in Cordoba

Cordoba is a city with great neighborhood diversity, each with its own character and personality. The Barrio de la Mezquita (Mosque Quarter) is the city's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. The Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos is a 14th-century palatial fortress that houses a museum and spectacular gardens. The Judería (Jewish Quarter) is the historic Jewish neighborhood, with the Synagogue (one of the few medieval synagogues preserved in Spain), the Jewish Museum, and the Patio de los Judíos (Court of the Jews). Santa Marina is a neighborhood with a Romanesque church and a square with orange trees. The Feria de Cordoba is the city's most important celebration, with casetas (fairground tents), carousels, flamenco music, and traditional costumes. The Patios de Cordoba is a celebration where residents open their courtyards to the public, decorated with flowers, plants, and fountains, and has been declared Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

### 10.4 Cordoban Gastronomy

Cordoban cuisine is one of the most varied in Andalusia, with dishes that reflect the influence of Andalusian, Castilian, and American cooking. Salmorejo is a cold soup made with tomato, bread, garlic, olive oil, and vinegar, served with cured ham and hard-boiled egg. Gazpacho andaluz is a cold soup made with tomato, cucumber, pepper, garlic, olive oil, and vinegar, and is especially popular in summer. Flamenquinas are a dish of breaded and fried pork, served with salad and french fries. Rabo de toro is an oxtail stew with vegetables and spices, a traditional dish from bullfighting culture. The wines of the D.O. Montilla-Moriles are reds, whites, and fortified wines (fino, amontillado, oloroso) from grapes such as Pedro Ximénez and muscatel. Cordoban desserts include pestiños (fried dough with honey), torrijas (bread with honey), and bienmesabe (almond and egg yolk cake).

## Chapter 11: Salamanca - The Golden City

### 11.1 History of Salamanca

Salamanca is a city located in western Spain, with over 150,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Vettones as Helmantica in the 6th century BC, the city was colonized by the Romans, who built a bridge over the Tormes and an amphitheater. During the Middle Ages, Salamanca was an important cultural and university center, with the University of Salamanca, one of Europe's oldest, founded in 1218 by Alfonso IX of León. The university was a center for the dissemination of culture and science, and attracted students from all over Europe and the Americas. Salamanca was a center for the discovery and conquest of the Americas, and several Spanish conquistadors such as Hernán Cortés, Francisco Pizarro, and Pedro de Valdivia studied at the university. During the 16th and 17th centuries, Salamanca experienced significant urban development, with the construction of palaces, churches, and plazas that turned the city into a top-tier cultural and artistic center.

### 11.2 Monuments of Salamanca

Salamanca is home to monuments of extraordinary historical and artistic importance. The University of Salamanca is one of Europe's oldest universities, with a Plateresque façade of extraordinary beauty, declared a National Monument. The façade, built in the 16th century, features decoration of human figures, animals, plants, and heraldic motifs. The New Cathedral, built between the 16th and 18th centuries, is a Baroque and Renaissance cathedral with a dome 93 meters high. The Old Cathedral, built between the 12th and 14th centuries, is a Romanesque and Gothic cathedral with an octagonal dome and a bell tower. The Plaza Mayor, built in the 18th century, is one of Spain's most beautiful squares, with arches, balconies, and frescoes by Fernando VII. The Convento de San Esteban is a 16th-century Dominican convent, with a Baroque church and a Renaissance cloister. The Colegio del Arzobispo is a 16th-century university college, with a Gothic façade and a Renaissance courtyard.

### 11.3 University and Cultural Life

Salamanca is a university city with vibrant cultural and student life. The University of Salamanca has over 30,000 students, including thousands of international students who come to study Spanish. The city hosts multiple cultural festivals, such as the International Festival of Ancient and Contemporary Music, the Salamanca Film Festival, and the Theater Festival. The Salamanca Art Center is a contemporary art museum located in a former convent. The University Library is one of Spain's most important, with medieval manuscripts and first editions of major works. Salamanca's festivals include Holy Week, with processions of pasos; the September Fair, a fair with amusement rides and bullfighting; and the Noche de San Juan (Night of Saint John), a celebration of fire and water that marks the beginning of summer.

### 11.4 Salamancan Gastronomy

Salamancan cuisine is one of the most varied in Castile and León, with dishes that reflect the influence of Castilian, Portuguese, and American cooking. Cocido marinero is a chickpea stew with vegetables and fish, and is Salamanca's most representative dish. Huevos rotos are a dish of fried eggs with Iberian ham and french fries, served as a tapa or main course. Torreznos are a dish of fried pork rinds served as a tapa. Chanfainas are a dish of fried pork blood with vegetables. The wines of the D.O. Ribera del Duero and the D.O. Rueda are reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as tempranilla, garnacha, and verdejo. Salamancan desserts include hornazos (a pastry with hard-boiled egg and ham), flores (fried dough with honey), and yemas de Santa Teresa (an egg yolk and sugar dessert).

## Chapter 12: Vitoria-Gasteiz and San Sebastian

### 12.1 Vitoria-Gasteiz

Vitoria-Gasteiz is the capital of the Basque Country, with over 250,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city was founded in 1181 by King Sancho VI of Navarre as Villafranca de Vitoria, and subsequently passed to Castile. The Casco Viejo (Old Town) of Vitoria is one of the best preserved in Spain, with narrow streets, arched squares, and historic buildings such as the Cathedral of Santa María (a 13th-century Gothic cathedral), the Palacio de la Diputación (a masterpiece of civil Gothic), and the Plaza de la Virgen Blanca. Vitoria-Gasteiz was named European Green Capital in 2012, an award recognizing its commitment to urban sustainability, waste management, public transportation, and green spaces. The city is home to multiple parks and green areas, such as the Parque de la Florida, the Parque de Sudamérica, and the Parque de Abandoibarra. Vitorian gastronomy includes pintxos (Basque tapas), cider, bacalao al pil-pil, marmitako, and kokotxa.

### 12.2 San Sebastian

San Sebastian (Donostia in Basque) is a coastal city located on the Bay of Biscay, with over 190,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city is known for its sandy beaches (La Concha, Zurriola, Ondarreta), its picturesque old town, and its top-tier gastronomy. La Concha is one of Spain's most beautiful beaches, with views of the Bay of La Concha and Santa Clara Island. Monte Igueldo, with views of the city and the coast, is a privileged observation point. The Parque de María Cristina is a historic park with fountains, statues, and gardens. San Sebastian is gastronomically renowned for its pintxos, its txakoli bars, and its fine-dining restaurants, including several with Michelin stars. The city hosts the San Sebastian International Film Festival, one of Europe's most important film festivals, held annually in September. San Sebastian's festivals include the Tamborrada (January 19), a celebration in which thousands of drummers parade through the city streets, and the Carnivals, which are among Spain's most important.

### 12.3 Detailed Basque Gastronomy

Basque cuisine is one of the most recognized in the world, with a gastronomic tradition that includes pintxos (Basque tapas), cider, bacalao al pil-pil (pil-pil cod), marmitako (tuna stew), kokotxa (cod cheek stew), besugos al horno (baked sea bream), capitones (fried conger eel), and desserts such as tarta de queso (cheesecake), Txantxangorros, and intxaurrak. The Basque Country has the highest concentration of Michelin stars per square meter in the world, with restaurants such as Arzak (three Michelin stars), Mugaritz (two Michelin stars), Azurmendi (three Michelin stars), and Martín Berasategui (three Michelin stars). The pintxo bars of San Sebastian, such as those along Calle 31 de Agosto and the Plaza de la Constitución, offer a variety of pintxos ranging from traditional (cod, tortilla, ham) to innovative (foie gras, shrimp, cheese). Cider is the traditional drink of the Basque Country, produced from local apples, and is served in cider houses where it is poured from a height of one meter to oxygenate it. The wines of the D.O. Txakoli are fresh, fruity white wines that are especially popular on the coast.

## Chapter 13: Las Palmas de Gran Canaria and Santa Cruz de Tenerife

### 13.1 Las Palmas de Gran Canaria

Las Palmas de Gran Canaria is the capital of Gran Canaria and the most populous city in the Canary Islands, with over 380,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city was founded in 1478 by Juan Rejón, conqueror of Gran Canaria, and became an important commercial and administrative center of the Canary Islands. The Vegueta neighborhood is the city's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers, and houses the Cathedral of Santa Ana (a Gothic and Renaissance cathedral), the Casa de Colón (a museum dedicated to Christopher Columbus), and the Museo Canario (a museum of Canarian history and archaeology). Las Canteras is a golden-sand beach in the city center, with views of Monte Santa María and the Atlantic Ocean. The city has a mild subtropical climate, with temperatures between 18 and 26 degrees Celsius year-round, making it an attractive destination year-round. Canarian gastronomy includes papas arrugadas con mojo (wrinkled potatoes with mojo sauce), gofio (a roasted cereal), sancocho (a fish and vegetable stew), bienmesabe (an almond dessert), and wines from the D.O. Las Islas Canarias.

### 13.2 Santa Cruz de Tenerife

Santa Cruz de Tenerife is the capital of Tenerife and the second most important city in the Canary Islands, with over 200,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city was founded in 1494 by Alonso Fernández de Lugo, conqueror of Tenerife, and became an important commercial and administrative center. The Auditorio de Tenerife, designed by Santiago Calatrava, is one of Spain's most important works of contemporary architecture, with a form reminiscent of a sail or an eye. The Plaza de España is the city's main square, with a monumental fountain and sea views. The Parque García Sanabria is a historic park with fountains, statues, and gardens. Tenerife is home to Teide (3,718 m), Spain's highest peak and the tallest volcano in the Atlantic Islands, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and Teide National Park, which protects the volcano and its surroundings. Santa Cruz's festivals include Carnival, one of the world's most important, with costumes, comparsas, and music.

### 13.3 La Gomera and El Hierro

La Gomera is the smallest island in the Canary archipelago, with an area of 369 square kilometers and a population of approximately 21,000 inhabitants. The island is known for its laurisilva forests (UNESCO World Heritage Site), its Silbo Gomero (a whistling system that allows communication across valleys, declared Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity), and its mountain landscapes with vertical cliffs. San Sebastian de La Gomera is the island's capital, with a picturesque old town and the Torre del Conde, a 15th-century fortress. El Hierro is the smallest island in the archipelago, with an area of 268 square kilometers and a population of approximately 11,000 inhabitants. El Hierro is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, with a dramatic volcanic landscape, cliffs, laurisilva forests, and significant biodiversity. The most important city is Valverde, the only Spanish provincial capital located at over 600 meters in altitude. The gastronomy of La Gomera and El Hierro includes potaje de berros (a watercress and chickpea stew), gofio (a roasted cereal), and mojo (a spicy sauce made with peppers, garlic, and vinegar).

### 13.4 Tourism in the Canary Islands

The Canary Islands are a world-class tourist destination, with over 14 million visitors annually. Tourism is the economic engine of the islands, generating 35 percent of GDP and 40 percent of employment. Tourists come primarily from Europe (Germany, United Kingdom, France, Scandinavia) and seek both sun-and-beach tourism and cultural and nature tourism. Tenerife and Gran Canaria are the most visited islands, with beaches such as Las Canteras (Gran Canaria) and the beaches of southern Tenerife. Fuerteventura and Lanzarote are known for their golden-sand beaches and desert landscapes. La Palma is known as the greenest island, with laurisilva forests and the volcanoes of the central ridge. La Gomera and El Hierro are less touristy islands, with more sustainable nature tourism. The Canary Islands face significant challenges such as urban pressure, mass tourism in summer, water scarcity, waste management, and the effects of climate change.

## Chapter 14: Toledo and Cuenca - Heritage Cities

### 14.1 Toledo - The City of Three Cultures

Toledo is the capital of Castilla-La Mancha, with over 85,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Romans as Toletum in the 3rd century BC, the city was an important administrative and cultural center of Roman Hispania. During the Visigothic period, Toledo was the capital of the Visigothic Kingdom, and during the Muslim period (8th-11th centuries) it was an important city with monumental architecture. The Reconquista of Toledo by Alfonso VI in 1085 marked the beginning of a new historical era, with the construction of the Cathedral and the conversion of the mosque into a church. Toledo was known as the City of Three Cultures, for the coexistence of Christians, Jews, and Muslims during the Middle Ages, a coexistence that enriched the city's culture, science, and art. The Old Town of Toledo was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986, and houses the Cathedral, the Synagogue of El Tránsito, the Mosque of Cristo de la Luz, and multiple palaces and stately homes.

### 14.2 Cuenca - The City of Hanging Houses

Cuenca is the capital of the province of Cuenca, with over 55,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Romans in the 2nd century BC, the city was an important administrative and cultural center of Roman Hispania. During the Visigothic period, Cuenca was the seat of an important bishopric, and during the Muslim period (8th-11th centuries) it was an important city with monumental architecture. The Reconquista of Cuenca by Alfonso VIII in 1177 marked the beginning of a new historical era, with the construction of the Cathedral and the creation of a walled city. The Old Town of Cuenca was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996, and houses the Casas Colgadas (houses built over the Huécar gorge), the Gothic Cathedral, the Spanish Museum of Abstract Art (located in a Renaissance palace), and the Puente de San Pablo (a stone bridge crossing the Huécar River). Cuenca's Holy Week is one of Spain's most important, with processions of pasos traversing the streets of the walled city.

### 14.3 Mudéjar Architecture of Aragon

The Mudéjar architecture of Aragon is a collection of buildings built by Muslim artisans who remained in Christian territory after the Reconquista. These buildings combine Islamic, Romanesque, Gothic, and Renaissance styles, and are unique in the world. The Mudéjar buildings of Aragon were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986, and include the Tower of El Salvador and the Tower of San Salvador in Teruel, the Tower of la Magdalena in Zaragoza, the Tower of la Malmuerta in Calatayud, and multiple churches and palaces throughout the region. Mudéjar architecture is characterized by the use of brick, horseshoe arches, polylobed arches, glazed ceramic decoration, and geometric reliefs. These buildings are witnesses to the cultural coexistence between Muslims and Christians during the Middle Ages, and to the influence of Islamic art on Spanish architecture.

### 14.4 Gastronomy of Castilla-La Mancha

The gastronomy of Castilla-La Mancha is one of the most varied in Spain, with dishes that reflect the influence of Castilian, Andalusian, and American cooking. Pisto manchego is a vegetable stew (eggplant, zucchini, peppers, tomato) with a fried egg on top, and is the region's most representative dish. Queso manchego is a cheese made from Manchega sheep's milk produced in the region, and is one of Spain's most famous cheeses. The saffron of La Mancha is a spice produced in the region and used in Spanish cooking. The wines of the D.O. Valdepeñas are reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as garnacha, tempranilla, and monastrell. Manchegan desserts include bizcochos de Almogaver (an almond cake), conservas de frutas (candied fruit dessert), and hojaldres rellenos de crema (cream-filled puff pastry).

## Chapter 15: Santander, Oviedo, and León

### 15.1 Santander

Santander is the capital of Cantabria, with over 175,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded in the 1st century BC as Portus Victoriae by the Romans, the city was an important commercial center during the Middle Ages, especially in the wool trade with Flanders and England. In the 19th century, Santander became an important commercial and banking center, with the creation of the Banco de Santander (founded in 1857) and the construction of the Palacio de la Magdalena, a royal summer residence. The Palacio de la Magdalena is a 20th-century neoclassical palace that houses a museum and cultural space, and is located on the Peninsula of la Magdalena, with views of the Cantabrian Sea. The Playa del Sardinero is one of Spain's most important beaches, with golden sand and views of the Cantabrian Sea. Santander Cathedral is a Gothic and neoclassical cathedral that houses the Capilla del Sagrario and the Capilla de los Castros. Santander's Holy Week is one of Cantabria's most important, with processions of pasos traversing the city streets.

### 15.2 Oviedo

Oviedo is the capital of Asturias, with over 220,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded in the 8th century by King Fruela I, the city was the capital of the Kingdom of Asturias, a Christian kingdom that survived the Muslim invasion of the Iberian Peninsula and became the nucleus of the Reconquista. The Historic Center of Oviedo was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998, and houses Santa María del Naranco (a 9th-century pre-Romanesque church), San Miguel de Lillo (a 9th-century pre-Romanesque church), and the Cathedral of San Salvador (a 13th-century Gothic cathedral). The Oviedo University Campus is one of Spain's most important, with multiple faculties, laboratories, and research centers. Oviedo's Holy Week is one of Asturias' most important, with processions of pasos traversing the city streets. Asturian festivals include the Bajada de la Cruz de mayo, the Noche de San Juan, and the celebration of the Principality of Asturias on September 8.

### 15.3 León

León is the capital of the province of León, with over 125,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Romans as Legio VII Gemina in the 1st century AD, the city was an important administrative and cultural center of Roman Hispania. During the Middle Ages, León was the capital of the Kingdom of León, one of the Christian kingdoms that led the Reconquista against the Muslims. León Cathedral is one of Spain's most important Gothic cathedrals, measuring 116 meters in length and 76 meters in width, and houses Spain's largest stained-glass windows (over 1,800 square meters of stained glass). The Basilica of San Isidoro is a 12th-century Romanesque church that houses the "Sistine Chapel of Romanesque art," with 12th-century frescoes depicting scenes from the life of Christ and the Virgin. León's Holy Week is one of Spain's most important, with processions of pasos traversing the city streets. The Camino de Santiago passes through León, and the city houses multiple hostels, churches, and monuments related to the Way.

### 15.4 Gastronomy of Northern Spain

The gastronomy of northern Spain is one of the most varied and recognized in the world, with dishes that reflect the influence of Cantabrian, Basque, Asturian, and Galician cooking. Cocido montañés is a chickpea stew with vegetables and meat, and is Cantabria's most representative dish. Fabada asturiana is a stew of fabes (white beans) with chorizo, morcilla (blood sausage), and bacon, and is Asturias' most representative dish. Cocido lebaniego is a chickpea stew with vegetables and meat, and is León's most representative dish. Northern cheeses are internationally recognized, including Queso de Burgos, Queso de Valdeón, Queso de Afuega el Pitu, Queso de Gamonéu, and Queso de Cabrales. Northern wines include those from the D.O. Ribera del Duero, D.O. Rueda, D.O. Bierzo, D.O. Valdeorras, and D.O. Ribeira Sacra. ## Chapter 16: Pamplona, Burgos, and Segovia

### 16.1 Pamplona

Pamplona is the capital of Navarre, with over 200,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The festivals also include street parties, concerts, fireworks, and religious events. The Camino de Santiago passes through Pamplona, and the city houses multiple hostels, churches, and monuments related to the Way.

Burgos is the capital of the province of Burgos, with over 175,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by Count Diego Rodríguez Porcelos in the 9th century, the city was an important administrative and cultural center of the Kingdom of Castile. Burgos Cathedral is one of Spain's most important Gothic cathedrals, measuring 116 meters in length and 76 meters in width, and houses the Capilla del Condestable, the Retablo Mayor, and the tomb of El Cid Campeador. The Monasterio de las Huelgas is a 12th-century Cistercian monastery that houses a collection of historical artifacts, including the Virgen de las Huelgas and the Códice de las Huelgas. Burgos' festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Pedro, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15.

### 16.3 Segovia

Segovia is a city located in the Sierra de Guadarrama, with over 55,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Romans in the 1st century BC, the city was an important administrative and cultural center of Roman Hispania. The Alcázar of Segovia is a palatial fortress that served as the residence of the Castilian kings, and according to legend inspired Walt Disney for the design of the Cinderella Castle. Segovia Cathedral is a 16th-century Gothic cathedral, with a three-part façade and an 88-meter bell tower. The Barrio de la Judería (Jewish Quarter) is the historic Jewish neighborhood, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. Segovia's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on November 25.

### 16.4 Gastronomy of Castile and León

The gastronomy of Castile and León is one of the most varied in Spain, with dishes that reflect the influence of Castilian, Portuguese, and American cooking. Cochinillo asado is a roasted suckling pig, and is Segovia's most representative dish. Lechazo asado is a roasted suckling lamb, and is Burgos' most representative dish. Morcilla de Burgos is a blood sausage with rice, onion, and spices, a traditional dish from Burgos. Jamón de Jabugo is an Iberian acorn-fed ham produced in Jabugo (Huelva), but eaten throughout Castile and León. The wines of the D.O. Ribera del Duero are reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as tempranilla, garnacha, and monastrell. The suckling pig is the most famous dish of Segovian gastronomy, served with vegetables and potatoes.

## Chapter 17: Santiago de Compostela, A Coruña, and Vigo

### 17.1 Santiago de Compostela

Santiago de Compostela is the capital of Galicia and the final destination of the Camino de Santiago, one of the most important pilgrimage routes in the Christian world. The city has over 95,000 inhabitants in the municipality, and houses the Cathedral of Santiago, one of Spain's most important Romanesque cathedrals, which houses the silver altar of the Virgen del Pilar and the tomb of the Apostle James. The cathedral's façade, the Puerta de la Gloria (Gate of Glory), is a masterpiece of European Romanesque art, with sculptures depicting the Last Judgment, the Adoration of the Magi, and the life of Christ. The Old Town of Santiago was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985, and houses the Plaza del Obradoiro, the Hostal de los Reyes Católicos, the Colegio de San Jerónimo, and the Parque de la Alameda. The Camino de Santiago is a trail of over 800 kilometers that crosses Spain from north to south, and attracts thousands of pilgrims from around the world each year. Pilgrims arrive in Santiago exhausted but happy, and tradition holds that they should touch the statue of the Apostle James at the high altar.

### 17.2 A Coruña

A Coruña is a coastal city located in northwestern Spain, with over 250,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city is known for the Torre de Hércules, a 1st-century BC Roman tower that is the oldest lighthouse in Spain and Europe, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009. The Torre de Hércules is 55 meters tall and has been used as a lighthouse for over 2,000 years. The city has a 13-kilometer maritime promenade, the longest in Spain, that circles the peninsula and offers spectacular Atlantic views. The Ciudad Vieja (Old Town) is the city's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. The Castelo de San Antón is a 16th-century fortress that houses an archaeological museum. The Playa de Orzán is one of Spain's most important urban beaches, with golden sand and views of the Torre de Hércules. A Coruña's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15.

### 17.3 Vigo

Vigo is Galicia's most populous city, with over 295,000 inhabitants in the municipality, and an important fishing and commercial port. The city was founded in the 1st century BC as Vicus, and was an important commercial center during the Middle Ages. The Casco Viejo (Old Town) is the city's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. The Playa de Samil is one of Spain's most important urban beaches, with golden sand and views of the Vigo Estuary. The Monte del Castro is a historic park with views of the city and the port. Vigo is known for its gastronomy, especially its seafood, mussels, and sardines. Vigo's Holy Week is one of Galicia's most important, with processions of pasos traversing the city streets. Vigo's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15. The Port of Vigo is Spain's most important fishing port, with a fleet of trawlers fishing in the Atlantic.

### 17.4 Detailed Galician Gastronomy

Galician cuisine is one of Spain's richest and most varied, with dishes that reflect the abundance of seafood, fish, and interior products. Pulpo a la gallega (Galician-style octopus), prepared with potatoes, paprika, and olive oil, is one of the most representative dishes. Empanadas gallegas (Galician pies), filled with tuna, sardines, cod, or meat, are a traditional dish eaten at any time of year. Caldo gallego (Galician broth), a vegetable stew with cabbage, turnip greens, turnips, and bacon, is the most traditional dish of Galician cooking. Vieiras a la gallega (Galician-style scallops), prepared with tomato and onion sauce, are a seafood dish especially popular during the Camino de Santiago. Sopa de marisco (seafood soup), prepared with fresh seafood and fish, is a hearty dish that reflects the richness of the Galician sea. Percebes (goose barnacles), crustaceans attached to coastal rocks, are a delicacy eaten boiled with sea salt. Galician wines, especially Albariño from the D.O. Rías Baixas, Treixadura from the Ribeiro, and Mencía from the Ribeira Sacra, are internationally recognized for their quality.

## Chapter 18: Palma de Mallorca and Ibiza

### 18.1 Palma de Mallorca

Palma de Mallorca is the capital of the Balearic Islands, with over 415,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city was founded by the Romans in the 1st century BC as Palma, and was an important commercial center during the Middle Ages. The Cathedral of Palma, known as La Seu, is one of Spain's most important Gothic cathedrals, with the largest rose window in Catalan Gothic (12 meters in diameter) and a 66-meter-tall façade. The Cathedral was built between the 13th and 16th centuries on the ruins of a mosque, and its interior features decoration of extraordinary beauty with marbles, frescoes, and stained glass. The Palacio Real de La Almudaina is a 13th-century palatial fortress that was the residence of the Kings of Mallorca, and houses a collection of historical artifacts. The Plaza Mayor is a 60-meter-long pedestrian square, with arches, balconies, and restaurants serving tapas and drinks. The Barrio de la Seu (Seu Quarter) is the city's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. Palma's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15.

### 18.2 Ibiza and Formentera

Ibiza is an island located in the western Mediterranean, with an area of 572 square kilometers and a population of approximately 150,000 inhabitants. Ibiza is internationally known for its nightlife and electronic music scene, with nightclubs such as Pacha, Amnesia, Privilege, and Ushuaïa. Dalt Vila, Ibiza's walled city, was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999, and houses the Cathedral of Ibiza, the Castle, and the Plaza de España. Ibiza's beaches include Playa de Bossa, Playa de Ses Salines, Playa de Cala Conta, and Playa de Cala d'Hort. Formentera is an island located south of Ibiza, with an area of 82 square kilometers and a population of approximately 12,000 inhabitants. Formentera is known for its white-sand beaches and turquoise waters, especially Playa de Ses Illetes and Playa de Migjorn. The island has a relaxed, bohemian atmosphere with a strong hippy tradition. Ibizan gastronomy includes bullit de peix (a fish stew), sofit (a vegetable stew with bread), hierbas ibicencas (an herbal liqueur), and wines from the D.O. Ibiza.

### 18.3 Tourism in the Balearics

The Balearic Islands are a top-tier tourist destination, with over 13 million visitors annually. Tourism is the economic engine of the islands, generating 35 percent of GDP and 40 percent of employment. Mallorca is the most visited island, with beaches such as Playa de Palma, Playa de Muro, and Cala Mondragó. Ibiza is internationally known for its nightlife and electronic music scene, attracting tourists seeking partying and leisure. Menorca is known for its virgin beaches, its talayotic megalithic structures (declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998), and its Mahón cheese production. Formentera is known for its white-sand beaches and turquoise waters, and its relaxed, bohemian atmosphere. The Balearic Islands face significant challenges such as urban pressure, mass tourism in summer, water scarcity, waste management, and the effects of climate change. Sustainable tourism is a priority for the Balearic government, which seeks to minimize environmental impacts and maximize social and economic benefits.

### 18.4 Balearic Gastronomy

Balearic cuisine is one of the most varied in Spain, with dishes that reflect the influence of Catalan, Mediterranean, and huerta (garden) cooking. Ensaimada is a typical Mallorcan dessert, a spiral-shaped pastry covered in powdered sugar, especially popular at Christmas. Sobrasada is a cured pork sausage produced in Mallorca, eaten in sandwiches, with pasta, or with bread. Caldereta de langosta is a lobster stew with tomato, onion, and wine, a typical dish from Ibiza and Formentera. Tumbet is a vegetable stew (eggplant, zucchini, peppers) with tomato, a typical Mallorcan dish. Xuixó is a cream-filled sweet, especially popular at Christmas. The wines of the D.O. Binissalem and D.O. Pla i Llevant are reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as manto negro, callet, and prensal.

## Chapter 19: Mérida, Cáceres, and Badajoz - Extremadura

### 19.1 Mérida

Mérida is the capital of Extremadura, with over 58,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Romans in 25 BC as Emerita Augusta, the city was the capital of the Roman province of Lusitania and one of the most important cities in Roman Hispania. The Roman Theater of Mérida is one of the best-preserved Roman theaters in the world, with a capacity for 6,000 spectators, and hosts the annual Mérida International Classical Theater Festival, one of Spain's most important theater festivals. The Roman Amphitheater of Mérida is a 1st-century BC amphitheater that houses a museum and cultural space. The Roman Bridge over the Guadiana is a 1st-century BC bridge with 82 arches and 792 meters in length, the longest Roman bridge in Spain. The Roman Circus of Mérida is a 1st-century BC circus that houses a museum and cultural space. The Casa del Mitreo is a 1st-century BC Roman villa with mosaics of extraordinary beauty. The Alcázar of Mérida is a 12th-century Arab fortress that houses a museum.

### 19.2 Cáceres

Cáceres is the capital of the province of Cáceres, with over 95,000 inhabitants in the municipality. Founded by the Romans in the 1st century BC as Norba Caesarina, the city was an important administrative and cultural center of Roman Hispania. The Old Town of Cáceres was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986, and houses Renaissance palaces, medieval towers, and monumental squares such as the Plaza Mayor, the Plaza de San Mateo, and the Plaza de la Constitución. The Palacio de los Golfines de Abajo is a 15th-century Renaissance palace that houses a museum and cultural space. The Palacio de los Comares de Cáceres is a 14th-century Mudéjar palace with a brick and ceramic façade. The Torre de Bujaco is a 12th-century Arab tower that dominates the city. Cáceres Cathedral is a 16th-century Gothic and Renaissance cathedral that houses a collection of historical artifacts. Cáceres' festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Jorge, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15.

### 19.3 Badajoz

Badajoz is Extremadura's most populous city, with over 150,000 inhabitants in the municipality, and a border city with Portugal. Founded by Count Alderico de la Mansura in the 9th century, the city was an important commercial and administrative center during the Middle Ages. The Alcazaba of Badajoz is a 9th-century Arab fortress that dominates the city and houses a museum and cultural space. Badajoz Cathedral is a 15th-century Gothic and Renaissance cathedral that houses a collection of historical artifacts. The Puente de Palmas (Bridge of Palms) is a stone bridge over the Guadiana that connects Spain with Portugal. The Plaza de San Francisco is a pedestrian square with arches, balconies, and restaurants. Badajoz's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15. Badajoz's Carnival is one of Spain's most important, with costumes, comparsas, and music.

### 19.4 Extremaduran Gastronomy

Extremaduran cuisine is one of the most varied in Spain, with dishes that reflect the influence of Extremaduran, Andalusian, and Portuguese cooking. Gazpacho extremeño is a cold soup made with tomato, cucumber, pepper, garlic, olive oil, and vinegar, and is especially popular in summer. Migas extremeñas are a dish of fried bread with garlic, pepper, and olive oil, especially popular during popular festivals. Gazpacho manchego is a meat stew with vegetables and bread, a traditional dish from the region. Queso de la Serena is a sheep's milk cheese produced in La Serena, and is one of Spain's most famous cheeses. The wines of the D.O. Ribera del Guadiana are reds, whites, and rosés from grapes such as garnacha, tempranilla, and monastrell. Extremaduran desserts include pestiños (fried dough with honey), tortas de aceite (olive oil pastry), and bienmesabe (almond and egg yolk cake). Iberian acorn-fed ham from the Extremaduran dehesa is one of the most prized products of Spanish gastronomy.

## Chapter 20: Ávila, Segovia, and El Escorial

### 20.1 Ávila

Ávila is a city located in the Sierra de Guadarrama, with over 58,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city is known for its medieval walls, the most complete in Spain, measuring 2,516 meters in length, with 88 towers, 9 gates, and a thickness of 3 meters. The walls were built in the 11th century to protect the city from Muslim attacks, and were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985. Ávila Cathedral is a 12th-century Gothic and Romanesque cathedral, with a two-part façade and a 43-meter bell tower. The Basilica of San Vicente is a 12th-century Romanesque church that houses the Tomb of San Vicente, a masterpiece of Spanish Romanesque art. The Convento de Santa Teresa is a 17th-century Carmelite convent that houses a collection of historical artifacts related to Santa Teresa de Jesús, who was born in Ávila in 1515. The Old Town of Ávila was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985, and houses the walls, the cathedral, the Basilica of San Vicente, and multiple palaces and stately homes. Ávila's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15.

### 20.2 El Escorial

El Escorial is a city located in the Sierra de Guadarrama, with over 18,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The Monasterio de El Escorial is a monastery, palace, and royal pantheon built by Philip II in the 16th century, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. The monastery is one of the masterpieces of Spanish Renaissance architecture, with a floor plan in the shape of a grill, an interior of marbles, frescoes, and stuccos, and a Library housing over 40,000 books and manuscripts. The Panteón Real (Royal Pantheon) is the crypt where the remains of the Spanish kings from Charles V to Juan Carlos I are found. The Basilica is a Renaissance church with a dome 72 meters high and an interior decoration of frescoes by Luca Giordano. The Patio de los Reyes (Courtyard of the Kings) is a courtyard with statues of the kings of Israel and Judah. The Monasterio de El Escorial is one of Spain's most important monuments, and attracts thousands of visitors from around the world each year.

### 20.3 La Granja de San Ildefonso

La Granja de San Ildefonso is a city located in the Sierra de Guadarrama, with over 5,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The Palacio de la Granja de San Ildefonso is an 18th-century Baroque palace built by Philip V, which houses a collection of historical artifacts and gardens of extraordinary beauty with 26 water fountains representing the elements of nature. The Jardines de La Granja (Gardens of La Granja) are among Spain's most important Baroque gardens, with fountains, statues, cascades, and flowerbeds depicting mythological scenes. The Palacio Real de La Granja (Royal Palace of La Granja) is the royal summer residence, with interiors of marbles, frescoes, and tapestries. The Real Fábrica de Cristales de La Granja (Royal Glass Factory of La Granja) is an 18th-century glass factory that produces glassware of extraordinary quality. La Granja's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15. La Granja is an important tourist destination, with thousands of visitors seeking the palace, gardens, and mountain landscapes.

### 20.4 Madrid and Segovian Gastronomy

Madrid's gastronomy is one of the most varied in Spain, with dishes that reflect the influence of Castilian, Andalusian, and American cooking. Cocido madrileño is a chickpea stew with vegetables and meat, and is Madrid's most representative dish. Callos a la madrileña are a stew of pork stomach with chorizo and morcilla (blood sausage), a traditional Madrid dish. The bocadillo de calamares (squid sandwich) is a fried squid sandwich, a popular tapa in Madrid's bars. Churros con chocolate are a traditional sweet especially popular in winter. Huevos rotos are a dish of fried eggs with Iberian ham and french fries, served as a tapa or main course. Segovian gastronomy is known for cochinillo asado (roasted suckling pig), a dish prepared in a wood-fired oven. The suckling pig is the most famous dish of Segovian gastronomy, served with vegetables and potatoes, and its slow cooking ensures tender, juicy meat.

Huesca is the capital of the province of Huesca, with over 53,000 inhabitants in the municipality. ## Chapter 22: Industrial Cities - Bilbao, Valencia, and Barcelona

### 22.1 Spain's Industrial Model

Spain experienced a significant industrial transformation during the 19th and 20th centuries, with the creation of industrial centers in cities such as Bilbao, Barcelona, Valencia, Zaragoza, and Madrid. Bilbao became Spain's steel center, with shipyards, steel mills, and a naval industry that supplied the merchant marine and the navy. Barcelona was Spain's textile and commercial center, with textile factories, machinery, and food products. Valencia was the region's agricultural and commercial center, with food, textile, and furniture industries. Zaragoza was a logistical and commercial center, with rail and road connections throughout the country. Madrid was the administrative and financial center, with company headquarters, banks, and government bodies. Spain's industrialization was a complex and uneven process, with regions that industrialized rapidly (Catalonia, Basque Country, Asturias) and regions that remained agricultural (Castilla-La Mancha, Extremadura, Andalusia). The deindustrialization of the 1970s and 1980s deeply affected industrial cities, which had to reinvent themselves and diversify their economies.

### 22.2 Bilbao's Transformation

Bilbao is an example of successful industrial transformation, moving from a declining steel and shipping city to a cultural, technological, and service city. The construction of the Guggenheim Museum in 1997 was the catalyst for the transformation, attracting millions of visitors and creating a new image of a modern, cosmopolitan city. Bilbao's urban renewal included the construction of new riverside promenades, parks, cultural spaces, and office buildings. Bilbao's public transportation system, with its Metro, Cercanías commuter trains, and buses, was modernized and expanded. Bilbao's economy diversified into services (finance, technology, tourism), the design industry, and gastronomy. Bilbao's pintxos are internationally recognized, and the city has the highest concentration of Michelin stars per square meter in the world. The Guggenheim Effect has been studied by urban planners worldwide as an example of how a cultural project can transform a city.

### 22.3 Barcelona as an Economic Center

Barcelona is one of the most important economic centers in Spain and Europe, with a GDP representing approximately 19 percent of Catalonia's total. Barcelona's economy is predominantly service-based, with a significant presence of the financial sector, telecommunications, professional services, commerce, and tourism. Barcelona houses multiple headquarters of national and international companies, and is a center of technological innovation with multiple technology parks and research centers. Tourism is a fundamental economic engine, with millions of annual visitors seeking both cultural tourism (museums, monuments, architecture) and beach tourism (Costa Brava, Costa Dorada). Barcelona's industry is one of Spain's most dynamic, with internationally leading companies in sectors such as automotive (SEAT in Martorell), chemicals (BASF in Tarragona), pharmaceuticals, and technology. Barcelona Tech City is a technology cluster that brings together startups, technology companies, and research centers.

### 22.4 Valencia as an Entrepreneurial City

Valencia has become one of Spain's most entrepreneurial cities, with a startup, incubator, and accelerator ecosystem that has grown significantly in recent years. The City of Arts and Sciences, designed by Santiago Calatrava, is a futuristic architectural cultural and scientific complex that attracts millions of visitors annually. The Oceanographic is Europe's largest aquarium, with over 500 marine species. The Hemisferic is an IMAX cinema and planetarium. The Prince Felipe Science Museum is an interactive science museum. The Palau de las Artes Reina Sofía is a performing arts center with four halls. Valencia is also an important logistical center, with the Port of Valencia, one of the Mediterranean's most important, and Valencia Airport. Valencian gastronomy is one of the most recognized in Spain, with paella as its signature dish. The Fallas of Valencia are the city's most famous festival, declared Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO.

## Chapter 26: The Camino de Santiago and Its Cities

### 26.1 The French Way

The Camino de Santiago is one of the most important pilgrimage routes in the Christian world, with a length of over 800 kilometers crossing Spain from north to south. The Camino Francés (French Way) is the most popular route, and begins in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port (France) and ends in Santiago de Compostela (Galicia). Along the Camino, pilgrims pass through cities such as Pamplona, Estella, Los Arcos, Logroño, Santo Domingo de la Calzada, Burgos, León, Astorga, Ponferrada, and Santiago de Compostela. Each of these cities houses hostels, churches, monasteries, and monuments that bear witness to the history of the Camino. The Camino de Santiago was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1993, and is a tourist and spiritual destination that attracts thousands of pilgrims from around the world each year. Pilgrims walk, cycle, or ride on horseback, and tradition holds that they should carry a scallop shell as a symbol of their pilgrimage.

The cities of the Camino de Santiago reflect Spain's cultural and artistic diversity. Pamplona is the capital of Navarre, known for its Fiestas de San Fermín and encierros. Astorga is a Roman city known for its Gothic cathedral and its Episcopal Palace, designed by Antonio Gaudí. Ponferrada is a mining city known for its Templar Castle. Santiago de Compostela is the capital of Galicia and the final destination of the Camino, known for its Cathedral and the altar of the Virgen del Pilar. These cities are rest, supply, and spiritual points for pilgrims, and house multiple services for them, including hostels, hospitals, churches, and monasteries.

### 26.3 Gastronomy of the Camino

The gastronomy of the Camino de Santiago is one of Spain's most varied, with dishes that reflect the influence of the cuisines of the regions through which the Camino passes. In Galicia, dishes include pulpo a la gallega (Galician-style octopus), empanada gallega (Galician pie filled with fish or meat), and wines from the D.O. Rías Baixas. The End of the Camino is celebrated with a mass in the Cathedral of Santiago, where pilgrims receive the Compostela (a certificate confirming they have completed the Camino).

## Chapter 33: Cities of Southern Spain

### 33.1 Cádiz

Cádiz is one of Europe's oldest cities, founded by the Phoenicians in the 11th century BC as Gadir. The city has over 115,000 inhabitants in the municipality, and is known for its history, culture, and beaches. Cádiz Cathedral is an 18th-century Baroque and neoclassical cathedral, with a 60-meter dome and a bell tower with views of the city and the sea. The Torre del Tavira is an 18th-century watchtower that houses a camera obscura with views of the city and the port. The Playa de la Caleta is an urban beach located between two castles (the Castillo de Santa Catalina and the Castillo de San Sebastián), with golden sand and views of the Atlantic. The Barrio del Pópulo is the city's historic quarter, with narrow streets, squares with orange trees, and courtyards with flowers. Cádiz's festivals include the Carnavales de Cádiz (one of Spain's most important), Holy Week, and the Feria de Abril. Gaditan gastronomy is known for pescaitos fritos (fried small fish), atún de almadraba (line-caught tuna), Iberian ham, and wines from the D.O. Jerez.

### 33.2 Córdoba

Córdoba is a city located in the Guadalquivir valley, with over 320,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city is known for the Mezquita de Córdoba (Mosque of Córdoba), one of the world's most important religious buildings, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The mosque was built between the 8th and 10th centuries, and covers 23,400 square meters, with 856 columns of marble, granite, and jasper. The Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos is a 14th-century palatial fortress that houses a museum and spectacular gardens. The Judería (Jewish Quarter) is the historic Jewish neighborhood, with the Synagogue, the Jewish Museum, and the Patio de los Judíos (Court of the Jews). The Patios de Córdoba is a celebration where residents open their courtyards to the public, decorated with flowers, plants, and fountains, and has been declared Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Córdoba's festivals include Holy Week, the Patios de Córdoba, and the Feria de Mayo.

### 33.3 Jaén

Jaén is the capital of the province of Jaén, with over 115,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city is known for its olive oil production, as the province of Jaén is the world's largest producer of olive oil. Jaén Cathedral is a 16th-century Renaissance cathedral, with a three-part façade and a 62-meter bell tower. The Castillo de Santa Catalina is a 13th-century Arab fortress that dominates the city and houses a Parador Nacional (a luxury hotel with spectacular views). The Museo de Jaén is a museum of history and archaeology that houses a collection of Roman and Visigothic artifacts. Jaén's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15. Jaenense gastronomy is known for extra virgin olive oil, pipirrana, fría pipirrana, ajo blanco, molletes de Antequera, and wines from the D.O. Sierra de Cazorla.

### 33.4 Huelva and the Costa de la Luz

Huelva is a coastal city located in southwestern Spain, with over 145,000 inhabitants in the municipality. The city is known for its beaches, red fruit production, and mining history. The Playa de la Costilla is one of Spain's most important urban beaches, with golden sand and views of the Atlantic. Huelva Cathedral is a 19th-century Neo-Gothic cathedral that houses a collection of historical artifacts. The Museo de Huelva is a museum of history and archaeology that houses a collection of Tartessian and Roman artifacts. Huelva's festivals include Holy Week, the Feria de San Juan, and the celebration of the city's day on May 15. Huelvan gastronomy is known for seafood, fish, cured meats, cheeses, Iberian acorn-fed ham from Jabugo, and wines from the D.O. Huelva. The Costa de la Luz is the Atlantic coast of Andalusia, with sandy beaches, cliffs, and fishing villages such as Ayamonte, Isla Cristina, and El Rocío.
