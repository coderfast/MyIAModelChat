# The Common Foreign and Security Policy of the European Union

## Chapter 1: Foundations of the Common Foreign and Security Policy

### The Origins of the Common Foreign and Security Policy

The Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP) is the framework through which the European Union defines and implements a common foreign policy and a common security policy among the Member States. The origins of the CFSP date back to the Treaty of Maastricht of 1992, which established the Common Foreign and Security Policy as the second pillar of the European Union, along with the internal market and justice and home affairs. The Treaty of Maastricht recognized that the European Union needed a common voice in external affairs to promote its interests and values on the international stage, and that the coordination of the foreign policies of the Member States was essential to maximize the EU's impact in international relations. The Treaty of Amsterdam of 1997 strengthened the CFSP by creating the position of High Representative for the Common Foreign and Security Policy, who had the responsibility of representing the EU in external affairs and coordinating the foreign policy of the Member States.

The Common Foreign and Security Policy has evolved significantly since its creation, adapting to changes in the geopolitical environment and to the new threats and challenges facing the EU. The Treaty of Nice of 2001 strengthened the EU's crisis management capabilities and established the Common Principles and Objectives of the CFSP. The Treaty of Lisbon of 2007 represented a profound reform of the CFSP by creating the position of High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy, who assumed the functions of the High Representative for the CFSP and the European Commissioner for External Relations, and by establishing the European External Action Service (EEAS), a permanent diplomatic service that supports the High Representative in the implementation of the EU's foreign policy. The Treaty of Lisbon also established the EU Strategic Compass, which defines the interests and strategic objectives of the EU in external relations, and the European Security Strategy, which identifies the main threats to EU security and proposes measures to address them.

### The Principles and Objectives of the Common Foreign and Security Policy

The principles and objectives of the Common Foreign and Security Policy are defined by Article 21 of the Treaty on European Union, which establishes that the EU's external action shall be guided by the principles that have inspired its own creation, including the promotion of the values of democracy, the rule of law, the universality and indivisibility of human rights and fundamental freedoms, respect for human dignity, the principles of the United Nations Charter and international law. Article 21 also establishes that the EU's external action shall have the objective of: consolidating and supporting democracy, the rule of law, human rights and the principles of international law; preserving peace, preventing conflicts and strengthening international security; fostering the sustainable development of peoples, including the eradication of poverty and respect for human rights; and promoting a system of multilateral and global governance. These principles and objectives guide the EU's foreign policy in its relations with third countries and international organizations, and provide a framework for decision-making in external affairs.

The principles and objectives of the CFSP reflect the fundamental values of the European Union, including democracy, the rule of law, human rights, human dignity and equality, which are the values on which European integration was built and which the EU seeks to promote on the international stage. The promotion of these values in the EU's external action is a fundamental objective that is reflected in all dimensions of foreign policy, including development cooperation, trade agreements, humanitarian aid, diplomacy and security policy. The EU has adopted instruments to promote these values on the international stage, including the conditionality of development aid to respect for human rights, human rights clauses in trade agreements, and sanctions against countries that violate human rights. The promotion of democratic values and human rights in the EU's external action is a constant commitment that reflects the EU's identity as a values-based project.

## Chapter 2: The High Representative and the European External Action Service

### The Role of the High Representative

The High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy is the highest authority on foreign policy matters of the European Union and has the responsibility of directing the EU's common foreign and security policy, of representing the EU in external affairs, and of coordinating the EU's external action with that of the Member States. The High Representative is appointed by the European Council, acting by qualified majority, with the agreement of the President of the European Commission, and exercises his functions for a renewable five-year term. The High Representative is simultaneously Vice-President of the European Commission and High Representative of the Council for foreign affairs and security policy, which enables him to articulate the external dimensions of all EU policies, including trade policy, development cooperation and neighbourhood policy. The High Representative chairs the meetings of the Ministers of Foreign Affairs of the Member States in the Foreign Affairs Council, which is the main body for formulating the EU's foreign policy.

The High Representative has several important functions in the EU's foreign policy, including representing the EU in international organizations, conducting negotiations with third countries and international organizations, proposing common positions and joint actions of the Council, overseeing the implementation of Council decisions on foreign policy, and coordinating the EU's external action with that of the Member States. The High Representative is also responsible for managing the EU's civilian and military missions abroad, which include crisis management missions, security force training missions and election observation missions. The High Representative works closely with the Member States, the European Commission and the European Parliament to ensure the coherence and effectiveness of the EU's external action, and to promote the EU's interests and values on the international stage.

### The European External Action Service

The European External Action Service (EEAS) is the permanent diplomatic service of the European Union that supports the High Representative in the implementation of the EU's foreign policy. The EEAS was created by the Treaty of Lisbon in 2009 and began operations in 2011, and is composed of officials from the European Commission, the EU Council and the national diplomatic services of the Member States. The EEAS is headquartered in Brussels and has EU delegations worldwide, which are the EU's diplomatic representations to third countries and international organizations. The EEAS has the responsibility of providing diplomatic and logistical support to the High Representative, preparing meetings of the Foreign Affairs Council, managing EU delegations abroad, and coordinating the EU's external action with that of the Member States.

The EEAS is a unique institution in the international diplomatic landscape, as it is the first supranational diplomatic service of its kind, combining officials from the European Commission, the EU Council and the national diplomatic services of the Member States. The EEAS is organized into thematic and geographical departments, which include departments for Africa, Asia-Pacific, the Americas, Europe and the Mediterranean, as well as departments for global issues such as climate change, human rights, non-proliferation of weapons of mass destruction and security policy. The EEAS currently has approximately 4,000 officials, including approximately 1,800 Council officials, approximately 1,200 Commission officials and approximately 1,000 officials from the national diplomatic services of the Member States. The EEAS has contributed significantly to strengthening the coherence and effectiveness of the EU's external action, by providing a permanent diplomatic service that supports the High Representative in the implementation of the EU's foreign policy.

## Chapter 3: Tools of the EU's Foreign Policy

### Multilateral and Bilateral Diplomacy

Multilateral diplomacy is a fundamental tool of the EU's foreign policy, as the EU is a committed advocate of multilateralism and strengthening the rules-based multilateral governance system. The EU is a member of the main international organizations, including the United Nations, the World Trade Organization, the G7, the G20 and numerous regional organizations, and participates actively in the formulation of international rules and standards. The EU uses its participation in international organizations to promote its interests and values, including the promotion of democracy, human rights, sustainable development, international peace and security, and strengthening of international law. The EU also participates in multilateral negotiations on key issues such as climate change, trade, non-proliferation of weapons of mass destruction and regulation of new technologies.

Bilateral diplomacy is another important tool of the EU's foreign policy, as the EU maintains bilateral relations with most countries in the world and with the main international organizations. The EU's bilateral relations are based on association agreements, cooperation agreements and other legal instruments that establish the framework for cooperation between the EU and third countries in areas such as trade, development cooperation, security policy, human rights and scientific and technological cooperation. The EU maintains delegations in more than 140 countries and representations to international organizations, which are the EU's diplomatic representations to third countries and international organizations. The EU delegations coordinate the EU's external action in the host country, represent the EU before the country's authorities, and manage cooperation between the EU and the country in all areas of external action.

### International Sanctions

International sanctions are an important tool of the EU's foreign policy, which uses sanctions to promote peace, security and human rights, and to address threats to peace and international security. EU international sanctions may include arms embargoes, travel restrictions, asset freezes, trade restrictions and other restrictive measures. International sanctions are adopted by the EU Council on the basis of the common positions and joint actions of the Council, and are implemented by the Member States and EU institutions. EU international sanctions are applied to persons, entities and countries that represent a threat to peace and international security, that violate human rights, that support terrorism or that engage in prohibited nuclear, chemical or biological activities.

The EU has imposed international sanctions on numerous countries and persons in recent years, including sanctions against Russia for the annexation of Crimea and aggression against Ukraine, sanctions against North Korea for its nuclear and missile program, sanctions against Iran for its nuclear program, sanctions against Syria for human rights violations, sanctions against Venezuela for the breakdown of democratic order, and sanctions against Myanmar for human rights violations against the Rohingya minority. EU international sanctions are an important tool for promoting EU values and interests on the international stage, but their effectiveness depends on the political will of the Member States to implement them and on international cooperation to ensure compliance. The EU also works with its international partners to coordinate sanctions and maximize their impact, which requires cooperation with the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan and other international partners who share the same foreign policy objectives.

## Chapter 4: Security and Defence Policy

### Common Security and Defence Policy

The Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP) is the defence and security component of the Common Foreign and Security Policy, and aims to provide the European Union with the necessary capabilities to act in matters of security and defence. The CSDP was established by the Treaty of Amsterdam in 1997 and has evolved significantly since then, adapting to changes in the security environment and the new threats facing the EU. The CSDP includes the definition of the EU's strategic interests, the development of the EU's military and civilian capabilities, the implementation of the EU's missions and operations abroad, and defence cooperation among the Member States. The CSDP is based on solidarity among the Member States, which are obliged to provide mutual assistance in the event of armed aggression against a Member State, in accordance with Article 42.7 of the Treaty on European Union.

The implementation of the CSDP is carried out through several instruments, including the EU's civilian and military missions and operations, the EU's military and civilian capabilities, and defence cooperation among the Member States. The EU's missions and operations include crisis management missions, security force training missions, election observation missions and counter-terrorism missions. These missions and operations are implemented under the authority of the EU Council, which takes decisions on the launch, direction and termination of missions and operations. The EU has implemented more than 30 missions and operations in recent years, including missions in the Western Balkans, Africa, the Middle East and Asia, which have contributed to the stabilization of regions affected by conflicts and to the promotion of peace and security.

### The EU's Military and Civilian Capabilities

The development of the EU's military and civilian capabilities is a fundamental component of the Common Security and Defence Policy, as the EU needs to have the necessary capabilities to implement its missions and operations abroad. The EU's military capabilities include the armed forces of the Member States, which may be made available to the EU for the implementation of CSDP missions and operations, and European military capabilities, which include rapid reaction forces, strategic transport capabilities, intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance capabilities, and combat capabilities. The EU's civilian capabilities include police, judicial, civil protection, border management and state-building capabilities, which are necessary for the implementation of the EU's civilian missions abroad.

The development of the EU's military and civilian capabilities is carried out through several mechanisms, including the European Defence Agency (EDA), which has the responsibility of promoting defence cooperation among the Member States and developing European military capabilities; the European Defence Fund (EDF), which finances research and development of defence technologies; and Permanent Structured Cooperation (PESCO), which allows Member States that wish to do so to jointly develop military capabilities and procure equipment jointly. The EDA was created in 2004 and is headquartered in Brussels, and is composed of representatives of all Member States. The EDA promotes defence cooperation by identifying common needs, developing joint capability projects and facilitating joint procurement of defence equipment. The European Defence Fund was created in 2021 with an endowment of 7.953 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period, and finances research and development of defence technologies, adaptation of existing capabilities and development of disruptive next-generation capabilities.

## Chapter 5: Neighbourhood and Enlargement Policy

### The European Union's Neighbourhood Policy

The European Union's neighbourhood policy aims to promote stability, security and prosperity in the EU's neighbouring regions, including Eastern and Southern Europe, North Africa and the Middle East. The neighbourhood policy is based on the principle of differentiation, which recognizes that neighbouring countries have different needs and capacities, and that the EU must adapt its action to the specific circumstances of each country. The neighbourhood policy includes the Eastern Partnership, which encompasses Armenia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Georgia, Moldova and Ukraine; the Union for the Mediterranean, which encompasses Algeria, Egypt, Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Morocco, Palestine, Syria and Tunisia; and cooperation with other neighbouring countries such as Russia, Turkey and the Western Balkan countries. The neighbourhood policy includes financial instruments such as the European Neighbourhood Instrument (ENI), which finances cooperation with neighbouring countries, and the European Instrument for Democracy and Human Rights (EIDHR), which finances the promotion of democracy and human rights in neighbouring countries.

The EU's neighbourhood policy has achieved significant results in recent years, including the signing of association agreements with several Eastern Partnership countries, the holding of free and fair elections in several neighbouring countries, improved cooperation in security and counter-terrorism, and the promotion of economic and political reforms in neighbouring countries. However, the neighbourhood policy also faces significant challenges, including political instability in several neighbouring countries, unresolved conflicts in the Middle East and the Caucasus, migratory pressure, terrorist threats and the growing influence of other international actors such as Russia and China in neighbouring regions. The EU has adapted its neighbourhood policy to address these challenges, including the adoption of new strategies for the Eastern Partnership and the Union for the Mediterranean, and the strengthening of cooperation in security and counter-terrorism.

### The European Union's Enlargement Policy

The European Union's enlargement policy aims to facilitate the accession of new Member States to the EU, promoting stability, security and prosperity in Europe. The enlargement policy is based on the Copenhagen criteria, established in 1993, which define the conditions that candidate countries must meet to be admitted to the EU, including the stability of institutions guaranteeing democracy, the rule of law, human rights and respect for minorities; a functioning market economy and the ability to cope with competitive pressure and market forces within the EU; and the ability to assume the obligations of membership, including the political, economic and monetary objectives of the Union. The enlargement policy is a powerful instrument for promoting reforms in candidate countries, as the prospect of EU accession provides an incentive for countries to implement significant political, economic and social reforms.

The EU has expanded its membership on several occasions since its creation, including the enlargements of 1973 (Denmark, Ireland and the United Kingdom), 1981 (Greece), 1986 (Spain and Portugal), 1995 (Austria, Finland and Sweden), 2004 (Cyprus, Slovakia, Slovenia, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Hungary, Malta, Poland and the Czech Republic), 2007 (Bulgaria and Romania) and 2013 (Croatia). Currently, the candidate countries for accession are Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, Turkey and Ukraine. The EU has opened accession negotiations with several candidate countries, and has granted candidate status to Ukraine, Moldova and Georgia in response to Russian aggression against Ukraine. The enlargement policy is a continuous process that requires the political will of candidate countries to implement significant reforms and the willingness of existing Member States to admit new members to the EU.

## Chapter 6: The European Union's Internal Security Policy

### Counter-Terrorism

Counter-terrorism is a priority of the European Union's internal security policy, as terrorism represents a significant threat to peace, security and the fundamental rights of European citizens. The EU has adopted a comprehensive approach to counter-terrorism, which includes prevention, protection, prosecution, response and recovery. Prevention includes combating radicalization and violent radicalization, education and awareness-raising, and cooperation with communities affected by radicalization. Protection includes the security of critical infrastructure, the protection of public spaces and the security of mass events. Prosecution includes the investigation and prosecution of terrorists, police intelligence and international judicial cooperation. Response includes the management of the consequences of terrorist attacks and the coordination of emergency services. Recovery includes support for victims and the rehabilitation of affected communities.

The EU has adopted several instruments for counter-terrorism, including the 2002 Framework Decision on combating terrorism, which establishes common definitions of terrorist offences and related offences, and minimum penalties for these crimes; the Directive on preventing the use of the financial system for the financing of terrorism, which establishes obligations for the prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing for financial entities; and the Aviation Package, which establishes aviation security standards for intra-Community flights and flights from or to third countries. The EU has also created Europol, the European Police Agency, which has the responsibility of coordinating police cooperation between the Member States in counter-terrorism and other forms of serious crime, and Eurojust, the EU Agency for Criminal Justice Cooperation, which has the responsibility of coordinating judicial cooperation between the Member States in counter-terrorism and other forms of serious crime.

### Combating Organized Crime

Combating organized crime is another priority of the European Union's internal security policy, as organized crime represents a significant threat to the security and fundamental rights of European citizens. Organized crime includes drug trafficking, human trafficking, arms trafficking, cybercrime, corruption, money laundering, fraud and other serious offences committed by structured criminal organizations. The EU has adopted a comprehensive approach to combating organized crime, which includes prevention, investigation, prosecution, international judicial and police cooperation, and victim protection. International police cooperation is carried out through Europol, which coordinates police cooperation between the Member States, and through Interpol, which coordinates police cooperation at the global level. International judicial cooperation is carried out through Eurojust, which coordinates judicial cooperation between the Member States, and through the European Judicial Network, which facilitates cooperation between the judicial authorities of the Member States.

The EU has adopted several instruments for combating organized crime, including the 2002 Framework Decision on combating organized crime, which establishes common definitions of offences committed within the framework of a criminal organization and minimum penalties for these crimes; the Directive on the rights of crime victims, which establishes minimum standards on the rights, support and protection of crime victims; and the Regulation on the transfer of criminal proceedings, which facilitates the transfer of criminal proceedings between the Member States. The EU has also created the European Border and Coast Guard Agency (Frontex), which has the responsibility of managing the EU's external borders and combating irregular immigration, and the European Asylum Agency (EASO), which has the responsibility of supporting the Member States in the management of asylum applications. Combating organized crime is a continuing challenge that requires close cooperation between the Member States and constant adaptation of strategies and tools to address new forms of crime.

## Chapter 7: European Defence Policy

### Development of Defence Capabilities

The development of defence capabilities is an essential component of the Common Security and Defence Policy, and aims to ensure that the EU has the necessary capabilities to implement its missions and operations abroad. The development of defence capabilities is carried out through several mechanisms, including the European Defence Agency (EDA), which promotes defence cooperation among the Member States and develops European military capabilities; the European Defence Fund (EDF), which finances research and development of defence technologies; and Permanent Structured Cooperation (PESCO), which allows Member States that wish to do so to jointly develop military capabilities and procure equipment jointly. The development of defence capabilities is a significant challenge for the EU, as it requires cooperation between the Member States, which have different interests and capabilities in defence, and the allocation of sufficient resources to finance research, development and procurement of military capabilities.

The EDA was created in 2004 with the mission of strengthening the EU's defence capacity, promoting cooperation between the defence industries of the Member States and supporting the EU's security and defence policy. The EDA identifies common capability needs, facilitates cooperation between defence industries and supports research and development of defence technologies. The EDA has launched numerous defence cooperation projects, including projects for the development of unmanned aircraft, satellite surveillance systems, secure communications and strategic transport capabilities. The EDF was created in 2021 with an endowment of 7.953 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period, and finances research and development of defence technologies, adaptation of existing capabilities and development of disruptive next-generation capabilities. The EDF is the first European financial instrument dedicated to defence research and development, and aims to strengthen the European defence industrial and technological base.

### The European Defence Industry

The European defence industry is a strategic sector that provides the Member States with the military capabilities necessary to ensure their security and to contribute to the Common Security and Defence Policy. The European defence industry is composed of numerous companies of all sizes, from large multinational corporations such as Airbus, BAE Systems, Leonardo, Thales and Safran, to small and medium-sized enterprises that supply components and services to the large corporations. The European defence industry produces a wide range of military products and services, including aircraft, warships, armoured vehicles, weapons systems, communications systems, intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance systems, and cybersecurity systems. The European defence industry is a major player in the international defence market, exporting military products and services to countries around the world.

The EU has adopted measures to strengthen the European defence industry, including the European Defence Industrial Strategy, which establishes the priorities and instruments for the development of the European defence industry, and the Defence Industry Action Plan, which defines concrete measures to strengthen the competitiveness of the European defence industry. The EU has also adopted measures to facilitate cooperation between the defence industries of the Member States, including the adoption of common standards for defence equipment procurement, the facilitation of defence product exports, and the development of common defence markets. Cooperation between the defence industries of the Member States is important to reduce duplication of efforts, to take advantage of economies of scale and to strengthen the European defence industrial and technological base. The EU also works to strengthen the strategic autonomy of European defence, reducing dependence on foreign technologies and equipment and developing European military capabilities that enable the EU to act autonomously in matters of security and defence.

## Chapter 8: Current Challenges in Foreign and Security Policy

### Russian Aggression Against Ukraine

Russian aggression against Ukraine, which began with the annexation of Crimea in 2014 and intensified with the full-scale invasion in February 2022, is the most serious security challenge the European Union has faced since the Cold War. Russian aggression against Ukraine has constituted a serious violation of international law, the sovereignty and territorial integrity of Ukraine, and has represented a significant threat to peace and security in Europe. The EU has responded to Russian aggression by adopting unprecedented sanctions against Russia, including arms embargoes, trade restrictions, asset freezes and travel restrictions, and by providing financial, humanitarian and military assistance to Ukraine. The EU has also granted Ukraine candidate status for accession, reflecting the EU's commitment to Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity and to its European future.

Russian aggression against Ukraine has had a significant impact on the EU's foreign and security policy, accelerating the transformation of European security policy and strengthening cohesion among the Member States on foreign policy. Russian aggression has highlighted the need to strengthen the EU's defence capabilities, diversify energy supply sources to reduce dependence on Russia, and strengthen the EU's resilience to hybrid threats, including disinformation, cyberattacks and political interference. The EU has adopted measures to strengthen its defence policy, including the adoption of the Strategic Compass, which defines the priorities and instruments of the EU's defence policy for the next ten years, and the creation of the EU Rapid Deployment Capacity, a rapid military force that can be deployed in crisis situations. Russian aggression against Ukraine has demonstrated that the EU needs to be able to act more quickly and decisively in matters of security and defence, and has strengthened the commitment of the Member States to the Common Security and Defence Policy.

### Hybrid Threats and Cybersecurity

Hybrid threats and cybersecurity are increasingly important security challenges for the European Union, as state and non-state actors use hybrid techniques, including disinformation, cyberattacks, political interference and covert operations, to weaken social cohesion, undermine democracies and affect EU interests. Hybrid threats are particularly difficult to combat because they combine military and non-military methods, and because they are often difficult to attribute to a specific actor. The EU has adopted a comprehensive approach to combating hybrid threats, which includes prevention, detection, deterrence, response and recovery. Prevention includes combating disinformation, media education and citizen awareness. Detection includes intelligence sharing and monitoring of hybrid threats. Deterrence includes imposing costs on the perpetrators of hybrid threats. Response includes crisis management and coordination of security services. Recovery includes the restoration of normality after a hybrid attack.

Cybersecurity is an increasingly important security challenge for the EU, as the digitalization of the economy and society has created new vulnerabilities that can be exploited by malicious actors. Cyberattacks can affect critical infrastructure, communications networks, financial systems, public administration and citizens, and can have significant consequences for the EU's economy and security. The EU has adopted a regulatory framework for cybersecurity, including the NIS Directive, which establishes information security obligations for entities in critical sectors, and the Cybersecurity Regulation, which establishes security standards for digital products and services. The EU has also created the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA), which has the responsibility of supporting the Member States in cybersecurity matters and promoting cooperation in combating cyber threats. The EU has also adopted a package of measures to strengthen cybersecurity, including the creation of the Cybersecurity Rescue, which provides technical assistance to Member States affected by severe cyberattacks, and the adoption of cybersecurity standards for connected devices, including vehicles, medical devices and industrial products.

## Chapter 3: Missions and Operations of the Common Security and Defence Policy

### Development of the EU's Military Capabilities

The development of the European Union's military capabilities is a fundamental aspect of the Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP), as the EU needs adequate military capabilities to carry out its missions and operations abroad effectively and autonomously. The EU has adopted several initiatives to develop its military capabilities, including Permanent Structured Cooperation (PESCO), which allows Member States that wish to do so to cooperate more closely on defence; the European Defence Fund, which finances research and development of defence technologies; and Capability Planning and Review, which assesses the military capabilities of the Member States and identifies capability needs for CSDP missions and operations. The development of the EU's military capabilities is a complex challenge that requires cooperation between the Member States and investment in innovative defence technologies.

Permanent Structured Cooperation (PESCO) is an important instrument for the development of the EU's military capabilities, as it allows Member States that wish to do so to cooperate more closely on defence, including joint planning of military operations, joint development of military capabilities and joint procurement of military equipment. PESCO was established by the Treaty of Lisbon and has been ratified by the majority of EU Member States. PESCO has resulted in the adoption of joint defence cooperation projects, including the development of armoured vehicles, air defence systems and cybersecurity capabilities. PESCO is a fundamental instrument for promoting defence cooperation among the Member States and for developing the EU's military capabilities more efficiently and effectively.

The European Defence Fund is another important instrument for the development of the EU's military capabilities, as it finances research and development of innovative defence technologies. The European Defence Fund has an endowment of 8 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period, and finances research and development projects in areas such as artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, autonomous systems and next-generation defence technologies. The European Defence Fund also finances the creation of defence industrial consortia that include companies from different Member States, which promotes industrial cooperation in the defence sector and reduces the fragmentation of the European defence industry. The European Defence Fund is a fundamental instrument for promoting innovation in defence and for developing the EU's military capabilities more efficiently and effectively.

### Civilian Missions of the CSDP

The civilian missions of the Common Security and Defence Policy are operations deployed in third countries to support peacebuilding, governance and the rule of law, and include civilian components such as police training, judicial assistance, election observation and political mediation. The EU has deployed numerous civilian missions in different regions of the world, including Africa, the Balkans, the Middle East and Asia, which have contributed significantly to stability and security in the regions of intervention. The CSDP's civilian missions are an important tool of the EU's foreign policy, as they enable the EU to act jointly and in a coordinated manner in matters of peacebuilding and governance, and to promote its values of democracy, human rights and the rule of law in developing countries.

The EU has deployed civilian missions in several areas, including police training, judicial assistance, election observation and political mediation. The EUPOL police training mission in Afghanistan, for example, provided training and assistance to the Afghan police forces to improve their capacity to maintain security and public order. The EUEOM election observation mission in several African and Eastern European countries provided election observers to oversee the holding of free and fair elections. The EUFOR political mediation mission in the Balkans provided political assistance to facilitate conflict resolution and the promotion of peace and stability. The CSDP's civilian missions are an important tool for the EU's foreign policy, as they enable the EU to act jointly and in a coordinated manner in matters of peacebuilding and governance.

The EU has also adopted measures to improve the effectiveness of its civilian missions, including improving the planning and management of missions, training mission personnel and coordinating with other international actors, including the United Nations, NATO and regional organizations. The EU has adopted the Strategic Concept for CSDP Civilian Missions, which establishes a framework for the planning, management and evaluation of civilian missions, and has created the Civilian Operations Centre, which provides operational and logistical support to CSDP civilian missions. Improving the effectiveness of civilian missions is a continuing challenge that requires cooperation between the Member States and investment in training and capabilities of mission personnel.

## Chapter 4: Security and Defence Policy and NATO

### The Relationship Between the EU and NATO

The relationship between the European Union and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) is a fundamental aspect of the EU's security and defence policy, as the majority of EU Member States are also NATO members, and cooperation between the EU and NATO is essential to ensure the security and defence of Europe. The EU and NATO share common objectives, including the defence of democratic values, the promotion of international peace and security, and the fight against security threats, including terrorism, cybersecurity and hybrid threats. Cooperation between the EU and NATO has intensified in recent years, especially after Russian aggression against Ukraine, which has highlighted the need for greater cooperation in defence between the two organizations.

The EU and NATO have adopted joint declarations that define the priorities and areas of cooperation between the two organizations, including collective defence, crisis management, communications security and resilience. The EU and NATO have also established cooperation mechanisms, including the EU-NATO Cooperation Group, which coordinates cooperation activities between the two organizations, and joint military exercises, which allow the EU and NATO military forces to train together and improve their interoperability. Cooperation between the EU and NATO is a fundamental instrument for ensuring the security and defence of Europe, and for promoting international peace and security.

However, the relationship between the EU and NATO also poses challenges, including the question of the EU's strategic autonomy, the competition between EU and NATO military capabilities, and the differences in the foreign policy priorities of the Member States. Some EU Member States are NATO members and see NATO as the main framework for their defence, while other EU Member States are not NATO members and see the CSDP as an instrument for promoting their strategic autonomy. The EU must find a balance between cooperation with NATO and the development of its own defence capabilities, and must ensure that cooperation with NATO does not compromise its strategic autonomy. The relationship between the EU and NATO is a complex challenge that requires cooperation and commitment between the Member States to find balanced solutions that benefit both organizations.

### The EU's Strategic Autonomy

The concept of the European Union's strategic autonomy refers to the EU's capacity to act autonomously in matters of security and defence, without excessive dependence on other international actors, including the United States and NATO. The EU's strategic autonomy is a concept that has gained importance in recent years, especially after Russian aggression against Ukraine, which has highlighted the need for the EU to be able to act autonomously in defence matters to protect its interests and values. The EU's strategic autonomy includes the EU's capacity to develop its own military capabilities, to plan and execute military operations autonomously, and to take foreign policy and security decisions independently.

The EU's strategic autonomy is a concept that poses significant challenges, including the need to increase defence spending by the Member States, the need to develop European military capabilities that enable the EU to act autonomously, and the need to overcome differences in the foreign policy and security priorities of the Member States. The EU has adopted several measures to strengthen its strategic autonomy, including the adoption of the Strategic Compass, which defines the priorities and instruments of the EU's defence policy for the next ten years; the creation of the EU Rapid Deployment Capacity, a rapid military force that can be deployed in crisis situations; and the adoption of the European Defence Fund, which finances research and development of defence technologies. The EU's strategic autonomy is a long-term challenge that requires cooperation between the Member States and investment in military capabilities and defence technologies.

The EU's strategic autonomy also includes the EU's capacity to address hybrid threats, including disinformation, cyberattacks and political interference, which are threats that cannot be addressed exclusively through military means. The EU has adopted a comprehensive approach to combating hybrid threats, which includes prevention, detection, deterrence, response and recovery. The EU has also adopted measures to improve its cybersecurity, including the creation of the Cybersecurity Rescue, which provides technical assistance to Member States affected by severe cyberattacks. The EU's strategic autonomy is a broad concept that includes not only military capabilities, but also civilian, economic and technological capabilities that are necessary to protect the EU's interests and values in an increasingly complex and competitive world.

## Chapter 5: The European Union's Neighbourhood Policy

### The Eastern Neighbourhood

The Eastern Neighbourhood is a priority region for the European Union's foreign policy, as it includes countries that share land borders with the EU and that have historical, cultural and economic ties with Europe. The Eastern Neighbourhood includes countries such as Ukraine, Moldova, Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan and Belarus, and the EU's neighbourhood policy towards this region aims to promote stability, security, democracy and economic development in these countries. The EU has adopted several instruments for the neighbourhood policy towards the Eastern Neighbourhood, including Association Agreements, which establish frameworks for political, economic and security cooperation between the EU and Eastern Neighbourhood countries, and the European Neighbourhood Policy, which establishes a general framework for the EU's neighbourhood policy.

Association Agreements are important instruments of the EU's neighbourhood policy towards the Eastern Neighbourhood, as they establish frameworks for political, economic and security cooperation between the EU and Eastern Neighbourhood countries. Association Agreements include provisions on trade liberalization, political cooperation, cooperation in justice and home affairs, and cooperation in areas such as energy, transport and the environment. The EU has concluded Association Agreements with Ukraine, Moldova and Georgia, which entered into force in 2014 and have resulted in the deepening of political and economic cooperation between the EU and these countries. Association Agreements also include provisions on human rights, democracy and the rule of law, which establish that the parties undertake to respect and promote these values in their bilateral relations.
