# The Climates of the World

## Introduction

The climates of the world are the expression of the complex interaction between the atmosphere, the oceans, the mountains, and human activity. From sparsely populated deserts to lush tropical rainforests, the climates of the world are as varied as the landscapes we inhabit. The climates of the world not only determine temperature and precipitation, but also shape the culture, economy, and biodiversity of each region. This book explores the most important climates of the world, with detailed information about their characteristics, their geographic distribution, and their importance for humanity.

The climates of the world are the result of multiple factors, including latitude, altitude, proximity to the sea, and ocean currents. The Köppen climate classification, one of the most widely used, divides climates into six main categories: tropical, dry, temperate, continental, polar, and highland. Each of these categories has subcategories that describe the specific characteristics of each climate. The climates of the world are dynamic and change over time, and climate change is altering climatic patterns globally.

The climates of the world have a profound impact on human life. People have adapted their cultures, economies, and lifestyles to the climates of their regions. The climates of the world also determine which crops can be grown, which animals inhabit a region, and which economic activities are possible. Understanding the climates of the world is essential for urban planning, agriculture, water management, and adaptation to climate change.

## Chapter 1: The Humid Tropical Climate

### The Climate of Tropical Rainforests

The humid tropical climate, also known as the equatorial climate, is the warmest and most humid climate in the world, with average temperatures of 25 to 28 degrees Celsius and annual precipitation exceeding 2,000 millimeters. This climate is found in equatorial regions, including the Amazon Basin, Southeast Asia, and West Africa. The humid tropical climate is home to tropical rainforests, which are the ecosystems with the greatest biodiversity in the world.

The tropical rainforests of the humid tropical climate harbor millions of species of plants, animals, and microorganisms. The leaf litter layer on the soil is thin, and nutrients are rapidly recycled through decomposition. Rainforest trees are gigantic, with straight trunks and dense crowns that create a canopy that blocks sunlight. Epiphytic plants, such as orchids and bromeliads, grow on tree trunks and obtain nutrients from the air and rain.

The people who inhabit the tropical rainforests of the humid tropical climate have developed unique strategies for living in this hostile environment. The indigenous peoples of the Amazon Basin, such as the Yanomami and the Guaraní, have developed traditional knowledge about medicinal plants and rainforest animals. Cities in humid tropical regions, such as Singapore, Kuala Lumpur, and Quito, face challenges such as high humidity, torrential rains, and flooding. Adapting to the humid tropical climate is a continuous challenge that requires traditional knowledge and modern technologies.

## Chapter 2: The Dry Tropical Climate

### The Climate of Savannas

The dry tropical climate, also known as the savanna climate, is the climate of tropical regions with well-defined dry and wet seasons. Average temperatures range from 20 to 30 degrees Celsius, and annual precipitation ranges from 500 to 1,500 millimeters, with a dry season of three to six months. This climate is found in sub-Saharan Africa, tropical South America, and southern Asia.

The savannas of the dry tropical climate are open landscapes with scattered trees and tall grass. Savanna animals include giraffes, elephants, lions, cheetahs, and wildebeest, which migrate in search of water and food during the dry season. Savannas are important for livestock and agriculture, and the people who inhabit these regions have developed strategies for living with climate variability.

Cities in dry tropical regions, such as Nairobi, Kampala, and La Paz, face challenges such as precipitation variability, recurrent droughts, and flooding. Adapting to the dry tropical climate is a continuous challenge that requires traditional knowledge and modern technologies. Conservation efforts seek to protect savannas from deforestation and soil degradation, which are significant threats in these regions.

## Chapter 57: The Köppen-Geiger Classification — A Universal System

### Origin and Foundations

The Köppen climate classification, developed by German climatologist Wladimir Köppen between 1884 and 1936, and refined together with geographer Rudolf Geiger, is the most widely used climate classification system in the world. Its fundamental principle is that the distribution of natural vegetation is the best indicator of climate, since plants respond to temperature and accumulated precipitation over time.

Köppen defined five main climate groups, identified with capital letters: A (tropical), B (dry), C (temperate), D (continental), and E (polar). Each group is subdivided into subtypes according to temperature and precipitation patterns, using lowercase letters. This system, despite its apparent simplicity, is extraordinarily precise for describing the climatic conditions of any point on the planet.

### The Five Main Groups

**Group A — Tropical Climates:** Defined by having at least one month with average temperature above 18 degrees Celsius and no dry season (all months have precipitation above 60 mm). Includes subtypes Af (equatorial), Am (monsoon), and Aw (savanna). The Af climate is found in the Amazon Basin, the Gulf of Guinea, and Southeast Asia, with precipitation above 60 mm in all months. The Am, or monsoon, climate has a brief dry season but is compensated by abundant rainfall the rest of the year; it occurs in regions such as the Kerala coast in India or southeastern Madagascar. The Aw, or savanna, climate has a marked dry season during the winter solstice; it is dominant in much of sub-Saharan Africa, central Brazil, and northern Australia.

**Group B — Dry Climates:** Defined by having potential evapotranspiration exceeding precipitation. Köppen used formulas that combine temperature and precipitation to distinguish between semiarid (BS) and desert (BW) climates. The threshold varies with temperature: in warmer regions, more precipitation is needed to avoid drought. BW (desert) climates are subdivided into BWk (cold) and BWh (hot). The Sahara (BWh), the Atacama Desert (BWk), the Gobi Desert (BWk), and the Australian Desert (BWh) are the greatest exponents of this group.

**Group C — Temperate Climates:** Have at least one month with temperature above 10 degrees Celsius, the coldest month between -3 and 18 degrees, and at least one wet quarter. Includes subtypes Cfa (humid subtropical), Cfb (oceanic), Cfc (subpolar oceanic), Csa (hot Mediterranean), Csb (cool Mediterranean), and Csc (cold Mediterranean). Cfa dominates in the southeastern United States, southeastern China, and southeastern Brazil. Cfb is characteristic of Western Europe, southwestern Chile, and New Zealand. Csa and Csb define the Mediterranean Basin, California, central Chile, southwestern Australia, and the Cape of Good Hope.

**Group D — Continental Climates:** Have at least one month with temperature above 10 degrees and the coldest month below -3 degrees (or 0 degrees, according to Geiger's modification). Includes Dfa, Dfb, Dfc, Dfd, Dwa, Dwb, Dwc, and Dwd, where the letter 'w' indicates dry winter and the final letter indicates summer temperature (a: hot, b: temperate, c: cool, d: extremely cold). Dfc (subarctic) covers enormous expanses of Siberia, Canada, and Alaska. Dfa is found in the central United States and eastern China. Dwd, the most extreme subtype, only occurs in Yakutia (eastern Siberia), with winters down to -50 degrees Celsius.

**Group E — Polar Climates:** No month has temperature above 10 degrees Celsius. The ET (tundra) subtype has at least one month with temperature above 0 degrees, while EF (perpetual ice) has all months below 0 degrees. The tundra extends across northern Alaska, Canada, Siberia, Greenland, and Antarctica. The EF climate dominates in the interior of Antarctica, where temperatures can drop to -89.2 degrees Celsius (record recorded at Vostok station).

### Modifications and Updated Versions

The original Köppen classification has been modified several times. The most widely used version currently is that of Trewartha and Horn (1980), which introduces significant changes in groups C and D. Trewartha modifies the threshold for group D to require at least eight months with temperature above 10 degrees (instead of one), which considerably reduces the extent of continental climates. Additionally, it introduces group H (highland) for climates determined more by altitude than by latitude.

In 2006, Beck et al. published an update of the Köppen classification with a spatial resolution of 1 square kilometer, using data from the WorldClim database. This version, known as high-resolution Köppen-Geiger, has enabled more precise analysis of local climates and has facilitated detection of changes in the distribution of climate types over time.

### Practical Applications

The Köppen classification is widely used in geography, ecology, agriculture, urban planning, and climate change studies. Global climate models use the Köppen classification to project how climate type distributions will change in the future. According to IPCC models, by 2100, 10-20 percent of the Earth's surface could change climate category, with significant expansions of dry (B) and tropical (A) climates at the expense of temperate (C) and continental (D) climates.

## Chapter 58: Climate and World Agriculture

### Relationship between Climate and Food Production

Agriculture is the human activity most directly affected by climate. Temperature, precipitation, solar radiation, relative humidity, and wind patterns determine which crops can be grown in a region, when they are sown, and when they are harvested. Each crop has optimal temperature and humidity ranges; outside these ranges, production decreases or the crop simply does not survive.

Wheat, for example, is a temperate-climate crop that requires temperatures between 10 and 25 degrees during growth and a cooling phase (vernalization) to produce grain. It is grown successfully in regions with Cfb (oceanic) climate, such as Western Europe, and in regions with Dfb (continental temperate) climate, such as Russia and Canada. Rice, on the other hand, is a tropical and subtropical crop that requires temperatures above 20 degrees and abundant water; it dominates in regions with Af, Am, and Aw climates of Southeast Asia, southern India, and West Africa.

### Agricultural Production Zones by Climate Type

**Tropical Climates (A):** Agricultural production focuses on perennial crops such as oil palm (Caribbean, Malaysia, Indonesia), sugarcane (Brazil, India, Cuba), cacao (Ivory Coast, Ghana, Ecuador), coffee (Colombia, Brazil, Vietnam), banana (Ecuador, Costa Rica, Philippines), and cassava (Nigeria, Brazil, Thailand). Rice is grown in all humid tropical regions, especially in the Mekong Delta, the Ganges Delta, and southern China. Agricultural productivity in tropical climates is high year-round, but faces challenges such as pests, fungal diseases, and soil degradation through leaching.

**Dry Climates (B):** Agriculture depends almost exclusively on irrigation. The Nile Valley in Egypt, the Tigris and Euphrates in Iraq, the Indus in Pakistan, and the Murray-Darling in Australia are examples of regions where irrigation enables productive agriculture in the middle of the desert. Main crops include cotton (Uzbekistan, Egypt, India), irrigated wheat (Saudi Arabia, Pakistan), dates (Tunisia, Morocco, Iraq), and citrus fruits (Israel, Jordan, Arizona). Irrigation consumes approximately 70 percent of freshwater extracted worldwide, and the expansion of irrigated agriculture in arid regions poses serious sustainability problems.

**Temperate Climates (C):** They are the world's most important food-producing region. Western Europe produces wheat, barley, corn, sugar beets, and wine grapes. The southeastern United States produces corn, soybeans, cotton, and tobacco. Central Chile produces export fruits (grapes, apples, cherries). Southern China produces rice in two or three annual harvests. The Mediterranean climate (Csa/Csb) is ideal for vines, olive trees, citrus fruits, and stone fruits; Spain, Italy, Greece, and France are the main producers of olive oil, wine, and Mediterranean fruits.

**Continental Climates (D):** Production is concentrated in the short growing season. Russia, Ukraine, and Kazakhstan are major producers of wheat, barley, and sunflower. Canada produces wheat and canola in the prairies of Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba. The central United States (Iowa, Illinois, Indiana) is the most productive corn and soybean region in the world. Continental climates with harsh winters limit crops to a single annual harvest, but the long summer daylight hours partially compensate for this limitation.

**Polar Climates (E):** Agriculture is virtually nonexistent. In tundra regions (ET), only some tubers and vegetables are grown during the short summer in Norway, Iceland, and Alaska. In Greenland and Antarctica, there is no commercial agricultural production.

### Climate Change and Agriculture

Climate change is altering global agricultural production patterns. The IPCC reports that by mid-century, global agricultural production could decrease by 2 to 6 percent per decade due to rising temperatures, changes in precipitation patterns, and more frequent extreme weather events. However, the effects are not uniform: while some high-latitude regions could benefit from longer growing seasons, tropical and subtropical regions face greater risks of droughts, floods, and productivity loss.

The main vulnerabilities include decreased wheat yields in tropical regions, reduced irrigation water availability due to glacier retreat in the Andes and Himalayas, salinization of coastal soils from sea level rise, and expansion of pests and diseases to higher latitudes. Agricultural adaptation includes developing drought- and heat-resistant crop varieties, adopting conservation agriculture techniques, crop diversification, and improving efficient irrigation systems.

## Chapter 59: Climate and Biodiversity

### Distribution of Life on the Planet

Biodiversity, understood as the variety of life in all its forms, is closely linked to climate. Temperature and precipitation are the two climatic factors that most influence species distribution, as they determine the availability of energy, water, and nutrients for organisms. Ecologist Robert Whittaker demonstrated in 1975 that plant species diversity increases with mean annual temperature and precipitation, reaching its maximum in tropical rainforests.

Humid tropical rainforests (Af), which cover less than 7 percent of the Earth's surface, harbor more than 50 percent of the world's plant and animal species. The Amazon Basin alone contains more than 80,000 plant species, 1,300 bird species, 3,000 freshwater fish species, and millions of insects. Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines) is the world's second largest biodiversity hotspot, with more than 100,000 vascular plant species.

### Biodiversity Hotspots and Their Climates

The concept of biodiversity hotspot, developed by Norman Myers in 1988 and refined by Conservation International, identifies 36 regions of the world that concentrate at least 50 percent of endemic vascular plant species on less than 2 percent of the Earth's surface, and that have lost at least 70 percent of their original habitat. Of the 36 hotspots, 21 are found in humid tropical regions.

Among the most important hotspots are the Brazilian Atlantic Forest (Af/Cfa climate), which harbors 20,000 endemic plant species; the Andean Forests (Cfb/H climate), with 15,000 endemic species; the Indian Ocean Islands (Af/Am climate), with 10,000 endemic species; and the Madrean Pine-Oak Forests of Mexico and Central America (Am/Aw climate), with 7,000 endemic species. Each of these hotspots faces significant threats from deforestation, extensive agriculture, and climate change.

### Climatic Zones and Species Adaptations

**Deserts (B):** Desert organisms have developed extraordinary adaptations to survive with scarce water. Cacti store water in their stems, mesquite roots can reach 50 meters deep to find groundwater, and animals like the desert kangaroo rat obtain all their water from the metabolism of the seeds they consume. Species diversity in deserts is low compared to other biomes, but the organisms present show unique morphological, physiological, and behavioral adaptations.

**Temperate Forests (C):** Temperate deciduous forests, such as those in Central Europe and eastern the United States, present moderate biodiversity but with a complex structure of layers. Deciduous trees (oaks, beeches, maples, elms) lose their leaves in autumn to reduce water loss and survive winter. The soils of these forests are extremely rich in organic matter due to the slow decomposition of leaf litter, supporting a great diversity of fungi, invertebrates, and microorganisms.

**Tundra (ET):** Despite extreme conditions, the tundra harbors surprisingly high biodiversity in terms of lichens, mosses, and herbaceous plants. Tundra plants grow very close to the ground to avoid wind, have small, thick leaves to reduce water loss, and can photosynthesize at temperatures near zero. Tundra animals, such as reindeer, muskoxen, and Arctic foxes, have developed thick fur coats and thermoregulation mechanisms to survive temperatures of -40 degrees Celsius.

**Oceans (Cfb):** The rainy temperate forests of southern Chile, New Zealand, and Tasmania are ecosystems of extraordinary biodiversity, with giant trees such as the alerce (Fitzroya cupressoides), which can live more than 3,600 years. These forests depend on the abundant precipitation and moderate temperatures provided by the oceanic climate, and are vulnerable to changes in precipitation patterns associated with climate change.

### Impact of Climate Change on Biodiversity

Climate change is shifting species distributions toward higher latitudes and altitudes. A study published in Science in 2008 estimated that terrestrial species are shifting an average of 16.9 meters toward the poles per decade, and 11 meters upward in mountains per decade. This shift is faster in polar regions and slower in the tropics.

Coral reefs, which harbor 25 percent of marine species, are particularly vulnerable. Coral bleaching, caused by rising sea water temperatures, has destroyed extensive reef areas in Australia (Great Barrier Reef), the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean. The IPCC projects that with a 2-degree Celsius increase, 99 percent of tropical coral reefs could disappear by 2050.

## Chapter 60: Urban Climates

### The Urban Heat Island Effect

Cities create their own microclimates due to the urban heat island (UHI) effect. This phenomenon, first described by Luke Howard in 1833, occurs because urban surfaces (asphalt, concrete, brick) absorb and retain more heat than natural surfaces (vegetation, bare soil, water). The temperature difference between a city center and its rural surroundings can reach 5-10 degrees Celsius at night, and 1-3 degrees during the day.

The heat island effect is intensified by several factors: urban geometry (narrow streets and tall buildings trap solar radiation and reduce ventilation), anthropogenic activity (heating, air conditioning, traffic, industry), reduced evapotranspiration (less vegetation and impervious surfaces), and the composition of building materials (low reflectivity and high thermal capacity).

### Urban Microclimates in Different Cities

**Madrid:** The Spanish capital experiences a significant heat island effect, with nighttime temperatures in the center that can be 4-6 degrees higher than in the suburbs. The historic center, with its narrow streets and limestone buildings, traps heat during the day and releases it slowly at night. Urban parks like El Retiro function as cold islands, reducing local temperatures by 2-3 degrees. The layout of Madrid, with its wide boulevards and fountains, helps partially mitigate the UHI effect.

**Tokyo:** The Japanese megacity is one of the most extreme examples of urban heat island, with differences of up to 8-10 degrees Celsius between the center and the suburbs. Steel and glass office towers absorb enormous amounts of heat, and the metro system releases residual heat to the surface. Tokyo has implemented measures such as green roofs, water fountains in plazas, and the Cool Biz program, which promotes wearing light clothing in summer to reduce air conditioning use.

**Mexico City:** Located at 2,240 meters in altitude, the Mexican capital combines the heat island effect with thermal inversion caused by air pollution. Suspended particles trap infrared radiation, intensifying warming. Extensive parks (Chapultepec Forest, Desierto de los Leones National Park) function as thermal regulators, but rapid urbanization is reducing their effectiveness.

**Sydney:** The Australian city faces the dual challenge of the heat island effect and extreme heat waves. During summer, temperatures in the center can exceed 40 degrees Celsius for several consecutive days. The City of Sydney program has installed green roofs, public fountains, and shade trees to reduce urban temperatures.

### Mitigation Strategies

Cities around the world are adopting strategies to combat the heat island effect. These include:

- **Green roofs and vertical gardens:** Berlin has more than 100 hectares of green roofs; Paris requires all new buildings to have green roofs or solar panels; Milan has built Bosco Verticale, a residential building with 900 trees on its facades.

- **High-reflectivity surfaces:** Light-colored roofs and pavements reflect more solar radiation and reduce heat absorption. Los Angeles has painted more than 100 downtown streets white to reduce surface temperatures by up to 5 degrees Celsius.

- **Urban trees:** Trees provide shade and evapotranspiration, reducing local temperatures. Singapore, known as the "Garden City," covers 30 percent of its surface with vegetation. Melbourne (Australia) aims to double its tree cover by 2040.

- **Urban irrigation systems:** Fountains, ponds, and sprinkler systems reduce temperatures through evaporation. Dubai has implemented urban mist systems in its public spaces.

- **Urban ventilation:** Some cities, such as Stuttgart (Germany), have designed their urban planning to facilitate the circulation of fresh air from surrounding hills toward the city center, using green corridors as ventilation channels.

## Chapter 61: Climate Change by Region

### Africa

Africa is the continent most vulnerable to climate change due to its high dependence on rainfed agriculture, limited infrastructure, and reduced adaptation capacity. The Sahel, the semiarid strip extending between the Sahara and the savanna, has experienced a temperature increase of 0.5-1.0 degrees Celsius per decade since 1980, with more frequent and intense droughts. Lake Chad, which in 1960 covered 25,000 km², has shrunk to less than 1,500 km² due to the combination of drought and overexploitation.

In the Horn of Africa, droughts have intensified, causing recurrent food crises in Somalia, Ethiopia, and Kenya. The IPCC projects that by 2050, agricultural production in Africa could decrease by 20 to 30 percent, and that 180 million additional people could face food insecurity. Sea level rise threatens coastal cities such as Lagos, Dar es Salaam, and Alexandria, with projections of 0.5-1.0 meters of rise by 2100.

### Latin America

Latin America faces multiple impacts of climate change. Andean glaciers are retreating at an accelerated rate: the Chacaltaya glacier in Bolivia disappeared completely in 2009, and Argentine glaciers have lost 30 percent of their mass since 1970. This threatens water supply for millions of people in cities like La Paz, Quito, Bogotá, and Mendoza.

The Amazon rainforest, which functions as a global carbon sink, faces a critical tipping point. Researchers from the University of São Paulo estimate that if deforestation exceeds 20-25 percent of the original surface (currently at 17 percent), the rainforest could irreversibly degrade toward a savanna state, releasing billions of tons of carbon into the atmosphere. Climate change exacerbates this process by increasing drought frequency and temperature.

In Central America and the Caribbean, hurricanes are intensifying. Hurricane Irma (2017) reached winds of 295 km/h, and Hurricane Maria caused the worst catastrophe in Puerto Rico's history. The IPCC projects that by the end of the century, hurricanes could intensify by 5 to 10 percent, with stronger winds and more abundant precipitation.

### Asia

Asia faces some of the worst impacts of climate change due to its enormous population and vulnerability to natural disasters. Sea level rise threatens more than 600 million people living in low-lying coastal areas, particularly in Bangladesh (where 17 million people could be displaced by 2050), Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines.

The Himalayan glaciers, known as the "third pole of the world," feed the Ganges, Indus, Brahmaputra, Mekong, Yangtze, and Yellow rivers, which supply water to more than 1.5 billion people. A study by ICIMOD (International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development) estimates that by 2100, Himalayan glaciers could lose between 36 and 64 percent of their mass, with catastrophic consequences for water supply.

South and Southeast Asia are particularly vulnerable to intensified monsoons. The 2022 floods in Pakistan affected 33 million people and destroyed 1.7 million homes, causing estimated losses of 30 billion dollars. The IPCC partially attributes this catastrophe to climate change, which has intensified monsoon rains.

### Europe

Europe is warming at a rate above the global average: the continent's mean temperature has risen 1.5 degrees Celsius since the pre-industrial era, compared to 1.1 degrees of global average. Warming is faster in the European Arctic (Scandinavia, Russia), where temperatures have risen 2-3 degrees since 1970.

Increasingly hot, dry summers cause devastating wildfires. The 2021 fires in Greece burned 125,000 hectares, and those in Portugal and Spain destroyed extensive forest areas. The IPCC projects that by 2050, the area burned annually in Europe could triple.

Heat waves are becoming more frequent and intense. The 2003 European heat wave caused more than 70,000 deaths, and the 2019 heat wave reached 48.9 degrees Celsius in France, a historical record. Sea level rise threatens coastal cities like Venice (which already experiences frequent flooding), Amsterdam, Barcelona, and Genoa.

### North America

North America experiences accelerated warming, particularly in the Canadian Arctic and Alaska, where temperatures have risen twice as fast as the global average. Permafrost, which covers 25 percent of northern hemisphere soil, is thawing, releasing methane (a greenhouse gas 84 times more potent than CO₂ in the short term) and causing infrastructure collapse.

Atlantic hurricanes are intensifying. Hurricane Harvey (2017) dropped 1.5 meters of rain on Houston in four days, causing unprecedented flooding. Hurricane Katrina (2005) destroyed New Orleans' infrastructure and caused 1,800 deaths. Wildfires in California, Oregon, and British Columbia have intensified dramatically: the 2020 "megafire" in California burned more than 4 million hectares.

Droughts in the western United States are the most severe in 1,200 years, according to a Columbia University study. Lake Mead and Lake Powell, the largest US reservoirs, are at their lowest levels since their construction in the 1930s, threatening water supply for 40 million people.

### Oceania

Australia and the Pacific Islands face dramatic impacts of climate change. The Great Barrier Reef has suffered five mass bleaching events since 2016, with the loss of more than 50 percent of its coral. The IPCC estimates that with a 1.5-degree Celsius increase, 70-90 percent of coral reefs would disappear, and with 2 degrees, 99 percent.

The Pacific Islands are particularly vulnerable to sea level rise. Tuvalu, Kiribati, and the Marshall Islands, with maximum elevations of just 2-3 meters, could be flooded by the end of the century. The government of Tuvalu has already acquired land in Fiji as a possible relocation destination for its population.

Australia faces extreme droughts, heat waves, and devastating wildfires. The 2019-2020 "Black Summer" burned 18.6 million hectares, destroyed 3,000 homes, and caused the deaths of 34 people and approximately 3 billion animals. The combination of record temperatures, prolonged drought, and intense winds created conditions for fires of unprecedented magnitude.

## Chapter 62: The Mediterranean Climate — An In-Depth Analysis

### Characteristics of the Mediterranean Climate

The Mediterranean climate is one of the most unique and threatened in the world. It is characterized by hot, dry summers, with temperatures exceeding 30 degrees Celsius for several months, and mild, rainy winters, with temperatures between 5 and 15 degrees. Annual precipitation ranges from 300 to 900 millimeters, concentrated almost exclusively in the autumn and winter months (October to March).

This particular distribution of precipitation is due to the seasonal migration of the subtropical pressure belt (the Hadley cell). In summer, the high-pressure belt shifts northward, blocking the arrival of Atlantic frontal systems and creating drought and atmospheric stability conditions. In winter, the belt shifts southward, allowing the entry of cyclonic fronts that bring rain.

### The Mediterranean Basin

The Mediterranean Basin is the most extensive region with Mediterranean climate in the world, with a surface area of approximately 2.5 million km². It includes southern Spain, southern France, Italy, Greece, Turkey, the Levant countries (Lebanon, Israel, Palestine), North Africa (Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria, Libya), and the islands of Crete, Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, and the Balearics.

Spain is the European country with the largest area of Mediterranean climate, covering approximately 60 percent of its territory. Spain's Mediterranean regions include Catalonia, the Valencian Community, Murcia, eastern Andalusia, Extremadura, and part of Castilla-La Mancha. The mean annual temperature ranges from 14 to 19 degrees, and precipitation from 300 to 800 millimeters, with maxima in autumn (September-November), when cold drops from the Mediterranean cause torrential rains.

### Mediterranean Biodiversity

The Mediterranean region is one of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots, with more than 25,000 endemic plant species (50 percent of native species). Natural vegetation is dominated by sclerophyllous forests, evergreen formations adapted to summer drought. Main tree species include the holm oak (Quercus ilex), the cork oak (Quercus suber), the Aleppo pine (Pinus halepensis), the stone pine (Pinus pinea), and the olive tree (Olea europaea).

Mediterranean shrublands, known as maquis in France, garriga in Spain, and macchia in Italy, are formations of aromatic shrubs such as lavender, rosemary, thyme, rockrose, and esparto grass. These ecosystems are highly adapted to fire; many species, such as the stone pine, need fire to reproduce (their pine cone only opens with heat).

### Threats to the Mediterranean Climate

The Mediterranean climate is among the most threatened by climate change. The IPCC classifies the Mediterranean region as a "hotspot" of climate change, with warming projected 20 percent above the global average. By 2100, temperatures could rise between 2 and 4 degrees Celsius, precipitation could decrease by 10 to 30 percent, and the frequency and intensity of droughts, wildfires, and heat waves will increase significantly.

Wildfires are one of the greatest threats. In 2023, Greece experienced the largest wildfire in the history of the European Union, with more than 96,000 hectares burned on the island of Evia. Spain, Portugal, Italy, and Turkey also suffer devastating fires every summer. The combination of drought, extreme temperatures, and winds creates conditions favorable for rapid-spreading, difficult-to-control fires.

## Chapter 63: The Monsoon Climate and Rice Cultivation

### Monsoon Mechanism

The monsoon is a seasonal climatic phenomenon characterized by the periodic reversal of winds, which brings torrential rains during the warm season and drought during the cold season. The Asian monsoon, the world's largest, affects southern Asia, Southeast Asia, and parts of China, and is caused by the differential heating between the Asian continent and the Indian Ocean.

During the boreal summer (June-September), the Asian continent heats up much faster than the ocean, creating a low-pressure zone over India and Pakistan. Humid winds from the Indian Ocean flow toward the continent, bringing abundant rainfall. During winter (October-May), the situation reverses: the continent cools faster than the ocean, and winds blow from the continent toward the sea, creating dry conditions.

### The South Asian Monsoon

The South Asian monsoon is responsible for 70-80 percent of the region's annual precipitation. It arrives in southern Kerala (India) around June 1 and advances northward, reaching Delhi by mid-July and northwestern India by mid-August. The withdrawal begins in September and is complete by December.

Monsoon precipitation varies enormously. Mawsynram, in the state of Meghalaya (India), with 11,871 millimeters annually, is the wettest place in the world, due to its position on the windward slope of the Khasi Hills. In contrast, western Rajasthan, in the orographic rain shadow of the monsoon, receives less than 100 millimeters annually.

The monsoon is vital for South Asian agriculture, which depends on rains for the cultivation of rice, wheat, cotton, and sugarcane. However, monsoon variability is extreme: a weak monsoon can cause droughts and famines, while a strong monsoon can cause devastating floods. The 2022 floods in Pakistan, which affected 33 million people, are an example of the consequences of an extreme monsoon.

### Rice: The Monsoon Crop

Rice (Oryza sativa) is the world's most important crop, feeding more than 3.5 billion people. It is grown primarily in regions with tropical and humid subtropical climates, particularly in Southeast Asia, southern China, southern India, Bangladesh, Indonesia, and the Philippines.

Rice needs temperatures above 20 degrees Celsius and abundant water during its growth. Traditional paddy rice cultivation requires fields to be submerged under 5-10 centimeters of water for most of the growing cycle. This system, although water-intensive, has advantages: it controls weeds, provides nutrients from sediment, and protects against pests.

Rice yields vary enormously according to climatic conditions and agricultural practices. In regions with favorable climate and advanced technology (Japan, South Korea, China), yields can exceed 7 tons per hectare. In regions with less favorable climate and limited technology (sub-Saharan Africa), yields can be less than 2 tons per hectare.

### Climate Change and the Monsoon

Climate change is altering Asian monsoon patterns. Climate models project a 10 to 30 percent increase in monsoon precipitation by the end of the century, with greater variability (more frequent droughts and more intense floods). Rising temperatures could accelerate the hydrological cycle, increasing evaporation and extreme precipitation.

These changes have enormous implications for agriculture and food security. A 10 percent increase in monsoon precipitation could benefit rice crops in Bangladesh and Vietnam, but could cause devastating floods in Pakistan and northern India. Increased variability would make agricultural planning and water management more difficult.

## Chapter 64: Climate and Water Resources

### The Hydrological Cycle and Climate

Water is the resource most affected by climate. The hydrological cycle —evaporation of water from the oceans, its atmospheric transport, precipitation over land, and its return to rivers and aquifers— is controlled by temperature, precipitation, and winds, all of which are climatic factors. Freshwater availability in a region depends on the balance between precipitation (input) and evapotranspiration plus runoff (output).

Glaciers are the world's most important freshwater reservoirs. It is estimated that glaciers contain approximately 69,000 km³ of water, equivalent to about 70 years of the world's river runoff. Mountain glaciers, such as those in the Andes, Himalayas, Alps, and Rocky Mountains, feed rivers that supply water to billions of people. Glacier retreat due to climate change threatens water supply for agriculture, industry, and human consumption.

### Water Resources by Climate Type

**Humid Tropical Climates (Af/Am):** These regions have abundant water, but face quality and distribution problems. The Amazon Basin contains 20 percent of the world's surface freshwater, but deforestation and pollution threaten its quality. The Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta, in Bangladesh and India, harbors the world's largest aquifer (the Ganges Aquifer System), but overexploitation and industrial pollution are reducing its quality and quantity.

**Dry Climates (B):** Water scarcity is the main problem. The Middle East and North Africa, with only 1 percent of global water resources, harbor 5 percent of the world's population. Israel has developed desalination technologies that provide 70 percent of its drinking water. Saudi Arabia depends almost exclusively on fossil groundwater (non-renewable), which is being rapidly depleted.

**Temperate Climates (C):** Western Europe has moderate water availability, but distribution is unequal. Southern Spain, with Mediterranean climate, faces chronic water stress; the Ebro River has lost 40 percent of its flow since 1950 due to overexploitation for irrigation. The southeastern United States has abundant water, but hurricanes and floods cause contamination of supply sources.

**Continental Climates (D):** Water resources depend heavily on snow and ice melt. Siberian rivers (Ob, Yenisei, Lena) have enormous flows but are frozen for 6-8 months of the year. The Mississippi River, which drains the center of the United States, has experienced increasingly extreme droughts and floods due to climate change.

### Water Management and Climate Change

Climate change is fundamentally altering global water resources. The IPCC reports that by 2050, between 1,000 and 2,000 million people could face water stress. The main threats include:

- Glacier retreat, which reduces the flow of rivers fed by ice melt (Andes, Himalayas, Alps).
- Decreased precipitation in already dry regions (Mediterranean, Sahel, Australia).
- Increased extreme precipitation, which causes flooding and aquifer contamination.
- Sea level rise, which contaminates coastal aquifers with saltwater.
- Increased temperature, which increases evaporation and reduces surface water availability.

Adaptation strategies include desalination (mainly in the Middle East), wastewater reuse (Israel recycles 85 percent of its wastewater for agriculture), reservoir construction (China has built more than 98,000 reservoirs since 1949), demand management (reducing consumption, tiered tariffs), and watershed protection (reforestation, wetland conservation).

## Chapter 65: Microclimates

### Definition and Scale

A microclimate is the climate of a very small area, which can differ significantly from the surrounding regional climate. Microclimates occur at the scale of meters or even centimeters, and are caused by factors such as topography, orientation, vegetation, built surfaces, the presence of water bodies, and land use. Understanding microclimates is fundamental for agriculture, architecture, urban planning, and nature conservation.

### Microclimates in Agriculture

Farmers have intentionally used microclimates for millennia. In the Nile Valley, microclimate variations allow different crops to be grown on slopes with different orientations. South-facing slopes (northern hemisphere) receive more sun and are warmer, suitable for vineyards and olive groves; north-facing slopes are cooler and more humid, suitable for pasture crops and forests.

In modern agriculture, greenhouses are constructed microclimates that allow year-round cultivation. The greenhouses of Almería (Spain) cover 31,000 hectares and produce vegetables for all of Europe, thanks to their ability to control temperature, humidity, and light. The greenhouses of the Netherlands, pioneers in controlled cultivation technology, produce 120 tons of tomatoes per hectare per year, compared to 60 tons in open cultivation.

### Urban Microclimates

Cities are mosaics of microclimates. An urban park can be 3-5 degrees cooler than the adjacent street; a paved square can be 5-8 degrees warmer than a nearby garden; a narrow alley can be 10 degrees cooler than a wide avenue at noon. These variations are caused by urban geometry, building materials, vegetation, and human activity.

Barcelona has studied its urban microclimates in detail. The Parque del Centro del Poblenou, with 5.5 hectares, reduces local temperatures by 2-3 degrees thanks to its vegetation and water fountains. The narrow streets of the Gothic Quarter provide shade for most of the day, maintaining cooler temperatures than wide avenues like the Diagonal.

### Natural Microclimates

Natural ecosystems present complex microclimates. In a tropical rainforest, the temperature at the forest floor can be 5-10 degrees lower than in the tree canopy, due to shade and evapotranspiration. On a mountain slope, the temperature difference between the base and the summit can be 20-30 degrees, creating a vertical zonation of vegetation (from tropical rainforest to nival).

Wetlands are humid microclimates that moderate extreme temperatures. The Doñana wetland (Spain) reduces summer temperatures in its immediate surroundings by 2-4 degrees thanks to water evaporation and marsh vegetation. Rivers and lakes also create microclimates: river valleys are usually warmer in winter and cooler in summer than surrounding areas, due to the thermal capacity of water.

## Chapter 66: Climate and Human Health

### Direct Impacts of Climate on Health

Climate directly affects human health through temperature, humidity, solar radiation, and air quality. Heat waves are the deadliest direct climatic risk in developed countries. The 2003 European heat wave caused more than 70,000 deaths, mainly among the elderly and chronically ill. In Spain, the heat wave caused 150 deaths per day during the hottest weeks.

Low temperatures are also dangerous. In Europe, cold waves cause more deaths than heat waves, due to cardiovascular and respiratory diseases aggravated by cold. The United Kingdom records 25,000 additional deaths during cold winters.

Excessive solar radiation causes sunburn, premature skin aging, and skin cancer. Australia has one of the world's highest rates, with 2,000 annual deaths from melanoma, due to the combination of high UV radiation, the population's fair skin, and outdoor culture.

### Indirect Impacts: Vector-Borne Diseases

Climate change is altering the geographic distribution of vector-borne diseases (mosquitoes, ticks, flies). Dengue, transmitted by the Aedes aegypti mosquito, has increased 30-fold in the last 50 years and now affects 390 million people annually in tropical and subtropical regions. Climate change is expanding the mosquito's habitat toward higher latitudes: dengue cases have been recorded in France, Italy, and Croatia, regions where the vector mosquito previously did not exist.

Malaria, transmitted by the Anopheles mosquito, kills more than 600,000 people per year, mainly in sub-Saharan Africa. Climate change could displace malaria to higher altitudes in East Africa, affecting populations that currently have no immunity. The Zika virus, also transmitted by Aedes aegypti, caused an epidemic in Brazil in 2015-2016, with 1.5 million cases and devastating consequences for newborns (microcephaly).

### Respiratory Diseases and Air Quality

Climate change worsens air quality through several mechanisms: it increases tropospheric ozone (smog) formation in cities, increases pollen and allergen concentrations (due to longer pollination seasons), and extends the wildfire season, which emits harmful suspended particles.

Air pollution causes 7 million premature deaths per year, according to the World Health Organization. The 2015 wildfires in Indonesia emitted 1.6 billion tons of CO₂ and caused 100,000 premature deaths in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. The 2019-2020 wildfires in Australia emitted 830 million tons of CO₂ and caused an estimated 445 deaths.

### Adaptation Strategies and Health Protection

Early warning systems for heat and cold waves are essential. France implemented the National Plan for Prevention of Heat Wave Effects after the 2003 catastrophe, with a color-coded system that activates protection measures when extreme temperatures are forecast. The program has significantly reduced heat deaths in France.

Vector protection includes the use of bed nets, repellents, biological control (fish that eat mosquito larvae) and, in some cases, the release of genetically modified mosquitoes to reduce vector populations. Singapore uses light traps and air conditioning to control Aedes aegypti in urban areas.

## Chapter 67: Climate and Energy

### Energy Resources and Climate

Climate determines the availability of renewable energy sources and affects energy demand for heating and cooling. Solar energy depends on the solar radiation received, which varies with latitude, cloudiness, and season. The Sahara and Atacama deserts are the places with the greatest solar potential in the world, with more than 3,000 hours of sunshine annually. Germany, despite having only 1,500 hours of sunshine annually, is Europe's largest solar energy producer thanks to its subsidy policies.

Wind energy depends on winds, which are influenced by climatic patterns. The best wind farm locations are in regions with constant winds: the Atlantic coast of Europe (Ireland, United Kingdom, Spain), the Great Plains of North America, the coasts of China, and Patagonia. Spain has an installed wind capacity of 28 GW, sufficient to cover 20 percent of its electricity demand.

Hydroelectric energy depends on river flow, which is controlled by precipitation and snow and ice melt. Brazil gets 65 percent of its electricity from hydroelectric plants, mainly in the Amazon Basin. Norway gets 98 percent of its electricity from hydroelectricity. Climate change threatens this source: the 2001 drought in Brazil caused an energy crisis that forced scheduled blackouts.

### Energy Demand and Climate

Energy demand is directly related to climate. In regions with cold winters, heating represents 40-60 percent of household energy consumption. In regions with hot summers, cooling represents 20-40 percent. Climate change is reducing heating demand in high-latitude regions (Scandinavia, Canada) but increasing cooling demand in mid- and low-latitude regions.

The most comprehensive study on the impact of climate change on energy demand, published in Nature Climate Change in 2019, estimates that by 2100, global energy demand could increase by 11 to 58 percent due to increased air conditioning use. The largest increase is expected in India, Indonesia, and Brazil, where the combination of higher temperatures and greater access to air conditioning will enormously increase demand.

### Energy and Climate Change Mitigation

The energy transition to renewable sources is one of the key strategies for mitigating climate change. The Paris Agreement (2015) commits countries to limiting global warming to 1.5-2 degrees Celsius, which requires reducing CO₂ emissions by 45 percent by 2030 and achieving net zero emissions by 2050.

Technological advances have drastically reduced the cost of renewable energy. The cost of photovoltaic solar energy has decreased by 89 percent since 2010, and wind energy by 70 percent. In 2023, renewable energy represented 30 percent of global electricity generation, surpassing natural gas for the first time.

However, the transition faces challenges: the intermittency of solar and wind energy requires storage systems (batteries, hydrogen, pumped hydro), grid infrastructure needs modernization, and fossil fuels continue to be subsidized in many countries. Climate change also affects energy infrastructure itself: nuclear power plants need river water for cooling, and droughts have forced production cuts in France and Germany.

## Chapter 68: The Climates of Spain and Portugal

### Iberian Climatic Diversity

The Iberian Peninsula presents extraordinary climatic diversity for its relatively small size (583,000 km²). This diversity is due to its geographic position (between 36 and 44 degrees of latitude), its rugged relief (mountain ranges, plateaus, valleys), the influence of the Atlantic Ocean, the Mediterranean Sea, and the Cantabrian Sea, and its proximity to the African continent.

Spain is climatically divided into several well-differentiated regions:

**Atlantic Spain (Cfb):** The northern strip, from Galicia to the Basque Country, has an oceanic climate with abundant precipitation (1,000-2,000 mm annually), mild temperatures (8-18 degrees), and cool summers. This region, known as Green Spain, is characterized by meadows, oak and beech forests, and agriculture based on corn, potatoes, and cattle farming.

**Mediterranean Spain (Csa/Csb):** The eastern and southern strip, from Catalonia to Andalusia, has a Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers and mild, rainy winters. Precipitation ranges from 300 to 800 mm, concentrated in autumn. This region produces olive oil, wine, citrus fruits, vegetables, and fruits.

**Continental Spain (Csb/Csa):** The central plateau, at 600-1,000 meters in altitude, has a continental Mediterranean climate with cold winters (0-5 degrees) and hot summers (25-30 degrees). Precipitation is scarce (300-500 mm) and concentrated in spring and autumn. Madrid, with 2,500 hours of sunshine annually and precipitation of only 436 mm, is one of the driest European capitals.

**Semidesert Spain (BSk/BWh):** The southeast (Almería, Murcia, Alicante) and the Ebro Valley have a semiarid or desert climate, with precipitation below 300 mm. The Tabernas Desert, in Almería, is Europe's only continental desert, with less than 200 mm of annual rainfall.

### Portugal

Portugal presents a transition between the oceanic climate of the north and the Mediterranean climate of the south. The north (Porto, Minho) has an oceanic climate (Cfb) with abundant precipitation (1,200-2,000 mm). The center (Lisbon) has a Mediterranean climate (Csa) with moderate precipitation (700-800 mm). The south (Algarve) has a drier, warmer climate, with precipitation of 500-600 mm and extremely hot summers.

### Impacts of Climate Change on the Iberian Peninsula

Spain is one of the hotspots of climate change in Europe. The mean temperature has risen 1.5 degrees Celsius since 1960, a rate higher than the European average. By 2050, an additional increase of 1.5-2.5 degrees is expected, with precipitation reductions of 5-15 percent in the south and center.

The most significant impacts include:

- **More frequent and intense droughts:** Climate change is reducing precipitation in the Spanish Mediterranean. The Aixó reservoir (Valencia) has been empty on several occasions in recent years, and the Segura River is permanently in a state of water emergency.

- **Wildfires:** Spain suffers between 10,000 and 15,000 fires annually, which burn between 50,000 and 200,000 hectares. The 2021 megafires in Castellón and Valencia destroyed more than 10,000 hectares in a few hours.

- **Biodiversity loss:** Mediterranean ecosystems are threatened by rising temperatures, droughts, and fires. The Aleppo pine, cork oak, and holm oak are retreating in southern areas.

- **Sea level rise:** Spain's coastline, with 8,000 km of coast, is threatened by sea level rise. Barcelona, Valencia, Málaga, and the Balearic Islands are particularly vulnerable. A 0.5-meter rise would flood 5 percent of the Spanish coast.

## Chapter 69: Climate and Coastal Cities

### Threats to Coastal Cities

More than 600 million people live in low-lying coastal areas (less than 10 meters above sea level), and this figure could double by 2050. Coastal cities face multiple climate-related threats: sea level rise, coastal flooding, erosion, aquifer salinization, tropical storms, and wave erosion.

Sea level rise is the most serious long-term threat. The IPCC projects a rise of 0.3-0.6 meters by 2100 under a moderate emissions scenario, and 0.6-1.1 meters under a high emissions scenario. If the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets partially collapse, the rise could reach 2 meters or more.

### Coastal Cities in Danger

**Venice (Italy):** The city of canals is sinking 1-2 millimeters per year due to groundwater extraction, while sea level rises 3 millimeters annually. The MOSE project, a mobile flood barrier, has cost 5.5 billion euros and has been partially operational since 2020.

**Miami (United States):** The city faces sea level rise, recurrent coastal flooding, and salinization of the Biscayne Aquifer. Models project that by 2060, much of Miami Beach could be below sea level during high tides.

**Bangladesh:** The world's most vulnerable country to sea level rise. A 1-meter rise would flood 17 percent of its territory, displacing 20 million people. The Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta, which covers two-thirds of the country, is extremely vulnerable.

**Shanghai (China):** With 24 million inhabitants, Shanghai is located on a flat delta just 4 meters above sea level. Models project that by 2050, coastal flooding could affect 17 million people.

**Lagos (Nigeria):** Africa's fastest-growing city, with 24 million inhabitants, is built on low-lying coastal land. Rising sea levels, combined with land subsidence from groundwater extraction, threaten millions of people.

### Adaptation Strategies

Coastal cities are developing adaptation strategies:

- **Flood defense:** The Netherlands have built the world's most ambitious coastal engineering work, the Delta Works, which protects 26 percent of the country that is below sea level. The Maeslant barrier in Rotterdam can close automatically when flooding is forecast.

- **Strategic retreat:** Some communities are adopting "managed retreat" policies from vulnerable coastal areas. Louisiana (United States) has resettled barrier island communities and acquired coastal properties to create buffer zones.

- **Green infrastructure:** Mangroves, coastal wetlands, and coral reefs function as natural barriers against flooding and erosion. New York invested 20 million dollars in coastal wetland restoration after Hurricane Sandy.

- **Resilient design:** Cities are incorporating climate resilience into their urban planning. Copenhagen has built parks that can be temporarily flooded during storms, functioning as urban drainage systems.

## Chapter 70: Climate and Oceans

### Oceans as Climate Regulators

Oceans cover 71 percent of the Earth's surface and contain 97 percent of the planet's water. They are the main regulator of global climate, as they absorb, store, and transport enormous amounts of energy. The ocean has absorbed more than 90 percent of the additional heat caused by global warming since the pre-industrial era, which has partially slowed the rise in atmospheric temperature, but at the cost of thermal expansion of water and sea level rise.

### Ocean Currents and Climate

Ocean currents are the planet's circulatory system, transporting warm water from the tropics toward the poles and cold water from the poles toward the tropics. The Gulf Stream, for example, transports 30 million cubic meters of warm water per second from the Caribbean toward Western Europe, keeping Europe's temperatures 5-10 degrees above what would correspond to its latitude. Without the Gulf Stream, London would have a climate similar to Labrador (Canada), at the same latitude.

Thermohaline circulation, driven by temperature and salinity differences, is the engine of deep ocean currents. Cold, salty water from the Arctic sinks in the North Atlantic and flows southward in the depths, while warm surface water flows northward. This system could weaken if global warming reduces sea ice formation in the Arctic, as sea ice releases freshwater that reduces water salinity and density, preventing it from sinking.

### Ocean Warming

Oceans are warming at an unprecedented rate. The mean ocean surface temperature has risen 0.88 degrees Celsius since 1900, and warming is accelerating. 2023 was the year with the highest ocean temperature ever recorded, with temperature anomalies exceeding 1 degree Celsius in large areas of the Atlantic and Pacific.

Ocean warming has multiple consequences: coral bleaching (75 percent of tropical reefs are threatened), thermal expansion of water (responsible for half of sea level rise), reduced dissolved oxygen (creating "dead zones" where marine organisms cannot live), and species displacement toward higher latitudes.

### The El Niño Phenomenon and the Southern Oscillation

El Niño is a natural climatic phenomenon that alters temperature and precipitation patterns worldwide. It occurs when the trade winds weaken and warm water from the western Pacific shifts eastward, warming the coasts of Peru and Ecuador and cooling the coasts of Australia and Indonesia.

Global effects of El Niño include droughts in Indonesia, Australia, and southern Africa; floods in Peru, Ecuador, and California; more intense storms in the Atlantic (though less intense in the Pacific); and disruptions in Asian monsoon patterns. The 2015-2016 El Niño was one of the strongest in the historical record, with ocean temperatures up to 3 degrees above average in the equatorial Pacific.

The Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the planet's most important climate variability pattern, with 2-7 year cycles that affect all continents. Climate models project that climate change could intensify extreme El Niño and La Niña events, with devastating consequences for agriculture, fishing, and water management.

## Chapter 71: Climate and Architecture

### Architecture as Response to Climate

Throughout history, architecture has been a direct response to climatic conditions. Traditional buildings in every region of the world reflect centuries of adaptation to temperature, precipitation, wind, and solar radiation. Traditional architecture uses local materials, climate-adapted construction techniques, and designs that maximize thermal comfort with minimal energy.

**Hot, Dry Climates:** Traditional desert houses (North Africa, Middle East, southern Spain) have thick adobe or stone walls that absorb heat during the day and release it at night, leveling thermal oscillations. Interior courtyards create cool microclimates through shade and evaporation of water from fountains and vegetation. Openings are small to reduce heat and dust entry.

**Hot, Humid Climates:** Traditional tropical houses (Southeast Asia, West Africa, Central America) are elevated on stilts to allow under-floor ventilation, have high ceilings and wide eaves to protect from sun and rain, and lightweight bamboo or wood walls that allow air circulation.

**Cold Climates:** Traditional houses in cold climates (Scandinavia, Siberia, Alaska) have thick wood or stone walls, small windows and double glazing to reduce heat loss, and sloped roofs to shed snow.

### Contemporary Bioclimatic Architecture

Modern bioclimatic architecture integrates principles of traditional architecture with contemporary technology to minimize energy consumption and maximize passive thermal comfort. Bioclimatic buildings use:

- **Solar orientation:** Buildings are oriented to maximize solar gain in winter (south-facing windows in the northern hemisphere) and minimize it in summer (solar shading on west-facing windows).

- **Thermal inertia:** High thermal mass materials (concrete, stone, brick) that store and release heat slowly, leveling temperature fluctuations.

- **Natural ventilation:** Design that takes advantage of prevailing winds and temperature differences to create air currents that renew indoor air without energy consumption.

- **Thermal insulation:** High thermal resistance materials that reduce heat transmission through walls, roofs, and floors.

- **Active facades:** Ventilation, shading, and cooling systems that automatically adjust to exterior climatic conditions.

### Iconic Examples

**Passivhaus Building (Darmstadt, Germany):** Built in 1991, it is the first certified Passivhaus building. It consumes 90 percent less energy than a conventional building, thanks to extreme insulation, heat recovery from air, and optimal solar orientation.

**Bosco Verticale Building (Milan, Italy):** Two residential towers with 900 trees, 5,000 shrubs, and 11,000 herbaceous plants on their facades. The trees provide shade in summer, allow sun entry in winter, filter air pollution, and reduce the heat island effect.

**National Museum of Qatar (Doha):** Designed by Jean Nouvel, the building uses overlapping concrete discs that create shade and ventilation, evoking the desert rose (a natural formation that protects seeds from extreme heat).

**Bosco Verticale (Medellín, Colombia):** The city has built "library parks" in marginalized neighborhoods, buildings that combine cultural functions with abundant vegetation that reduces local temperatures in a tropical mountain climate.

## Chapter 72: The Future of the World's Climates

### Projections for 2100

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has developed emissions scenarios (Shared Socioeconomic Pathways, SSP) that project different climatic futures depending on the decisions humanity makes in the coming decades. The five scenarios range from SSP1-1.5 (sustainable development, zero emissions by 2050) to SSP5-8.5 (fossil fuel-based growth, tripled emissions by 2100).

**Optimistic Scenario (SSP1-2.6):** Limits warming to 1.5-2.0 degrees Celsius. Precipitation increases in the tropics and decreases in the subtropics. Glaciers decrease by 30-50 percent. Sea level rises 0.3-0.6 meters. Extreme events increase moderately.

**Intermediate Scenario (SSP2-4.5):** Warming of 2.1-3.5 degrees. Dry climates expand significantly. Wildfires double or triple. Sea level rises 0.4-0.8 meters. Droughts and floods intensify in all regions.

**Pessimistic Scenario (SSP5-8.5):** Warming of 3.3-5.7 degrees. Desert climates expand drastically. Glaciers almost completely disappear. Sea level rises 0.6-1.1 meters (or more if ice sheets collapse). Extreme weather events are much more frequent and intense.

### Adaptation and Mitigation

Adaptation to climate change involves adjusting human systems to reduce vulnerability to climatic impacts. Examples include developing drought-resistant crops, building resilient infrastructure, creating early warning systems, protecting natural ecosystems, and sustainable urban planning.

Mitigation involves reducing greenhouse gas emissions to limit warming. The main strategies include transitioning to renewable energy, improving energy efficiency, protecting and restoring forests, adopting sustainable agricultural practices, and developing carbon capture and storage technologies.

### The Need for Urgent Action

IPCC scientists are unequivocal: to limit warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius, global CO₂ emissions must be reduced by 45 percent by 2030 (relative to 2010) and reach net zero by 2050. This requires unprecedented transformations in all sectors of the economy: energy, transportation, industry, agriculture, forestry, and waste management.

Time is running out. Each year of delay in reducing emissions makes goals more difficult and costly to achieve. The impacts of climate change are already being felt by millions of people around the world, and will worsen if urgent measures are not taken. The future of the world's climates, and of human civilization, depends on the decisions we make in the coming decades.

## Chapter 73: Glossary of Climatic Terms

**Albedo:** Proportion of solar radiation reflected by a surface. Snow has a high albedo (0.8-0.9), while asphalt has a low albedo (0.05-0.10).

**Anticyclone:** Zone of high atmospheric pressure, associated with clear skies and stable weather. In Spain, the Azores anticyclone dominates in summer, causing heat and drought.

**Hadley Cell:** Global atmospheric circulation that transports heat from the tropics to mid-latitudes. Its seasonal displacement determines the distribution of arid and humid zones.

**Greenhouse Effect:** Process by which certain atmospheric gases (CO₂, methane, water vapor) absorb infrared radiation emitted by the Earth's surface, warming the atmosphere. It is a natural phenomenon that keeps the planet's mean temperature at 15 degrees Celsius; its intensification by human activities causes global warming.

**El Niño:** Periodic warming of equatorial Pacific waters, with global climatic effects. It manifests every 2-7 years and can last 9-12 months.

**Evapotranspiration:** Combined process of water evaporation from soil and plant transpiration. It is a key component of the hydrological cycle and regulates local temperature.

**Front:** Zone of contact between two air masses of different temperature and humidity. Fronts cause most weather phenomena (rain, snow, storms).

**Vertical Thermal Gradient:** Decrease in temperature with altitude, approximately 6.5 degrees Celsius per 1,000 meters in the troposphere (moist adiabatic lapse rate).

**Urban Heat Island:** Phenomenon by which cities are warmer than surrounding rural areas, due to heat absorption by built surfaces and anthropogenic heat generation.

**Monsoon:** Seasonal reversal of winds, associated with abrupt changes in precipitation patterns. The Asian monsoon is the world's most important.

**Southern Oscillation (ENSO):** Natural climate variability pattern that alters oceanic and atmospheric temperatures in the equatorial Pacific, with global effects.

**Permafrost:** Permanently frozen soil found in polar and high mountain regions. It contains enormous quantities of organic carbon that could be released as CO₂ and methane if it thaws.

**Orographic Precipitation:** Rain or snow caused by the uplift and cooling of humid air when encountering a mountain. It is responsible for abundant rainfall on the windward slope of mountains.

**Solar Radiation:** Electromagnetic energy emitted by the Sun. The amount of radiation received depends on latitude, altitude, cloudiness, and season.

**Mean Annual Temperature:** Average of daily mean temperatures throughout a year. It is the most widely used climatic indicator for classifying climates.

**Tundra:** Biome characterized by permanently frozen soils (permafrost), low vegetation (mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs), and absence of trees. Found in polar and high mountain regions.

**Föhn (or Katabatic) Wind:** Warm, dry wind that descends the leeward slope of a mountain, warming through adiabatic compression. It is responsible for abnormally high temperatures in some Alpine valleys.
