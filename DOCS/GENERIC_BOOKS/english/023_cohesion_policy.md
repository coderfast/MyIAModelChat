# The European Cohesion Policy

## General Introduction

The Cohesion Policy of the European Union is one of the most ambitious and fundamental policies of the European project, representing a massive investment in the economic and social development of the least developed European regions. With a budget that has historically represented approximately one-third of the total EU budget, cohesion policy has been the main financial instrument of European solidarity, transferring resources from the richest regions to the least developed in order to reduce territorial disparities and promote balanced and sustainable development of the entire European territory. Over more than three decades of existence, cohesion policy has financed billions of euros in investments in transport and energy infrastructure, innovation and technology, employment and vocational training, environmental protection, and urban and rural development.

Cohesion policy emerged from the awareness that European economic integration, while generating significant benefits for the European economy as a whole, could also create or widen disparities between the most prosperous regions and the least developed ones. The creation of the single market, trade liberalization, and economic competition could disproportionately benefit already prosperous regions, while the least developed regions risked falling behind. To prevent this divergence and ensure that all European citizens could benefit from integration, European treaties established cohesion policy as an instrument of financial solidarity that would transfer resources from the richest regions to the least developed with the aim of reducing disparities and promoting balanced development.

Cohesion policy has undergone notable evolution since its creation. The first structural funds were established in 1975 as the European Regional Development Fund and the European Agricultural Guidance and Guarantee Fund, Guidance Section, and have been progressively strengthened and expanded over the decades. Cohesion policy has traditionally focused on the least developed regions, but has also included measures for transition regions, regions with a moderate delay in development, and regions with specific problems such as border areas, rural areas, and urban areas. Cohesion policy has financed investments that have profoundly transformed the landscape and economy of many European regions, improving infrastructure, creating employment, driving innovation, and protecting the environment.

In this book we will analyze European cohesion policy in depth, examining its objectives and fundamental principles, the structural funds and their functioning, the cohesion fund and its specific role, the thematic objectives and investment priorities, project evaluation and policy management, the results achieved over the decades, the territorial dimension of cohesion, and the future challenges facing cohesion policy. Understanding cohesion policy is essential to understand how the EU promotes solidarity among its regions and citizens, and how it contributes to balanced and sustainable development of the entire European territory.

## Chapter 1: Objectives and Principles

### The Fundamental Objective of Economic and Social Cohesion

The fundamental objective of cohesion policy is to reduce economic and social disparities between the regions of the European Union and to promote balanced and sustainable development of the entire European territory. This objective is established in the European treaties, which state that the EU must reduce differences in development levels between regions and contribute to the harmonious development of the entire territory. Cohesion policy is the main financial instrument for achieving this objective, transferring resources from the most prosperous regions to the least developed through the European structural and investment funds.

Economic and social cohesion is a fundamental principle of the European project, which recognizes that economic integration cannot generate lasting benefits if it is not accompanied by a reduction of disparities between regions and citizens. Cohesion seeks to ensure that all European citizens, regardless of where they live, can benefit from the opportunities offered by European integration, and that all European regions can develop their economic and social potential. Cohesion policy contributes to this objective through investments in infrastructure, innovation, employment, environment, and social development that improve the living conditions of citizens in the least developed regions and strengthen their capacity to compete with the most prosperous regions.

### The Guiding Principles of Cohesion Policy

Cohesion policy is based on several fundamental principles that guide its design, implementation, and evaluation. The principle of territorial concentration establishes that cohesion policy resources must be concentrated in the regions that need them most, avoiding their dispersion across too many areas or regions. The principle of programming establishes that resources must be planned through multiannual programs that define investment priorities, result indicators, and monitoring mechanisms. The principle of co-financing establishes that EU-financed investments must be complemented by national, regional, or local public or private financing, which ensures the commitment of local and national authorities to the funded projects. The principle of efficiency establishes that resources must be used in an efficient and effective manner, maximizing the impact of investments on the economic and social development of regions.

The principle of partnership establishes that regional and local authorities must participate actively in the design and implementation of cohesion programs, which ensures that investments respond to the needs and priorities of the beneficiary regions. The principle of equal opportunities and non-discrimination establishes that cohesion policy must promote equality between men and women, social inclusion, and non-discrimination in all its interventions. The principle of sustainability establishes that investments financed by cohesion policy must be environmentally, socially, and economically sustainable, contributing to the green and digital transition of regions. The principle of good governance establishes that the management of funds must be transparent, efficient, and accountable, ensuring the proper use of public resources.

### The Territorial Dimension of Cohesion

The territorial dimension of cohesion recognizes that development disparities exist not only between countries but also within them, between urban and rural regions, between coastal and interior regions, and between central and peripheral regions. Cohesion policy addresses these territorial disparities by adapting its interventions to the specific needs of each type of region. The outermost regions, located far from the European continent, receive specific aid to compensate for their additional transport and communication costs. Border areas receive aid for cross-border cooperation that enables regions from different countries to work together on common projects. Rural areas receive aid for economic diversification, infrastructure improvement, and environmental protection. Urban areas receive aid for urban regeneration, sustainable mobility improvement, and social cohesion.

The territorial dimension of cohesion is also manifested in the integrated approach to investments, which seeks to articulate different European policies in a specific territory to maximize their impact. Integrated territorial development is an approach that promotes the coordination of interventions from different European policies, such as cohesion policy, the common agricultural policy, fisheries policy, sustainable development policy, and research and innovation policy, in a specific territory, to avoid duplication, complement interventions, and maximize impact on territorial development. This integrated approach is particularly relevant for rural areas, where cohesion policy interventions and common agricultural policy interventions complement each other to promote sustainable development.

## Chapter 2: The Structural Funds

### The European Regional Development Fund

The European Regional Development Fund is the main financial instrument of cohesion policy, with an allocation of approximately 243 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period. The ERDF finances investments in transport and energy infrastructure, research, development and innovation, support for small and medium-sized enterprises, energy transition and environmental protection, sustainable urban development, and territorial cooperation between regions. The ERDF operates in all EU regions, with a greater concentration in the least developed regions, which receive the largest share of funding, and with a co-financing rate that varies according to the development level of each region. The least developed regions receive European co-financing of up to 85 percent, while the most prosperous regions receive a maximum co-financing of 50 percent.

The ERDF finances a wide range of interventions in European regions, including the construction and improvement of transport infrastructure such as motorways, secondary roads, high-speed railway lines, airports and seaports; the construction and improvement of energy infrastructure such as electrical grids, renewable energy infrastructure and gas distribution networks; investment in research, development and innovation, including the construction of research centers, laboratories and science and technology parks; support for small and medium-sized enterprises through investment aid, access to financing and internationalization; environmental protection, including the construction of waste treatment plants, remediation of contaminated land and biodiversity protection; and sustainable urban development, including the regeneration of disadvantaged neighborhoods, improvement of sustainable urban transport and creation of quality public spaces.

### The European Social Fund

The European Social Fund is the EU's financial instrument for investing in people, with an allocation of approximately 98.5 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period. The ESF finances projects that promote employment, education, vocational training, social inclusion, equal opportunities and accessibility in all Member States. The ESF operates in all EU regions, but with a greater concentration in the least developed regions. The ESF finances a wide range of interventions, including vocational training programs for the unemployed and workers, hiring subsidies for young people and people with difficulties accessing employment, social inclusion projects for people at risk of exclusion, education and training programs to improve the skills of the workforce, gender equality projects in the workplace, and accessibility aids for people with disabilities.

The ESF+ is the new designation of the European Social Fund for the 2021-2027 period, integrating the European Social Fund, the European Fund for the Most Disadvantaged and the Employment and Social Innovation Programme. The ESF+ has an integrated approach that combines interventions in employment, education, social inclusion and the fight against poverty. The ESF+ finances projects that promote social cohesion and equal opportunities, contributing to the objective of building a more social and inclusive Europe. The priorities of the ESF+ include the promotion of employment and labor mobility, improvement of education and training, social inclusion and the fight against poverty, and improvement of institutional effectiveness and public governance.

### The Cohesion Fund

The Cohesion Fund is a specific financial instrument for Member States whose gross domestic product per capita is below 90 percent of the EU average, with the aim of reducing economic and social disparities between Member States. The Cohesion Fund finances investments in trans-European transport infrastructure, trans-European energy infrastructure, and sustainable development, including environmental protection and the energy transition. For the 2021-2027 period, the Cohesion Fund has an allocation of approximately 42.6 billion euros, which is allocated to Member States with an income level below 90 percent of the EU average, such as Bulgaria, Czechia, Estonia, Greece, Croatia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia and Slovenia.

The Cohesion Fund complements the European structural and investment funds, but with a more specific focus on infrastructure investments and the green transition of the least developed Member States. The Cohesion Fund has financed significant investments in transport infrastructure, such as the construction of motorways, high-speed railway lines and improvements to ports and airports, which have improved the connectivity of the least developed Member States with the rest of Europe. The Cohesion Fund has also financed investments in energy infrastructure, such as the construction of electrical grids, modernization of distribution networks and investment in renewable energy, which have improved energy efficiency and sustainability in these Member States.

## Chapter 3: Thematic Objectives

### Investment Priorities of Cohesion Policy

Cohesion policy for the 2021-2027 period is structured into eleven thematic objectives that define the investment priorities of the European structural and investment funds. These thematic objectives are more streamlined and better defined than in previous budgetary periods, which allows for greater concentration of resources on the most important priorities for the economic and social development of regions. The thematic objectives of cohesion policy 2021-2027 include smarter research and innovation, the green and digital transition, a more connected Europe, a more inclusive and cohesive Europe, and a Europe closer to citizens.

The first thematic objective, a smarter Europe, promotes innovation, digital transition, economic transformation and support for small and medium-sized enterprises. This objective finances research and innovation, technology transfer, the creation of centers of excellence, support for innovative companies, digitalization of public administrations and enterprises, and improvement of access to digital technologies. This objective is particularly relevant for regions seeking to diversify their economies and improve their competitiveness through innovation and technology.

The second thematic objective, a greener Europe, a low-carbon Europe, promotes the green transition, the fight against climate change and environmental protection. This objective finances investment in renewable energy, improvement of energy efficiency, protection and restoration of biodiversity, sustainable management of water resources, waste management and the transition towards a circular economy. This objective is especially relevant for regions facing significant environmental challenges, such as water pollution, soil degradation or biodiversity loss.

The third thematic objective, a more connected Europe, promotes improvement of trans-European transport and energy infrastructure, digital connectivity and sustainable mobility. This objective finances the construction and improvement of transport infrastructure such as motorways, high-speed railway lines, ports and airports; the construction and modernization of energy infrastructure such as electrical grids and gas distribution infrastructure; improvement of digital connectivity through the expansion of broadband and 5G networks; and the promotion of sustainable mobility through investment in public transport, cycle paths and pedestrian infrastructure.

The fourth thematic objective, a more inclusive Europe, promotes social inclusion, equal opportunities, quality employment, education, training and public health. This objective finances the promotion of employment, the fight against poverty, inclusion of minorities, improvement of education and training, accessibility for people with disabilities, and improvement of access to health services. This objective is especially relevant for regions facing significant social challenges, such as high unemployment, poverty or social exclusion.

The fifth thematic objective, a Europe closer to citizens, promotes sustainable urban development, social and territorial cohesion, culture, heritage, social inclusion, and the reduction of geographical barriers. This objective finances urban regeneration, improvement of quality of life in cities, protection of cultural heritage, development of sustainable tourism, the fight against rural depopulation, and improvement of connectivity in remote and island areas.

## Chapter 4: Project Evaluation

### The Fund Management System

The management of European structural and investment funds is carried out through a complex system of institutions and procedures that includes the European Commission, managing authorities, certified contact points, independent auditors and project beneficiaries. The European Commission is responsible for the general supervision of cohesion policy, the approval of operational programs, verification of compliance with rules, and authorization of payments. Managing authorities are designated by Member States or regions and are responsible for the daily management of programs, including project selection, monitoring of implementation, verification of expenditure eligibility and preparation of progress reports.

The fund management system is based on a combination of real-time controls and ex-post controls. Real-time controls include verification of expenditure eligibility before payment, checking compliance with public procurement rules, and verification of proper project implementation. Ex-post controls include audits conducted by independent auditors designated by Member States, audits carried out by the European Commission, and controls performed by the European Anti-Fraud Office. The management system also includes an error correction mechanism that allows identification and correction of program implementation errors and recovery of payments made incorrectly.

### Project Selection Criteria

The selection of projects financed by the structural funds is carried out on the basis of predefined criteria that include coherence with the program objectives, expected impact on the economic and social development of the region, project sustainability, technical and financial viability, and contribution to cross-cutting objectives such as gender equality, social inclusion and environmental protection. The selection criteria are established by the managing authorities in the operational programs, in cooperation with the European Commission and with the participation of stakeholders. Project selection is carried through calls for proposals published by the managing authorities that establish the requirements, evaluation criteria and application deadlines.

The evaluation of submitted projects is carried out by independent evaluation panels that apply the selection criteria established in the operational program. The evaluation panels are composed of qualified experts in the relevant fields for the projects, including technical, financial, environmental and social experts. The evaluation panels issue evaluation reports containing project scores according to the selection criteria, funding or rejection recommendations, and specific conditions that funded projects must meet. The managing authorities take the final funding decision on the basis of the evaluation reports and budgetary availability.

### Monitoring and Evaluation

Monitoring and evaluation are essential components of the life cycle of cohesion programs, allowing measurement of implementation progress, evaluation of investment impact and extraction of lessons to improve policy effectiveness. Monitoring is carried out continuously throughout program implementation, and provides information on financial implementation, the performance of result indicators, and compliance with established deadlines and conditions. Evaluation is carried out at different moments of the program life cycle, including initial evaluations to verify the relevance of program objectives, intermediate evaluations to assess progress and make adjustments if necessary, and ex-post evaluations to measure the impact and effectiveness of investments made.

Result indicators are fundamental tools for monitoring and evaluating cohesion policy. Result indicators measure the specific changes that investments financed by cohesion policy generate in regions, including job creation, infrastructure improvement, reduction of greenhouse gas emissions, increased investment in research and innovation, and improvement of citizens' quality of life. The European Commission and Member States use these indicators to evaluate the effectiveness of cohesion policy and to identify areas for improvement. The annual cohesion reports regularly publish data on the implementation and results of cohesion policy, providing citizens and stakeholders with transparent and accessible information about the impact of European investments in their regions.

## Chapter 5: Results Achieved

### Impact on Infrastructure

The structural funds have decisively contributed to the improvement of transport, energy and telecommunications infrastructure in the least developed EU regions, profoundly transforming the physical landscape and connectivity of vast areas of the European territory. Investments in transport infrastructure include the construction and improvement of motorways and main roads that have significantly reduced travel times and transport costs between European regions; the construction of high-speed railway lines that have connected major European cities with fast and reliable services; and the improvement of seaports and airports that have facilitated international trade and tourism. Investments in energy infrastructure include the construction and modernization of electrical grids that have improved the reliability and efficiency of electricity supply; investment in gas distribution infrastructure that have diversified energy sources and reduced dependence on a single supplier; and investment in renewable energy infrastructure such as wind farms and solar plants that have reduced greenhouse gas emissions and contributed to the energy transition.

The results of infrastructure investments financed by the structural funds have been impressive. The trans-European transport network has experienced significant improvements in its connectivity, with the construction of thousands of kilometers of motorways, high-speed railway lines and maritime connections between Member States. Investments in energy infrastructure have improved energy security, reduced energy costs for households and businesses, and facilitated the transition to renewable energy sources. Investments in telecommunications infrastructure have extended high-speed broadband to areas that previously had no access to quality internet services, reducing the digital divide between urban and rural regions.

### Impact on Employment and the Economy

The structural funds have generated a significant impact on employment and economic activity in European regions. Investments financed by the structural funds have contributed to the creation of millions of direct and indirect jobs in the beneficiary regions, both through infrastructure investment that generates employment in the construction phase and through investment in innovation, training and support for enterprises that generates sustainable long-term employment. Investments in small and medium-sized enterprises, which are the economic engine of most European regions, have facilitated the creation of new businesses, the expansion of existing ones and the improvement of their competitiveness. Investments in vocational training have improved the skills of the workforce, facilitating their entry into the labor market and their geographical mobility.

Investments in innovation and technology have contributed to improving the competitiveness of European regions, attracting foreign investment, fostering the creation of technology-based companies and facilitating knowledge transfer between universities, research centers and companies. Investments in tourism have contributed to diversifying the economies of regions that were excessively dependent on a single sector, creating new employment and income opportunities. Investments in urban regeneration have improved quality of life in cities, attracting new population and new economic activity to neighborhoods that were previously in decline. The multiplier effects of investments financed by the structural funds have generated significant economic impact in the beneficiary regions, contributing to the growth of regional gross domestic product and the improvement of socioeconomic indicators.

### Impact on the Environment

The structural funds have significantly contributed to environmental protection and restoration in European regions, financing investments that have reduced greenhouse gas emissions, improved air and water quality, protected biodiversity and promoted the transition towards a more sustainable economy. Investments in renewable energy, including wind farms, solar plants and biomass facilities, have contributed to reducing dependence on fossil fuels and decreasing greenhouse gas emissions. Investments in building energy efficiency, including thermal rehabilitation, installation of efficient heating systems and use of sustainable construction materials, have reduced energy consumption and associated emissions. Investments in sustainable transport, including improvement of public transport, construction of cycle paths and promotion of electric vehicles, have contributed to reducing air pollution in cities.

Investments in water and waste management have improved water quality in European rivers, lakes and coasts, reduced nitrate and pesticide pollution from agricultural sources, and promoted sustainable waste management through reduction, recycling and recovery of municipal and industrial waste. Investments in biodiversity protection and restoration have contributed to the conservation of natural habitats, the protection of endangered species and the creation of protected areas that are important both for biodiversity and for the development of sustainable tourism. The structural funds have financed projects for the remediation of land contaminated by past industrial activities, returning abandoned land to productive use that had represented a risk to public health and the environment.

## Chapter 6: Territorial Cohesion

### The Territorial Dimension of Cohesion Policy

Territorial cohesion is a fundamental objective of cohesion policy that seeks to ensure balanced and sustainable development of all European regions, regardless of their geographical location, development level or natural characteristics. Territorial cohesion recognizes that territorial disparities are a significant challenge that can generate social exclusion, depopulation of peripheral areas, excessive concentration of economic activity in large cities, and degradation of the environment and landscape. Cohesion policy addresses these challenges through investment in all European regions, with a greater concentration in the regions that need it most, and through the promotion of cooperation between regions for the development of common projects that transcend administrative boundaries.

The territorial dimension of cohesion policy is manifested in the adaptation of interventions to the specific needs of each type of region. The least developed regions, which have a gross domestic product per capita below 75 percent of the EU average, receive the largest share of structural fund financing, with the aim of accelerating their economic convergence with the rest of the EU. The transition regions, which have a gross domestic product per capita between 75 and 90 percent of the EU average, receive an intermediate level of financing, with the aim of completing their economic convergence. The more developed regions, which have a gross domestic product per capita above 90 percent of the EU average, receive financing to promote innovation, digital transition and social cohesion, with the aim of maintaining their competitiveness and contributing to the development of the least developed regions.

### European Territorial Cooperation

European territorial cooperation is a specific instrument of cohesion policy that finances cooperation projects between regions of different Member States, with the aim of promoting territorial integration, solving common problems and enhancing the benefits of cross-border cooperation. European territorial cooperation finances cross-border cooperation programs between regions of neighboring countries, interregional cooperation programs between regions across Europe, and transnational cooperation programs between regions that share common challenges such as mountain areas, coastal areas or outermost regions.

Cross-border cooperation programs are the most numerous and cover border areas between neighboring Member States, where cooperation between regions on both sides of the border is especially important for solving common problems such as labor mobility, environmental protection, public service delivery and tourism development. Interregional cooperation programs finance projects that enable regions across Europe to share experiences and good practices in areas such as innovation, social cohesion, environment and public governance. Transnational cooperation programs finance large-scale projects in areas such as research, energy, transport and environment, which require the cooperation of multiple regions from different countries.

## Chapter 7: Future Challenges

### The Financial Sustainability of Cohesion Policy

The financial sustainability of cohesion policy is a significant challenge that will intensify in the coming years due to increasing pressure on the European budget. The cohesion policy budget represents a significant part of the total EU budget, and its maintenance at adequate levels requires continuous political commitment from Member States. The financial challenges include the need to fund new priorities such as the digital and green transition, defense and public health, without significantly reducing financing for cohesion policy. Efficiency in the use of resources is fundamental to ensure that every euro invested generates the maximum possible impact on the economic and social development of regions.

The absorption capacity of structural funds is a recurring challenge in some Member States, where slow program implementation, administrative complexity and lack of technical capacities can limit the effective use of available resources. Simplification of administrative procedures, strengthening of management capacities, and use of digital technologies for fund management are important measures to improve absorption capacity and the effectiveness of cohesion policy. Coordination with other sources of financing, such as the Recovery and Resilience Facility, Next Generation EU, and private financing, is essential to maximize the impact of investments on the development of European regions.

### Regional Convergence Challenges

Despite the significant progress achieved by cohesion policy, regional disparities remain a significant challenge in the European Union. The differences in development levels between the richest and least developed regions continue to be considerable, and in some cases disparities have widened rather than narrowed. The factors contributing to the persistence of regional disparities include differences in institutional and governance capacities, differences in human capital and innovation, differences in connectivity and infrastructure, and the effects of globalization and the digital revolution that can disproportionately benefit the more dynamic regions. Cohesion policy must evolve to address these structural challenges more effectively, adapting its interventions to the specific needs of each region and promoting endogenous development and innovation as drivers of regional growth.

Demographic challenges, including population aging, low birth rates and internal migration towards large cities, are especially acute in the least developed regions and in rural areas of Europe. These demographic challenges can create a vicious cycle of depopulation, reduction of economic activity, deterioration of public services and loss of viability of rural communities. Cohesion policy must contribute to breaking this vicious cycle through investments that improve living conditions in the least developed regions, creating employment, improving public services, promoting innovation and facilitating labor and residential mobility. Territorial cohesion is fundamental to ensure that all European regions can offer their citizens dignified living conditions and opportunities for personal and professional development.

## General Conclusion

European cohesion policy has been one of the most ambitious, most successful and most transformative policies of the European Union. Over more than three decades, cohesion policy has financed investments that have profoundly transformed the landscape and economy of European regions, improving infrastructure, creating employment, driving innovation and protecting the environment. Cohesion policy has significantly contributed to reducing regional disparities, promoting solidarity among European citizens, and strengthening the economic, social and territorial cohesion of the European Union.

The achievements of cohesion policy are undeniable. Thousands of kilometers of motorways and high-speed railway lines connect European regions, reducing geographical barriers and facilitating the mobility of people and goods. Millions of jobs have been created in the least developed regions, improving living standards and reducing poverty. Investment in research and innovation has strengthened the competitiveness of European regions and attracted foreign investment. Investment in environmental protection has contributed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions, improving water and air quality, and protecting European biodiversity. Territorial cooperation has promoted integration between regions of different countries and facilitated the resolution of common problems.

However, cohesion policy faces significant challenges that require innovative and bold solutions. The persistence of regional disparities, demographic challenges, pressure on the European budget, the need to adapt to the digital and green transition, and improvement of the effectiveness and efficiency of fund management are challenges that will define cohesion policy in the coming decades. Cohesion policy must evolve to respond to these challenges, maintaining its commitment to solidarity and cohesion, and adapting to the new economic, social and environmental realities facing European regions.

Cohesion policy remains, and will continue to be, a fundamental instrument of European solidarity that contributes to the balanced and sustainable development of the entire European territory. Understanding cohesion policy is essential to understand how the EU promotes equal opportunities among its citizens and regions, and how it contributes to building a fairer, more prosperous and more cohesive Europe. The future of cohesion policy will depend on the capacity of the European Union, the Member States and the regions to work together to achieve balanced territorial development that benefits all European citizens, regardless of where they live.

## Chapter 8: Cohesion Policy and Urban Development

### The European Urban Development Objective

Sustainable urban development has become an increasingly important priority of cohesion policy, recognizing that cities are engines of economic growth, centers of innovation and creativity, and places where the majority of European citizens live. The European urban development objective is to promote the sustainable development of urban areas through investment in urban regeneration, sustainable mobility, energy efficiency, social inclusion, innovation and public governance. Cohesion policy finances urban development through multiple instruments, including the European structural and investment funds, which dedicate a significant part of their resources to urban areas; the Urban Development Initiative, which promotes integrated urban development planning; and the European Commission Urban Strategies, which facilitate the exchange of experiences and good practices among European cities.

The Urban Development Initiative is a specific instrument of cohesion policy that promotes integrated urban development through the designation of urban authorities responsible for the development and implementation of integrated urban development strategies. Urban authorities are designated by Member States and receive funding from the structural funds to finance investments included in their urban development strategies. The Urban Development Initiative has financed urban regeneration projects in hundreds of European cities, including the rehabilitation of disadvantaged neighborhoods, the creation of quality public spaces, improvement of sustainable urban transport, investment in energy efficiency of public buildings, and promotion of social inclusion and territorial cohesion.

### Urban Regeneration

Urban regeneration is one of the investment priorities of cohesion policy in urban areas. Urban regeneration seeks to improve quality of life in cities through the rehabilitation of disadvantaged neighborhoods, the creation of quality public spaces, improvement of urban infrastructure, promotion of sustainable mobility and investment in public services. The structural funds finance urban regeneration projects that include the rehabilitation of social housing, the creation of parks and green areas, improvement of public lighting, the creation of sports and cultural infrastructure, rehabilitation of historic buildings, and improvement of accessibility for people with disabilities.

The results of urban regeneration financed by the structural funds have been significant in many European cities. The rehabilitation of disadvantaged neighborhoods has improved the living conditions of thousands of families, reducing poverty and social exclusion. The creation of quality public spaces has improved social cohesion and urban quality of life. Improvement of transport infrastructure has reduced congestion and pollution, and facilitated the mobility of citizens. Investment in energy efficiency has reduced energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions, contributing to the green transition of cities. Urban regeneration has also had a positive impact on economic activity, attracting investment, creating employment and revitalizing urban centers.

### Sustainable Urban Mobility

Sustainable urban mobility is an increasingly important priority of cohesion policy, recognizing that urban transport is responsible for a significant part of greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution in cities. The structural funds finance investments in sustainable urban mobility that include the improvement and expansion of public transport, the construction of infrastructure for cyclists and pedestrians, the installation of public bike-sharing systems, the creation of low-emission zones, investment in electric and alternative vehicles, and the planning of integrated urban mobility. These investments seek to reduce dependence on private cars, promote more sustainable forms of transport and improve air quality in cities.

Cohesion policy has financed sustainable urban mobility projects in numerous European cities, including the construction of metro lines, trams and high-capacity buses, the creation of cycle lanes and pedestrian zones, the installation of charging points for electric vehicles, the implementation of intelligent traffic management systems, and the creation of low-emission zones. These investments have contributed to reducing traffic congestion, improving air quality, reducing greenhouse gas emissions and improving the quality of life of citizens. Sustainable urban mobility is an essential component of the green transition of European cities, and cohesion policy remains the main financial instrument for promoting this transition.

## Chapter 9: Cohesion Policy and Innovation

### Innovation as a Driver of Regional Development

Innovation has become a fundamental priority of cohesion policy, recognizing that the capacity to innovate is a decisive factor in the competitiveness and economic development of regions. The structural funds finance innovation through multiple instruments, including investment in research and research infrastructure, support for the creation of centers of excellence and science and technology parks, support for innovative companies, promotion of cooperation between universities, research centers and companies, and training of qualified human capital in innovation. Cohesion policy seeks to reduce innovation disparities between European regions, promoting the creation of innovation ecosystems in the least developed regions that can compete with those of the most prosperous regions.

The structural funds have financed the creation of research centers, science and technology parks, and innovation networks in numerous European regions, contributing to strengthening the research and innovation capacity of these regions. These centers and parks have facilitated cooperation between universities, research centers and companies, promoting technology transfer and the creation of technology-based companies. Innovation investments financed by the structural funds have contributed to creating thousands of qualified jobs in the beneficiary regions, attracting foreign investment and improving regional competitiveness. Cohesion policy has also promoted social innovation, financing projects that seek new solutions to social problems such as social exclusion, aging, housing and education.

### Regional Digital Transition

The digital transition is a key priority of cohesion policy for the 2021-2027 period, recognizing that digitalization is a decisive factor in the economic competitiveness and social cohesion of regions. The structural funds finance the digital transition through investment in high-speed broadband infrastructure, digitalization of public administrations, support for enterprise digitalization, digital skills training of the population, and promotion of e-commerce and digital services. Cohesion policy seeks to reduce the digital divide between European regions, ensuring that all regions have access to quality digital infrastructure and that their citizens and businesses can benefit from the opportunities of the digital economy.

The digital divide between European regions is a significant challenge that cohesion policy must address effectively. Rural regions and least developed regions typically have more limited access to high-speed digital infrastructure, lower internet and digital technology penetration, and lower availability of digital skills. These digital differences can widen economic and social disparities between regions, as the digital economy generates new growth and employment opportunities that are more accessible to regions with greater digital capacities. Cohesion policy must invest in reducing the digital divide, ensuring that all European regions can fully participate in the digital economy and benefit from its opportunities.

## Chapter 10: Cohesion Policy and the Green Transition

### Environmental Investments of the Structural Funds

Environmental protection and the green transition are fundamental priorities of cohesion policy, which recognizes the responsibility of regions in the fight against climate change and in the protection of natural resources. The structural funds finance significant environmental investments, including investment in renewable energy, improvement of energy efficiency, protection and restoration of biodiversity, sustainable management of water resources, waste management and the transition towards a circular economy, remediation of contaminated land, and protection of the landscape and natural heritage. These investments contribute to reducing greenhouse gas emissions, improving environmental quality and promoting the transition towards a more sustainable and resilient economy.

Cohesion policy has financed thousands of environmental projects in European regions, including the construction of waste treatment plants, installation of renewable energy infrastructure, thermal rehabilitation of public buildings, construction of wastewater treatment systems, restoration of wetlands and coastal areas, and creation of protected areas. These investments have contributed to significantly improving environmental quality in the beneficiary regions, reducing water, air and soil pollution, protecting biodiversity and contributing to climate change mitigation and adaptation. Cohesion policy has also promoted environmental innovation, financing the research and development of new clean technologies and sustainable solutions for environmental challenges.

### The Contribution of Cohesion Policy to EU Climate Objectives

Cohesion policy makes a significant contribution to the climate objectives of the European Union, which seeks to achieve climate neutrality by 2050 and reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 55 percent by 2030. The structural funds dedicate a significant part of their resources to investments that contribute to climate change mitigation and adaptation, including investment in renewable energy, improvement of energy efficiency, promotion of sustainable transport, protection of carbon sinks such as forests and wetlands, and adaptation of infrastructure and territories to the effects of climate change such as sea level rise, floods, droughts and heat waves. Cohesion policy is a fundamental instrument for the green transition of European regions, providing the financial resources necessary to finance the investments needed to achieve the EU's climate objectives.

The contribution of cohesion policy to climate objectives is measured through climate expenditure indicators, which quantify the share of structural funds allocated to investments that contribute to the fight against climate change. For the 2021-2027 period, a climate expenditure target of 30 percent is established for the European structural and investment funds as a whole, which is equivalent to an investment of approximately 150 billion euros in climate investments over the budgetary period. This climate expenditure target is an indicator of the commitment of cohesion policy to the green transition and the fight against climate change.

## Chapter 11: Governance of Cohesion Policy

### The Programming Process

The programming process of cohesion policy is a complex and participatory process that defines the investment priorities and management mechanisms of the European structural and investment funds for a given budgetary period. The programming process begins with the definition of the EU strategic guidelines for cohesion policy, which are established by the European Parliament and the Council of the EU. On the basis of these strategic guidelines, Member States develop the Partnership Agreements, which define national investment priorities and coordination mechanisms with other European financing sources. From the Partnership Agreements, Member States and regions develop the operational programs, which detail the investment priorities, result indicators, management mechanisms and budgets allocated to each priority.

The programming process includes the ex-ante evaluation of programs, which verifies the relevance of objectives, the coherence of priorities and the effectiveness of program design. The European Commission evaluates the operational programs and approves them through funding decisions that establish the conditions for program implementation. The programming process also includes the participation of stakeholders, including regional and local authorities, business organizations, trade unions, civil society organizations and citizens, which ensures that investment priorities respond to the real needs of regions and citizens.

### Shared Governance

Cohesion policy is based on a principle of shared governance between the European Commission, the Member States and the regions, which ensures the participation of all levels of government in the design, implementation and evaluation of the policy. The European Commission is responsible for defining the general rules of the policy, approving operational programs, supervising implementation and ensuring compliance with rules. Member States are responsible for designating managing authorities, developing operational programs, supervising implementation and accountability. The regions are responsible for identifying investment needs, participating in the development of operational programs and implementing projects.

The shared governance of cohesion policy ensures that resources are used efficiently and effectively, that investments respond to the real needs of regions, and that all levels of government have a stake in the success of the policy. Shared governance also promotes responsibility and transparency in fund management, as all actors involved have accountability obligations and are subject to controls and audits. The European Commission, the Member States and the regions work together to ensure that structural funds are used in an efficient, effective and transparent manner, and that investments generate the maximum possible impact on the economic and social development of European regions.

## Chapter 12: Cohesion Policy and New Challenges

### The Response to the COVID-19 Pandemic

Cohesion policy demonstrated its strategic importance by providing a financial safety net that enabled the regions most affected by the pandemic to maintain their essential services, support their businesses and citizens, and begin their economic recovery. The mobilization of structural funds for the pandemic response included the reallocation of resources towards healthcare equipment, purchase of protective materials, support for medical research and vaccine development, and investment in digital infrastructure to facilitate remote working and distance learning. The pandemic experience reinforced awareness of the need for European financial instruments that can respond quickly to crises, and of the importance of cohesion policy as a mechanism of European solidarity in emergency situations.

The COVID-19 pandemic also exposed the structural vulnerabilities of many European regions, including dependence on vulnerable economic sectors such as tourism, hospitality and trade; the fragility of health systems in some regions; the persistence of the digital divide between urban and rural regions; and pre-existing social inequalities that widened during the crisis. Cohesion policy must contribute to the recovery of affected regions through investments that strengthen economic resilience, improve health systems, close the digital divide and reduce social inequalities. The Recovery and Resilience Facility, financed by Next Generation EU, complements cohesion policy by providing additional resources for economic recovery and the green and digital transformation of European regions, with an allocation of 723.8 billion euros that supplements the structural funds to finance the investments needed for post-pandemic reconstruction.

Cooperation between the Recovery and Resilience Facility and cohesion policy is essential to maximize investment impact and avoid duplication. The Recovery and Resilience Plans of Member States must be coherent with the investment priorities of cohesion operational programs, and coordination between both instruments must ensure efficient and complementary use of available resources. The European Commission has established coordination mechanisms to ensure that the Recovery and Resilience Facility and cohesion policy work together to support economic recovery, the green and digital transition, and the social cohesion of European regions.

### Climate Change and Territorial Adaptation

Climate change poses significant challenges for European regions, including sea level rise threatening coastal areas, more frequent and intense floods affecting river valleys, more prolonged droughts affecting Mediterranean regions, heat waves affecting public health, and biodiversity loss affecting natural ecosystems. Cohesion policy must contribute to regional adaptation to climate change through investment in resilient infrastructure, protection and restoration of natural ecosystems that provide ecosystem services, sustainable management of water resources, and territorial planning that takes climate risks into account. Cohesion policy must also promote climate change mitigation through investment in renewable energy, energy efficiency, sustainable transport and waste management.

Adaptation to climate change is a challenge that requires an integrated response from all European policies, including cohesion policy, the common agricultural policy, energy policy and environmental policy. Cohesion policy has a particularly important role to play in adaptation to climate change, as it finances investments in infrastructure and environmental protection that are fundamental to regional resilience. Cohesion policy must ensure that all financed investments are compatible with the EU's climate objectives and that they contribute both to mitigation and adaptation to climate change.

### Refugee Integration and Social Cohesion

The integration of refugees and migrants is a growing challenge for European regions that receive significant flows of displaced persons. Cohesion policy can contribute to refugee integration through investment in housing, education, vocational training, employment and social services that facilitate their integration into the society and labor market of the host regions. The structural funds can finance reception programs, language courses, vocational training, employment assistance, and social inclusion projects that help refugees become productive members of society. Refugee integration is a challenge that requires a solidarity-based and effective response from all European regions, and cohesion policy is a fundamental instrument for providing the resources needed for this response.

## Chapter 6: History and Evolution of Cohesion Policy

### The Origins of Cohesion Policy (1957-1975)

The origins of cohesion policy date back to the founding treaties of the European Communities, which established the principles of solidarity and reduction of disparities as pillars of the European integration project. The Treaty of Rome of 1957 created the European Agricultural Guidance and Guarantee Fund, Guidance Section, which was the first financial instrument intended to support rural development and modernization of agricultural structures in the least developed regions. This fund laid the groundwork for the subsequent creation of the structural funds, by establishing the principle that financial solidarity between Member States was essential to ensure that all could benefit from economic integration. The historical context of the post-war period, marked by the need to rebuild European economies and reduce disparities between the most prosperous regions of northern Europe and the least developed of the south, was decisive for the conception of these first instruments of financial solidarity.

The creation of the European Regional Development Fund in 1975 marked the official birth of cohesion policy as such. This fund was established by the Paris European Council in response to the growing awareness that European economic integration was generating regional disparities that could undermine the political and social stability of the European project. The ERDF was conceived as an instrument to finance investments in transport, energy and communications infrastructure in the least developed regions, with the aim of improving their connectivity and economic competitiveness. The creation of the ERDF also responded to the pressure from southern European countries such as Italy, Greece and Spain, which joined the Community in 1981 and 1986 respectively, and needed significant investments to close the development gap with the northern countries.

The European Social Fund was created in 1960 as the EU's financial instrument for investing in people, but its role in cohesion policy has been progressively strengthened over the decades. The ESF finances projects that promote employment, education, vocational training, social inclusion and equal opportunities, contributing to the objective of building a more social and inclusive Europe. The evolution of the ESF reflects the growing awareness that economic cohesion cannot be achieved without social cohesion, and that investment in human capital is as important as investment in physical infrastructure for the balanced development of the European territory.

### Expansion and Consolidation (1975-1988)

The period between 1975 and 1988 was one of expansion and consolidation of cohesion policy, with the creation of new financial instruments and the expansion of existing structural fund interventions. During this period, cohesion policy experienced significant growth in its financial allocation, going from being a marginal instrument to becoming one of the most generously budgeted European policies. This growth was driven by the accession of new Member States with development levels significantly below the European average, such as Greece in 1981, Spain and Portugal in 1986, which needed significant investments to modernize their economies and infrastructure.

The 1986 enlargement, with the accession of Spain and Portugal, represented a turning point in cohesion policy. These two countries had development levels significantly below the European average, and their accession required an ambitious cohesion policy that could finance the investments needed to close the development gap. The structural fund budget was significantly increased to meet the needs of the new Member States, and concentration rules were established to ensure that the majority of resources were allocated to the regions most in need. This expansion of cohesion policy was fundamental to ensure the viability of the enlargement and to demonstrate that European integration could generate tangible benefits for all Member States, including the least developed.

The Single European Act of 1986 introduced important provisions on cohesion policy, formally recognizing its role in reducing regional disparities and promoting balanced development of the European territory. The treaty established that cohesion policy must contribute to the harmonious development of the entire European territory and to the reduction of differences in development levels between regions. This explicit reference to cohesion in the treaties strengthened the legal basis of the policy and gave it an institutional recognition it previously lacked. The Single Act also established the principle of programming for cohesion policy, which required the development of multiannual development plans that defined investment priorities and result indicators, introducing greater discipline and effectiveness in the management of structural funds.

### Major Reforms (1988-2006)

The period between 1988 and 2006 was one of major reforms in cohesion policy, with a series of reforms that profoundly transformed its design, management and effectiveness. The 1988 reform, known as the Delors I reform, represented a comprehensive reform of cohesion policy that introduced fundamental innovations such as the concentration of funds in the least developed regions, multiannual programming of investments, co-financing of projects with national and regional resources, and systematic evaluation of results. This reform was driven by the President of the European Commission, Jacques Delors, who recognized the need to make cohesion policy more effective and efficient to ensure that resources were used in the best possible way to reduce regional disparities.

The 1993 reform, known as the Delors II reform, continued the path of previous reforms by introducing innovations such as the creation of the Cohesion Fund, a specific financial instrument for Member States with a gross national product per capita below 90% of the EU average. The Cohesion Fund was conceived as an instrument to finance investments in transport and environmental infrastructure in the countries most affected by development disparities, and has played an important role in modernizing the infrastructure of countries such as Spain, Portugal, Greece and Ireland. The 1993 reform also introduced improvements in the management of structural funds, including simplification of co-financing procedures, improvement of monitoring and control mechanisms, and evaluation of investment effectiveness.

The 1999 reform, known as the Berlin reform, continued the path of previous reforms by introducing innovations such as the creation of a single programming framework that simplified the management of structural funds, the introduction of common performance indicators to evaluate investment results, and improvement of coordination between different structural funds. The Berlin reform also introduced the principle of non-commitment, which established that funds not used in a given period had to be returned to the European budget, incentivizing Member States and regions to use funds efficiently and within established deadlines. This reform was fundamental to improve the effectiveness of cohesion policy and to ensure that resources were used efficiently.

The 2006 reform, known as the cohesion reform for the 2007-2013 period, represented the next major reform of cohesion policy. This reform introduced innovations such as the concentration of funds on the strategic objectives of the Lisbon Strategy for growth and employment, the introduction of the integrated territorial approach to promote coordination of interventions in a specific territory, improvement of policy effectiveness through the introduction of more detailed result indicators, and simplification of management procedures to reduce the administrative burden. The 2006 reform also introduced the partnership principle, which required the participation of regional and local authorities, economic and social agents, and civil society in the design and implementation of cohesion programs, thus ensuring that investments responded to the needs and priorities of the beneficiary regions.

### Recent Reforms (2006-2027)

The period between 2006 and 2027 has seen recent reforms that have adapted cohesion policy to the new economic, social and environmental challenges facing Europe. The reform for the 2014-2020 period, known as the cohesion reform for growth, employment and social cohesion, introduced significant innovations such as the concentration of funds on the objectives of smart specialization, energy transition and the low-carbon economy, and youth employability. This reform also introduced improvements in the governance of cohesion policy, including ex-ante evaluation of programs, continuous monitoring of implementation, and ex-post evaluation of results. The 2014 reform also strengthened macroeconomic conditionality, establishing that access to structural funds was conditional on compliance with the Council's specific recommendations on economic policy, which generated controversies about the relationship between cohesion policy and fiscal discipline.

The reform for the 2021-2027 period, known as cohesion policy for sustainable recovery and territorial cohesion, represents the most recent adaptation of cohesion policy to the new challenges facing Europe. This reform introduced significant innovations such as the concentration of funds on the objectives of green transition, digital transition, social cohesion and economic resilience, the introduction of the external cost compensation mechanism that compensates countries facing additional costs due to their geographical location, and improvement of governance through the introduction of more detailed performance indicators and more robust evaluation mechanisms. The 2021-2027 reform also introduced the rule of law conditionality, establishing that access to structural funds was conditional on Member States' respect for the rule of law, which generated controversies about the relationship between cohesion policy and democratic principles.

The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on cohesion policy, as many European regions were severely affected by the health and economic crisis. The EU adopted exceptional measures to allow greater flexibility in the use of structural funds, including the possibility of transferring resources between objectives and between programs, simplification of public procurement procedures, and acceleration of payments to provide liquidity to affected regions. The EU also created the Recovery and Resilience Facility, a temporary financial instrument to support the economic recovery of the Member States most affected by the pandemic. Cohesion policy played a fundamental role in the pandemic response, providing resources to invest in healthcare infrastructure, support affected businesses, protect employment and facilitate the economic recovery of the most affected regions.

## Chapter 7: European Structural and Investment Funds

### The Multiannual Financial Framework and Cohesion Policy

The multiannual financial framework of the European Union establishes the maximum EU budget for a seven-year period and defines the distribution of resources among different European policies. Cohesion policy is one of the most important budgetary items of the financial framework, representing approximately one-third of the total EU budget. For the 2021-2027 period, cohesion policy has an allocation of 392 billion euros, which is distributed among the European structural and investment funds: the European Regional Development Fund, the European Social Fund Plus, the Cohesion Fund, the European Agricultural Fund for Rural Development and the European Maritime, Fisheries and Aquaculture Fund. This allocation reflects the importance the EU attaches to cohesion policy as an instrument of solidarity and balanced development.

The cohesion policy budget is financed by the EU's own resources, which include customs duties, contributions based on VAT, contributions based on gross national product and, more recently, new own resources such as carbon emission rights and the single-use plastics tax. Financing cohesion policy through own resources ensures the financial autonomy of the EU and prevents it from depending on voluntary contributions from Member States. The debate on the reform of the EU's own resources and on the financial sustainability of cohesion policy remains open, as the growing demand for resources for new priorities such as the green transition, defense and research may affect the availability of funds for cohesion policy.

The distribution of cohesion policy resources among Member States and regions is carried out through a formula that takes into account the development level of each region, the population, the unemployment rate and other socioeconomic indicators. The least developed regions, with a gross national product per capita below 75% of the EU average, receive the largest share of financing, with European co-financing of up to 85%. The transition regions, with a gross national product per capita between 75% and 100% of the EU average, receive European co-financing of up to 85% for research and innovation investments and digital transition, and 60% for other investments. The more prosperous regions, with a gross national product per capita above 100% of the EU average, receive a maximum European co-financing of 50%. This distribution ensures that resources are concentrated in the regions that need them most, while maintaining a level of support for all EU regions.

### The European Regional Development Fund in Detail

The European Regional Development Fund is the main financial instrument of cohesion policy, with an allocation of approximately 243 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period. The ERDF operates in all EU regions, with a greater concentration in the least developed regions, which receive the largest share of financing. The ERDF finances investments in five main areas: research and innovation, digital transition, support for small and medium-sized enterprises, energy transition and the low-carbon economy, and sustainable mobility and key infrastructure connections. These investment priorities are aligned with the strategic objectives of the Europe 2020 Strategy for smart, sustainable and inclusive growth.

The ERDF finances a wide range of interventions in European regions. In the field of research and innovation, the ERDF finances the construction and equipment of research centers, laboratories and science and technology parks; cooperation between universities, research centers and companies; technology transfer and creation of technology-based companies; and innovation in small and medium-sized enterprises. In the field of digital transition, the ERDF finances the deployment of high-speed broadband networks in rural and urban areas; digitalization of public administrations and public services; digital training of citizens and workers; and innovation in digital technologies such as artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things and cloud computing.

In the field of support for small and medium-sized enterprises, the ERDF finances investment aid for modernization and diversification of enterprises; access to financing through guarantees and favorable loans; internationalization assistance for enterprises to facilitate access to new markets; and support for business creation and entrepreneurship. In the field of energy transition, the ERDF finances investments in renewable energy such as solar, wind, hydroelectric and biomass energy; improvement of energy efficiency in public and private buildings; modernization of electrical grids and creation of smart grids; and sustainable mobility, including public transport, bicycle infrastructure and electric vehicles. In the field of sustainable mobility, the ERDF finances the construction and improvement of transport infrastructure such as roads, railways, airports and seaports, with a focus on sustainability and territorial connectivity.

### The European Social Fund Plus in Detail

The European Social Fund Plus is the EU's financial instrument for investing in people, with an allocation of approximately 98.5 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period. The ESF+ integrates the former European Social Fund, the European Fund for the Most Disadvantaged and the Employment and Social Innovation Programme, with the aim of providing an integrated approach that combines interventions in employment, education, social inclusion and the fight against poverty. The ESF+ has a results-oriented approach that seeks to maximize the impact of investments on people's lives and on the social cohesion of regions.

The ESF+ finances a wide range of interventions in Member States. In the field of employment, the ESF+ finances vocational training programs for the unemployed and workers, hiring subsidies for young people and people with difficulties accessing employment, support for self-employment and entrepreneurship, and employment services to facilitate job search. In the field of education, the ESF+ finances education and training programs to improve the skills of the workforce, support for early childhood education, prevention of early school leaving, and vocational training for young people and adults. In the field of social inclusion, the ESF+ finances social inclusion projects for people at risk of exclusion, such as homeless people, people with disabilities, ethnic minorities and migrants; housing assistance for people in energy poverty; and childcare and dependent care services to facilitate work-life balance.

The ESF+ also finances the improvement of institutional effectiveness and public governance, through modernization of public administrations, improvement of public service quality, the fight against corruption and fraud, and the promotion of citizen participation. The ESF+ also finances gender equality in all its areas of intervention, promoting equal access to employment, equal pay, the prevention of gender-based violence and work-life balance. The ESF+ also finances the inclusion of young people in the labor market through the Youth Employment Initiative, which seeks to reduce youth unemployment through training, hiring subsidies and youth entrepreneurship.

### The Cohesion Fund in Detail

The Cohesion Fund is a specific financial instrument for Member States with a gross national product per capita below 90% of the EU average, which for the 2021-2027 period includes Bulgaria, Croatia, Estonia, Greece, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Portugal, Romania and Slovakia. The Cohesion Fund has an allocation of approximately 43 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period and finances investments in sustainable transport infrastructure and trans-European transport networks, and in renewable energy infrastructure and energy efficiency. The Cohesion Fund was created in 1993 as an instrument to support the economic convergence of the least developed Member States through investment in infrastructure that would improve their connectivity and competitiveness.

The Cohesion Fund finances investments in transport infrastructure such as roads, motorways, high-speed railway lines, airports and seaports, with a focus on sustainability and connectivity with trans-European transport networks. These investments seek to improve the connectivity of the beneficiary Member States with the rest of the EU, facilitating trade, the mobility of people and economic integration. The Cohesion Fund also finances investments in renewable energy infrastructure such as wind farms, solar plants and hydroelectric power stations, and in the improvement of energy efficiency of electrical grids and public buildings. These investments seek to reduce dependence on fossil fuels, decrease greenhouse gas emissions and improve the energy security of the beneficiary Member States.

The Cohesion Fund has had a significant impact on the modernization of infrastructure in the beneficiary Member States. In Spain, the Cohesion Fund has contributed to the construction of an extensive network of motorways and high-speed railway lines that have significantly improved the country's connectivity. In Portugal, the Cohesion Fund has contributed to the modernization of transport and energy infrastructure that has facilitated the country's economic integration in the EU. In Greece, the Cohesion Fund has contributed to the improvement of transport and energy infrastructure in the islands and rural areas, reducing internal territorial disparities. In the Central and Eastern European countries, the Cohesion Fund has contributed to the construction of transport and energy infrastructure that has facilitated their integration in the European single market.

### The European Agricultural Fund for Rural Development in Detail

The European Agricultural Fund for Rural Development is the EU's financial instrument for supporting rural development and the common agricultural policy in the field of rural development, with an allocation of approximately 95.5 billion euros for the 2021-2027 period. The EAFRD finances rural development measures that seek to improve agricultural competitiveness, promote environmental sustainability of rural areas, improve quality of life in rural areas and diversify the rural economy. The EAFRD operates in all rural regions of the EU, with a focus on areas with the greatest development needs.

The EAFRD finances a wide range of rural development measures. In the field of agricultural competitiveness, the EAFRD finances investment aid for the modernization of agricultural holdings, aid for the installation of young farmers, aid for the creation of producer groups, aid for the diversification of agricultural activity towards non-agricultural activities such as rural tourism, and aid for the creation of small rural enterprises. In the field of environmental sustainability, the EAFRD finances aid for organic farming, aid for agri-environmental practices, aid for sustainable management of natural resources, aid for biodiversity protection, and aid for adaptation to climate change. In the field of quality of life, the EAFRD finances aid for village renewal, aid for improvement of rural public services such as education, health and transport, aid for preservation of rural cultural heritage, and aid for the creation of rural development associations.

The EAFRD also finances Cooperation for Rural Development, which promotes cooperation between different rural actors, including farmers, businesses, civil society organizations and public administrations, to develop common projects that improve competitiveness, sustainability and quality of life in rural areas. The EAFRD also finances Rural Development Groups, which are local associations that design and implement participatory rural development strategies that respond to the needs and priorities of each territory. Rural Development Groups are an example of multilevel governance in cohesion policy, as they involve local, regional and national actors in the design and implementation of rural development strategies.

## Chapter 8: Evaluation and Results of Cohesion Policy

### The Evaluation of Cohesion Policy

The evaluation of cohesion policy is a systematic process that seeks to determine the effectiveness, efficiency and impact of investments financed by the European structural and investment funds. Evaluation is carried out at different levels: ex-ante evaluation, which analyzes the initial situation and investment needs before program implementation; continuous monitoring, which monitors program implementation and result indicators; intermediate evaluation, which analyzes progress made halfway through the programming period; and ex-post evaluation, which evaluates results achieved at the end of the programming period. The evaluation of cohesion policy is fundamental to ensure the effectiveness of investments, identify best practices, detect problems and propose improvements for future programming periods.

The European Commission has developed a robust evaluation framework that includes common performance indicators, evaluation methodologies and guidelines for evaluators. Common performance indicators are quantitative indicators that allow measurement of investment results in areas such as employment, innovation, connectivity, energy transition and social inclusion. These indicators are used to evaluate program progress, compare results between regions and Member States, and determine whether established objectives have been achieved. The Commission also commissions thematic evaluations that analyze specific aspects of cohesion policy, such as the effectiveness of financial instruments, the contribution of cohesion policy to gender equality, or the impact of cohesion policy on rural areas.

Member States and regions also have the responsibility to evaluate the implementation and results of cohesion programs. Program management bodies must collect data on program implementation, report periodically on progress made, and carry out monitoring evaluations to identify problems and propose corrections. Monitoring committees, which include representatives of national and regional authorities, the European Commission, and economic and social agents, supervise program implementation and issue opinions on investment priorities and results achieved. The evaluation of cohesion policy is a participatory process that involves multiple actors, ensuring transparency, accountability and continuous improvement of the policy.

### Results Achieved

Cohesion policy has achieved significant results over more than three decades of existence, contributing substantially to the economic and social development of European regions and to the reduction of territorial disparities. The most important results include improvement of transport and energy infrastructure in the least developed regions, job creation in regions with high unemployment rates, the boost to innovation and research in regions with a delay in technological development, environmental protection and restoration in regions with significant environmental problems, and promotion of social cohesion through investment in education, training and social inclusion. These results have contributed to improving the quality of life of millions of European citizens and to strengthening the economic and social cohesion of the EU.

In the field of infrastructure, cohesion policy has financed the construction and improvement of thousands of kilometers of roads and motorways, hundreds of kilometers of high-speed railway lines, dozens of airports and seaports, and thousands of kilometers of electrical and communications networks. These investments have significantly improved the connectivity of the least developed regions, facilitating trade, the mobility of people and economic integration. Infrastructure improvement has had a positive impact on regional competitiveness, attracting foreign investment, creating employment and stimulating economic growth. The most notable examples include the construction of the motorway network in Spain, modernization of transport infrastructure in Portugal, improvement of communications in Greece, and construction of transport and energy infrastructure in the Central and Eastern European countries.

In the field of employment, cohesion policy has contributed to the creation of millions of jobs in the least developed regions, through investment in vocational training, support for small and medium-sized enterprises, creation of infrastructure that facilitates economic activity, and promotion of innovation and competitiveness. The European Social Fund has financed training programs that have improved the skills of millions of workers, facilitating their access to employment and improving their productivity. The Youth Employment Initiative, financed by the European Social Fund, has contributed to reducing youth unemployment in the regions most affected by the economic crisis, providing training, hiring subsidies and entrepreneurship opportunities to young unemployed people. These results have contributed to reducing unemployment rates in the least developed regions and improving social cohesion.

In the field of innovation, cohesion policy has financed the creation of research centers, science and technology parks, and laboratories in regions with a delay in technological development. These investments have facilitated technology transfer between universities and companies, the creation of technology-based companies, and improvement of regional competitiveness. Cooperation programs between universities, research centers and companies have promoted innovation and knowledge creation in regions that previously had a weak innovation ecosystem. These innovation investments have contributed to diversifying regional economies, creating qualified jobs and improving regional competitiveness in the global economy.

In the field of the environment, cohesion policy has financed the construction of waste treatment plants, remediation of contaminated land, protection and restoration of natural ecosystems, improvement of water quality, and promotion of renewable energy and energy efficiency. These investments have contributed to improving the environmental quality of regions, reducing greenhouse gas emissions and promoting the green transition. Cohesion policy has financed the installation of wind farms, solar plants and other renewable energy infrastructure that have reduced dependence on fossil fuels and contributed to the energy transition. These environmental investments have had a positive impact on citizens' quality of life and on regional sustainability.

## Chapter 9: Future Challenges of Cohesion Policy

### The Green Transition and Cohesion Policy

The green transition is one of the most important challenges facing cohesion policy in the coming decades. The EU has committed to achieving climate neutrality by 2050, and cohesion policy must play a fundamental role in investing in renewable energy infrastructure, energy efficiency, sustainable mobility and waste management that are needed to achieve this objective. The green transition will require massive investments in all European regions, especially in regions that depend on carbon-intensive industries such as mining, the steel industry and the petrochemical industry. Cohesion policy must ensure that the green transition is fair and inclusive, providing financial and technical support to the regions and citizens most affected by the structural changes that the energy transition entails.

The European Green Deal, presented by the Commission in 2019, establishes an ambitious framework for the transition towards a climate-neutral economy by 2050. Cohesion policy is one of the main financial instruments for financing the investments needed to achieve the objectives of the Green Deal, including investment in renewable energy, building energy efficiency, sustainable mobility, the circular economy and biodiversity protection. Cohesion policy must also contribute to regional adaptation to climate change, through investment in resilient infrastructure, protection and restoration of natural ecosystems, and sustainable management of water resources. The green transition is a challenge that requires significant and sustained investment over the coming decades, and cohesion policy must ensure that resources are available to finance this investment.

The green transition also poses specific challenges for regions that depend on carbon-intensive industries. These regions, known as carbon-intensive regions, face the risk of losing employment and economic activity as a result of the energy transition, and need financial and technical support to diversify their economies and restructure their industries. Cohesion policy must provide specific support to these regions through investment in vocational training for affected workers, aid for the creation of new businesses and economic activities, investment in renewable energy infrastructure, and support for research and innovation in clean technologies. Just transition is a fundamental principle of cohesion policy that ensures that the green transition is inclusive and that no one is left behind.

### The Digital Transition and Cohesion Policy

The digital transition is another of the important challenges facing cohesion policy in the coming decades. Digitalization is transforming all sectors of the economy and society, and European regions need to invest in digital infrastructure, digital training and digital innovation to take advantage of the opportunities of the digital revolution and avoid falling behind. The digital divide between urban and rural regions, and between the most prosperous and least developed regions, is a significant challenge that cohesion policy must address through investment in high-speed broadband connectivity in rural areas, digital training of citizens and workers, and innovation in digital technologies such as artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things and cloud computing.

The EU Digital Strategy for 2030 establishes ambitious objectives for the digitalization of the European economy and society, including connectivity of all inhabited areas with high-speed broadband networks, digitalization of public services, digital training of the workforce, and innovation in digital technologies. Cohesion policy is one of the main financial instruments for achieving these objectives, as it finances investments in digital infrastructure, digital training and digital innovation in all EU regions. Cohesion policy must ensure that all regions, including rural areas and the least developed regions, have access to high-speed broadband connectivity and quality digital services.

The digital transition also poses specific challenges for regions that lack digital infrastructure and qualified human capital in digital technologies. These regions need significant investments in connectivity, training and innovation to be able to take advantage of the opportunities of the digital economy and avoid being excluded from the digital revolution. Cohesion policy must provide specific support to these regions through investment in high-speed broadband infrastructure, digital training of citizens and workers, support for enterprises in the adoption of digital technologies, and promotion of innovation in digital technologies. The digital divide is a challenge that requires a coordinated response from all European policies, including cohesion policy, education policy and industrial policy.

### Social Cohesion and Equality

Social cohesion is one of the fundamental objectives of cohesion policy, and faces significant challenges in the coming decades. Social inequalities, including poverty, social exclusion, discrimination and disparities in opportunities, are a significant challenge in many European regions that cohesion policy must address through investment in education, training, employment and social inclusion. Child poverty, energy poverty, social exclusion of migrants and ethnic minorities, and gender inequalities are some of the social challenges that cohesion policy must address to ensure that all European citizens can benefit from European integration.

Cohesion policy has financed significant projects in the field of social cohesion, including training programs for the unemployed, hiring subsidies for young people and people with difficulties accessing employment, social inclusion projects for people at risk of exclusion, and education and training programs to improve the skills of the workforce. These projects have contributed to reducing poverty, social exclusion and disparities in opportunities in many European regions. However, social cohesion remains a significant challenge, especially in the regions most affected by the economic crisis and the structural changes of the economy. Cohesion policy must maintain and strengthen its investment in social cohesion to ensure that all European citizens can benefit from European integration.

Gender equality is a fundamental principle of cohesion policy that must be promoted in all its interventions. Cohesion policy must ensure that women have equal access to employment, training, education and public services, and that investments financed by the structural funds contribute to reducing gender inequalities. Gender-based violence, the gender pay gap, occupational segregation and the lack of shared responsibility in care are some of the gender challenges that cohesion policy must address to promote equality between men and women. Cohesion policy must also promote the social inclusion of people with disabilities, migrants, ethnic minorities and other vulnerable groups, ensuring that all people can benefit from the opportunities offered by European integration.

### Multilevel Governance and Citizen Participation

Multilevel governance is a fundamental challenge for cohesion policy, as the implementation of cohesion programs involves multiple levels of government, from European administrations to local and regional authorities, through national governments. Coordination between these different levels of government is essential to ensure the effectiveness of investments, avoid duplication and complement interventions. Cohesion policy has developed multilevel governance mechanisms that include monitoring committees with representation from all levels of government, coordination mechanisms between different structural funds, and evaluation procedures that involve all relevant actors.

Citizen participation is a fundamental principle of cohesion policy that ensures that investments respond to the needs and priorities of citizens. Cohesion policy promotes the participation of economic and social agents, civil society organizations and citizens in the design and implementation of cohesion programs. Monitoring committees include representatives of economic and social agents, and cohesion programs are designed in consultation with stakeholders. Citizen participation is essential to ensure the democratic legitimacy of cohesion policy and to ensure that investments benefit the citizens who need them most. Cohesion policy must strengthen citizen participation mechanisms to ensure that citizens play an active role in defining investment priorities and in monitoring program implementation.

Transparency and accountability are fundamental principles of cohesion policy that ensure the proper use of public resources. Cohesion policy has developed transparency mechanisms that include the publication of information on programs, projects and results achieved; independent evaluation of investment effectiveness; and control and audit mechanisms that prevent fraud and misuse of funds. Transparency and accountability are essential to maintain citizens' trust in cohesion policy and to ensure that resources are used efficiently and effectively. Cohesion policy must strengthen these mechanisms to ensure maximum transparency and accountability in the management of structural funds.

### Cohesion Policy and EU Enlargement

The possible enlargement of the EU towards the Western Balkans, Ukraine, Moldova and other candidate countries poses significant challenges for cohesion policy. The accession of new Member States with development levels significantly below the European average will require an adaptation of cohesion policy to be able to finance the investments needed to close the development gap. Cohesion policy will need to find a balance between the need to provide sufficient resources to new Member States and the financial sustainability of the European budget. EU enlargement also poses governance challenges, as the accession of new Member States will increase the complexity of coordination between different levels of government and of structural fund management.

The experience of previous enlargements shows that cohesion policy can be an effective instrument to support the integration of new Member States and to facilitate their economic convergence with the more developed countries. The enlargements of 1981, 1986, 1995, 2004 and 2007 were accompanied by significant structural fund investments that contributed to the modernization of infrastructure, job creation and improvement of competitiveness of the new Member States. Cohesion policy played a fundamental role in preparing candidate countries for accession, providing technical and financial assistance for institutional reform, infrastructure modernization and adaptation to the acquis communautaire. The experience of previous enlargements can serve as a reference for future enlargements, demonstrating that cohesion policy can be an effective instrument to support the integration of new Member States.

Cohesion policy must also consider the external dimension of European integration, including cooperation with neighboring countries and development assistance. The Neighbourhood, Development and International Cooperation Instrument finances cooperation projects with the EU's neighboring countries, including the Western Balkans countries, the Eastern Partnership countries and the Southern Mediterranean countries. This cooperation seeks to promote stability, prosperity and security in the EU's neighboring countries, and contributes to preparing candidate countries for accession. Cohesion policy and external cooperation are complementary and must be coordinated to maximize their impact on the development of neighboring countries and on regional stability.

## Chapter 10: Cohesion Policy and Urban Development

### The Urban Challenge in Europe

Europe is a highly urbanized continent, where more than 75% of the population lives in urban areas. European cities are engines of economic growth, innovation and culture, but also face significant challenges such as pollution, traffic congestion, lack of affordable housing, social inequalities, territorial segregation and the effects of climate change. Cohesion policy has progressively recognized the importance of urban development as an essential component of territorial cohesion, and has developed specific instruments to finance investments in European cities that improve the quality of life of their inhabitants and promote sustainable and inclusive urban development.

The European Sustainable Territorial Development Objective, known as the EU Urban Initiative, was established in 2007 as an instrument to promote sustainable urban development through cooperation between European cities. This initiative financed urban development projects in areas such as sustainable mobility, energy efficiency, environmental protection, social inclusion and innovation, and promoted the exchange of good practices among European cities. The Urban Initiative demonstrated that cooperation between cities could generate innovative solutions to common urban challenges, and laid the groundwork for strengthening the urban dimension of cohesion policy in subsequent programming periods.

The URBAN Initiative, launched in 2014, was a complementary instrument of cohesion policy that sought to promote sustainable urban development through cooperation between European cities and the transfer of knowledge and good practices. URBAN financed urban development projects in areas such as regeneration of disadvantaged neighborhoods, improvement of sustainable mobility, building energy efficiency, urban environmental protection and social inclusion. URBAN also promoted the creation of city networks that enabled the exchange of experiences and cooperation between European cities facing similar urban challenges. The URBAN Initiative demonstrated that cooperation between cities could generate innovative and transferable solutions to other urban contexts, and contributed to strengthening the urban dimension of cohesion policy.

### Sustainable Urban Development in Cohesion Policy

Sustainable urban development is a priority of cohesion policy for the 2021-2027 period, with a focus on sustainable urban mobility, building energy efficiency, urban environmental protection, social inclusion and innovation. Cohesion policy finances investments in sustainable urban transport infrastructure such as public transport, infrastructure for cyclists and pedestrians, and shared mobility systems. These investments seek to reduce traffic congestion, greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution in cities, improving air quality and public health. Cohesion policy also finances investments in the energy efficiency of public and private buildings, including energy rehabilitation of residential buildings, installation of efficient heating and cooling systems, and creation of nearly zero-energy buildings.

Cohesion policy also finances investments in the protection and restoration of the urban environment, including the creation and improvement of urban green spaces, sustainable management of water and urban waste, remediation of contaminated land, and adaptation to climate change through green and blue infrastructure that reduce the effects of urban heat islands and improve water management. Cohesion policy also finances the regeneration of disadvantaged neighborhoods through investments in housing, public infrastructure, social services and quality public spaces that improve the quality of life of residents and reduce territorial segregation. Social inclusion is an essential component of sustainable urban development, and cohesion policy finances projects that promote the inclusion of migrants, ethnic minorities and vulnerable groups in urban life.

### The Urban Network of Cohesion Policy

The Urban Network of Cohesion Policy is a platform for cooperation between European cities that seeks to promote knowledge exchange, transfer of good practices and cooperation between cities facing similar urban challenges. The Urban Network brings together cities from different Member States that work together on sustainable urban development projects, sharing experiences and learning from each other. The Urban Network is an example of how cohesion policy can promote cooperation and innovation among European cities, generating solutions transferable to other urban contexts.

The Urban Network organizes knowledge exchange activities such as seminars, workshops and study visits that enable city representatives to learn about good practices from other cities and adapt them to their local context. The Urban Network also facilitates cooperation between cities on sustainable urban development projects, including projects on sustainable mobility, energy efficiency, environmental protection and social inclusion. The Urban Network also promotes citizen participation in sustainable urban development, organizing citizen participation processes that allow residents to contribute to defining investment priorities and implementing urban development projects. The Urban Network is an effective instrument for promoting cooperation between European cities and generating innovative solutions to common urban challenges.

### Integrated Urban Development

Integrated urban development is an approach that seeks to articulate different European policies in a specific urban territory to maximize their impact on sustainable urban development. This approach promotes the coordination of interventions from different European policies, such as cohesion policy, the common agricultural policy, energy policy and research and innovation policy, in a specific urban territory, to avoid duplication, complement interventions and maximize impact on urban development. Integrated urban development is especially relevant for cities that face multiple urban challenges such as pollution, congestion, lack of housing and social inequalities, and that need a coordinated response from all European policies to address these challenges in a comprehensive manner.

Integrated urban development requires the creation of coordination mechanisms between the different public administrations that manage different European policies, including national, regional and local administrations. Integrated urban development also requires the participation of economic and social agents, civil society organizations and citizens in the design and implementation of urban development strategies. Integrated urban development is an approach that seeks to maximize the impact of European investments on sustainable urban development, ensuring that interventions from different European policies are coherent and complementary in a specific urban territory.

Cohesion policy promotes integrated urban development through the strategic planning of investments in urban territories, coordination of interventions from different structural funds, and participation of relevant actors in the design and implementation of urban development strategies. Cohesion policy also promotes cooperation between cities and between regions to develop common solutions to cross-border urban challenges, such as air pollution affecting several cities in different countries, or traffic congestion in metropolitan areas that extend across national borders. Integrated urban development is an approach that seeks to maximize the impact of European investments on sustainable urban development, ensuring that interventions from different European policies are coherent and complementary in a specific urban territory.

## Chapter 11: Cohesion Policy and Cross-Border Cooperation

### The Cross-Border Dimension of Cohesion

The cross-border dimension of cohesion policy recognizes that national borders can be obstacles to the economic and social development of border regions, and that cooperation between regions of different countries can generate significant benefits for citizens living in border areas. Cohesion policy promotes cross-border cooperation through the financing of programs that enable regions of different countries to work together on common projects that transcend national borders. Cross-border cooperation seeks to improve connectivity between border regions, promote economic and social integration, and facilitate the resolution of common problems such as pollution, traffic congestion and natural resource management.

Cross-border cooperation programs are financed by European structural funds and jointly managed by the authorities of participating countries. These programs finance investment projects in transport and energy infrastructure that improve connectivity between border regions, cooperation projects in research and innovation, cooperation projects in education and training, cooperation projects in environmental protection, and cooperation projects in economic and social development. Cross-border cooperation programs also promote cultural exchange and the strengthening of social ties between citizens of border regions, contributing to social cohesion and European identity.

Cross-border cooperation faces specific challenges such as differences in national legislation, language barriers, differences in administrative systems and lack of trust between authorities of different countries. Cohesion policy has developed mechanisms to overcome these challenges, including the creation of joint program management structures, training of officials in cross-border cooperation, and promotion of cultural and linguistic exchange between citizens of border regions. Cross-border cooperation is an example of how cohesion policy can promote European integration at the local and regional level, generating tangible benefits for citizens living in border areas.

### Interreg Territorial Cooperation Programs

The Interreg programs are the main instruments of cohesion policy for promoting territorial cooperation between regions of different European countries. Interreg programs finance cooperation projects in areas such as research and innovation, economic development, environmental protection, education and training, and transport and energy infrastructure. Interreg programs are organized into different categories: cross-border cooperation, transnational cooperation and interregional cooperation, each with its own priorities and financing mechanisms. Interreg programs are jointly managed by the authorities of participating countries, which ensures the participation of all stakeholders in the design and implementation of cooperation projects.

Cross-border cooperation programs finance cooperation projects between regions of neighboring countries that share a land or maritime border. These programs seek to improve connectivity between border regions, promote economic and social integration, and facilitate the resolution of common problems. Transnational cooperation programs finance cooperation projects between regions of different countries that share a common geographical space, such as the Mediterranean region, the Atlantic region or the Central European region. These programs seek to promote regional integration, cooperation in research and innovation, and environmental protection in transnational spaces. Interregional cooperation programs finance projects of good practice exchange and knowledge transfer between regions of different countries that face similar challenges.

Interreg programs have financed thousands of cooperation projects that have generated significant benefits for European citizens. Among the most notable examples are the improvement of cross-border transport infrastructure, such as the construction of bridges and tunnels connecting regions of different countries; cooperation in research and innovation, such as the creation of research center networks working together on common projects; environmental protection, such as joint management of river basins that extend across national borders; and cooperation in education and training, such as the creation of student mobility programs that allow students to study in other countries. Interreg programs are an example of how cohesion policy can promote European integration at the local and regional level, generating tangible benefits for citizens living in border areas.

### Challenges of Cross-Border Cooperation

Cross-border cooperation faces significant challenges that require innovative solutions and the participation of all relevant actors. One of the main challenges is the difference in national legislation, which can make cooperation difficult in areas such as territorial planning, environmental protection and public service delivery. Cohesion policy must promote the convergence of national legislation in areas relevant to cross-border cooperation, facilitating cooperation between authorities of different countries. Another important challenge is the language barrier, which can hinder communication between citizens and authorities of border regions. Cohesion policy must promote language learning and facilitate communication in border areas, through the financing of language training programs and the promotion of bilingualism.

Lack of trust between authorities of different countries is another important challenge for cross-border cooperation. Cohesion policy must promote the creation of joint program management structures that enable authorities of different countries to work together and build mutual trust. Cohesion policy must also promote cultural exchange and the strengthening of social ties between citizens of border regions, contributing to social cohesion and European identity. Cross-border cooperation is a challenge that requires the active participation of all stakeholders, including national, regional and local authorities, economic and social agents, civil society organizations and citizens.

Cross-border cooperation also faces financial challenges, as the costs of cross-border projects may be higher than those of national projects due to the need to coordinate interventions between different countries. Cohesion policy must provide sufficient financing for cross-border cooperation projects, and must establish simplified financing procedures that facilitate the management of cross-border projects. Cross-border cooperation also faces political challenges, as national priorities may conflict with cross-border cooperation priorities. Cohesion policy must promote political dialogue between neighboring countries to overcome these challenges and ensure that cross-border cooperation is a priority for all stakeholders.

### The Future of Cross-Border Cooperation

The future of cross-border cooperation will depend on the capacity of the EU to overcome existing challenges and to adapt cohesion policy to new political and social realities. Cross-border cooperation will be increasingly important in a context of globalization and growing interdependence between European regions, as many challenges such as climate change, pollution, traffic congestion and natural resource management require cross-border responses. Cohesion policy must maintain and strengthen its investment in cross-border cooperation to ensure that border regions can address these challenges jointly and effectively.

Cross-border cooperation will also be important for the integration of new Member States and for the preparation of candidate countries for accession. Cross-border cooperation can facilitate the economic and social integration of new Member States, improving connectivity with neighboring countries and promoting cooperation in areas such as research, innovation and environmental protection. Cohesion policy must promote cross-border cooperation as an instrument of regional integration and accession preparation, providing financing and technical assistance for cross-border cooperation projects.

Cross-border cooperation will also be important for the promotion of social cohesion and European identity. Cross-border cooperation can promote cultural exchange, language learning and the strengthening of social ties between citizens of border regions, contributing to social cohesion and European identity. Cohesion policy must promote cross-border cooperation as an instrument of social cohesion and European identity building, providing financing for cultural exchange projects, language training projects and citizen participation in cross-border cooperation. Cross-border cooperation is a fundamental instrument of cohesion policy that can contribute significantly to European integration and to the building of a more united and more cohesive Europe.

## Chapter 12: Cohesion Policy and Innovation

### Innovation as a Driver of Regional Development

Innovation is a fundamental driver of the economic and social development of European regions, and cohesion policy has progressively recognized its importance as an instrument to improve competitiveness, create employment and diversify regional economies. Innovation is not limited to research and technological development, but also encompasses innovation in production processes, business models, work organization and public service delivery. Cohesion policy promotes innovation in all its forms through investment in research infrastructure, cooperation between universities, research centers and companies, support for innovative small and medium-sized enterprises, and training of qualified human capital in innovation.

The Europe 2020 Strategy for smart, sustainable and inclusive growth established research and innovation as one of the EU's strategic priorities, with the objective of allocating 3% of the EU's gross national product to research and development. Cohesion policy is one of the main financial instruments for achieving this objective, as it finances investments in research infrastructure, cooperation between universities and companies, and training of researchers. Cohesion policy also promotes smart specialization, which is an approach that enables regions to identify and develop their competitive advantages in specific areas of innovation, rather than trying to be competitive in all areas. Smart specialization is an approach that promotes the concentration of research and innovation resources in areas where regions have significant development potential, thus maximizing the impact of innovation investments.

Cohesion policy promotes innovation through the financing of a wide range of instruments, including investment in research infrastructure such as research centers, laboratories and science and technology parks; cooperation between universities, research centers and companies through the creation of research consortia and technology transfer networks; support for innovative small and medium-sized enterprises through research and development aid, aid for the creation of technology-based companies and aid for enterprise innovation; and training of qualified human capital in innovation through doctoral training programs, research scholarships and international mobility programs. Cohesion policy also promotes innovation in public services through digitalization of public administrations, improvement of public service quality and promotion of citizen participation in public service design.

### Innovation Clusters and Smart Specialization

Innovation clusters are geographical groupings of companies, universities, research centers and other organizations that work together in a specific area of innovation, generating synergies and economies of scale that strengthen regional competitiveness. Cohesion policy promotes the creation and development of innovation clusters through investment in shared infrastructure, financing of cooperation projects between cluster members, and promotion of knowledge and good practice exchange. Innovation clusters are an effective instrument for promoting innovation in regions, as they enable concentration of research and innovation resources in specific areas, generation of synergies between companies and research centers, and attraction of foreign investment and international talent.

Smart specialization is a strategic approach that enables regions to identify and develop their competitive advantages in specific areas of innovation, rather than trying to be competitive in all areas. This approach is based on the analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each region, the identification of innovation areas with the greatest development potential, and the concentration of research and innovation resources in these areas. Smart specialization is a requirement for accessing cohesion policy financing in the field of research and innovation, and has been adopted by all European regions as an instrument to guide their innovation strategies. Smart specialization promotes the concentration of resources in areas where regions have significant development potential, thus maximizing the impact of innovation investments and contributing to regional economic development.

Innovation clusters and smart specialization are complementary and reinforce each other. Innovation clusters provide an institutional framework for cooperation between companies, universities and research centers in a specific area of innovation, while smart specialization provides a strategic framework for identifying and developing innovation areas with the greatest development potential. Cohesion policy promotes the combination of both approaches to maximize the impact of innovation investments and to contribute to regional economic development. Innovation clusters and smart specialization are fundamental instruments of cohesion policy for promoting innovation and competitiveness in European regions.

### Technology Transfer and Business Creation

Technology transfer is the process by which the results of scientific and technological research are transferred from the academic and research field to the productive field, for use in the development of new products, processes and services. Cohesion policy promotes technology transfer through the financing of technology transfer centers, the creation of technology transfer networks, and the training of professionals specialized in technology transfer. Technology transfer is a fundamental instrument for converting scientific knowledge into economic activity and employment, and for improving the competitiveness of companies through the adoption of innovative technologies.

The creation of technology-based companies is a fundamental instrument for technology transfer and for the creation of qualified employment in regions. Technology-based companies are companies that are created to commercialize the results of scientific and technological research, developing new products, processes and services based on innovation. Cohesion policy promotes the creation of technology-based companies through the financing of business acceleration programs, the creation of technology business incubators, and the training of entrepreneurs in business and technological skills. The creation of technology-based companies is an effective instrument for diversifying regional economies, creating qualified jobs and improving regional competitiveness in the global economy.

Cohesion policy also promotes innovation in small and medium-sized enterprises, which are the predominant business fabric in most European regions. Small and medium-sized enterprises play a fundamental role in job creation, innovation and economic diversification of regions, but often lack the financial and technical resources needed to invest in research and development. Cohesion policy promotes innovation in small and medium-sized enterprises through the financing of research and development aid, aid for the adoption of innovative technologies, aid for the internationalization of innovative enterprises, and aid for the creation of cooperation networks between small and medium-sized enterprises. Innovation in small and medium-sized enterprises is fundamental to improving regional competitiveness and creating qualified employment in rural and urban areas.

### Training of Human Capital in Innovation

The training of human capital in innovation is an essential element for the development of a knowledge economy in European regions. Cohesion policy promotes the training of human capital in innovation through the financing of doctoral training programs, research scholarships, international mobility programs, and vocational training in technological and innovative skills. The training of human capital in innovation is fundamental to ensure that regions have the qualified professionals needed to develop and maintain competitive innovation ecosystems, and to attract foreign investment and international talent.

Cohesion policy promotes cooperation between universities, research centers and companies in the training of human capital in innovation. This cooperation materializes in dual training programs that combine academic training with practical experience in companies, in international mobility programs that allow students and researchers to undertake stays at other universities and research centers, and in international cooperation programs that promote the exchange of knowledge and good practices between researchers from different countries. Cooperation between universities, research centers and companies is fundamental to ensure that human capital training responds to the needs of the labor market and innovation.

Cohesion policy also promotes vocational training in technological and innovative skills, which are essential for the digital transition and for the adoption of new technologies in companies and public administrations. Vocational training in technological skills includes training in programming, data analysis, artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things, cybersecurity and other emerging technologies. Vocational training is fundamental to ensure that the workforce can adapt to technological changes and to reduce the digital divide between regions. Cohesion policy finances vocational training programs that enable workers to acquire the skills needed to thrive in the digital economy, contributing to social cohesion and regional economic development.

### Innovation in Public Services

Innovation in public services is a fundamental instrument for improving citizens' quality of life and promoting social cohesion. Cohesion policy promotes innovation in public services through digitalization of public administrations, improvement of public service quality, and promotion of citizen participation in public service design. ## Chapter 13: Cohesion Policy and Regional Governance

### Regional Participation in Structural Fund Management

The participation of regions in structural fund management is a fundamental principle of cohesion policy that ensures that investments respond to the needs and priorities of the beneficiary regions. Regional authorities participate in the design and implementation of cohesion programs through monitoring committees, which include representatives of regional and local authorities, the European Commission, national governments, and economic and social agents. Monitoring committees supervise program implementation, evaluate results achieved and issue opinions on investment priorities and necessary modifications. Regional participation in monitoring committees ensures that investments respond to the needs of regions and that local actors participate in decision-making on cohesion policy.

Regional authorities also play a fundamental role in structural fund management as intermediate management bodies. Intermediate management bodies are authorities designated by national governments to manage the implementation of cohesion programs in a specific territory, usually at the regional level. Intermediate management bodies are responsible for project selection, monitoring of implementation, verification of expenditure and evaluation of results. Structural fund management at the regional level allows greater adaptation of investments to the needs and priorities of each territory, and greater effectiveness in program implementation. Cohesion policy has developed management mechanisms that ensure coordination between intermediate management bodies of different regions and different countries, facilitating cross-border and transnational cooperation.

Regional participation in structural fund management also extends to the strategic planning of investments. Regional authorities participate in the development of regional development strategies that define the investment priorities of cohesion policy in each region. These strategies are developed in consultation with stakeholders, including economic and social agents, civil society organizations and citizens, and are based on the analysis of the needs and potential of each region. Strategic planning at the regional level ensures that cohesion policy investments are oriented towards the development of each region's competitive advantages and the reduction of internal territorial disparities.

### Coordination Between Levels of Government

Coordination between the different levels of government is essential to ensure the effectiveness of cohesion policy. Cohesion policy involves multiple levels of government, from European administrations to local and regional authorities, through national governments. Coordination between these levels of government is fundamental to avoid duplication, complement interventions and maximize the impact of investments. Cohesion policy has developed coordination mechanisms that include monitoring committees, coordination conferences and political dialogue mechanisms between the different levels of government.

Monitoring committees are the main coordination mechanism between the different levels of government in cohesion policy. Monitoring committees include representatives of all stakeholders, including national, regional and local authorities, the European Commission, economic and social agents, and civil society organizations. Monitoring committees supervise program implementation, evaluate results achieved and issue opinions on investment priorities and necessary modifications. Monitoring committees are a forum for dialogue and cooperation between the different actors of cohesion policy, and ensure that investments respond to the needs of all stakeholders.

Coordination conferences are coordination mechanisms between intermediate management bodies of different regions and different countries, which seek to facilitate cross-border and transnational cooperation. Coordination conferences enable intermediate management bodies to share experiences, identify cooperation opportunities and coordinate interventions in cross-border and transnational territories. Coordination conferences are an effective instrument for overcoming administrative and language barriers that can hinder cooperation between regions of different countries, and for promoting regional integration at the local and regional level.

Political dialogue mechanisms between the different levels of government are fundamental to ensuring the coherence of public policies and to resolving conflicts that may arise between different administrations. Cohesion policy promotes political dialogue through the creation of inter-institutional conferences, dialogue tables and other mechanisms that enable representatives of the different levels of government to debate investment priorities, resolve conflicts and coordinate interventions. Political dialogue is fundamental to ensuring that cohesion policy is coherent with other public policies and that investments respond to the needs of all citizens, regardless of the level of government to which they are subject.

### Evaluation of Regional Governance

The evaluation of regional governance is a process that seeks to determine the effectiveness of regional participation mechanisms in structural fund management and coordination between the different levels of government. The evaluation of regional governance is carried out through performance indicators that measure the participation of regions in decision-making, the effectiveness of coordination mechanisms, the quality of regional strategic planning, and citizen satisfaction with structural fund management. The evaluation of regional governance is fundamental to identify best practices, detect problems and propose improvements in regional participation mechanisms.

The European Commission has developed guidelines for the evaluation of regional governance, which establish the criteria and indicators that must be used to evaluate the effectiveness of regional participation mechanisms. These guidelines promote participatory evaluation, which involves local and regional actors in the evaluation process, ensuring that evaluation reflects the needs and perspectives of the beneficiary regions. The evaluation of regional governance is a continuous process that is carried out throughout the implementation of cohesion programs, allowing problems to be identified at an early stage and necessary corrections to be made to improve the effectiveness of cohesion policy.

The results of regional governance evaluation are used to improve regional participation mechanisms in structural fund management. Cohesion policy has incorporated lessons learned from previous evaluations to improve management procedures, strengthen coordination mechanisms and promote regional participation in decision-making. The evaluation of regional governance is a fundamental instrument for the continuous improvement of cohesion policy and to ensure that investments respond to the needs and priorities of the beneficiary regions. Regional governance is an essential component of cohesion policy that ensures effectiveness, democratic legitimacy and accountability in the management of structural funds.

Cohesion policy has demonstrated that it is possible to build a more united, more prosperous and more just Europe, in which all citizens, regardless of where they live, can benefit from the opportunities offered by European integration.

Cohesion policy will continue to be a fundamental instrument of European solidarity that contributes to building a fairer, more prosperous and more cohesive Europe for all its citizens.
