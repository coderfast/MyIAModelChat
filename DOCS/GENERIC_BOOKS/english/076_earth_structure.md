This discontinuity is defined as the level where the P-wave velocity increases abruptly from approximately 6.4 kilometers per second at the base of the crust to approximately 8.1 kilometers per second in the upper mantle, an increase that reflects a compositional and mineralogical change between both media. This variation in depth is consistent with the principle of isostasy, which states that regions with greater surface elevation require a thicker crust that acts as a "root" to compensate for the excess mass. The Gutenberg discontinuity is a geophysical boundary of enormous importance that separates the silicate mantle from the metallic core of the Earth, and was discovered in 1914 by the German seismologist Beno Gutenberg, who demonstrated the existence of this discontinuity through the analysis of seismic waves generated by distant earthquakes. ### The Seismic Shadow and Evidence of the Liquid Core

## Chapter 19: Earth's Isostasy

### Principles of Isostasy

Earth's isostasy is the physical principle that describes the gravitational equilibrium of the Earth's lithosphere, according to which the great masses of the Earth's crust such as mountain ranges, continents, and oceanic basins float on the underlying asthenosphere in a manner similar to how icebergs float on water, with a direct relationship between surface elevation and the thickness of the crust beneath it. The concept of isostasy was developed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by geophysicists such as George Airy, John Pratt, and Harold Jeffreys, who proposed different models to explain the isostatic compensation of the Earth's great topographic masses. The Airy model (1855) proposes that the Earth's crust has a constant density but variable thickness, so that regions with greater elevation have a thicker crust that sinks more deeply into the mantle, like a wooden board that sinks deeper into the water the thicker its submerged part. The Pratt model (1855) proposes, conversely, that the crust thickness is constant but the density is variable, so that regions with greater elevation have a less dense crust that floats higher on the mantle. Modern gravimetric and seismic data have demonstrated that both models have elements of truth, and that the actual isostatic compensation is a combination of both mechanisms: the continental crust presents variations in both thickness and density, while the oceanic crust presents compensation primarily through thickness variations. The concept of isostasy also includes the principle of isostatic feedback, according to which changes in surface topography such as erosion, sedimentation, or glacial loading cause isostatic adjustments that modify the depth of the Moho and surface elevation over time.

### Whole-Mantle vs. Layered Convection

Mass flow in the Earth's mantle is the fundamental process that drives plate tectonics, volcanic activity, and heat distribution in the Earth's interior, and there is debate over whether this flow is organized into a single convection system involving the entire mantle (whole-mantle convection) or into two systems separated by the transition zone (layered convection). The whole-mantle convection model proposes that mantle material circulates in large currents extending from the lithosphere to the base of the mantle, with hot material rising at mid-ocean ridges and hot spots, and cold material descending at subduction zones, creating a global circulation system with time scales of 100-200 million years. The layered convection model proposes, conversely, that the mantle transition zone (410-660 km) acts as a barrier separating two independent convection systems: one in the upper mantle (0-660 km) driven by partial melting and basal heating, and another in the lower mantle (660-2,900 km) driven by thermal convection and core interaction. Current evidence suggests that reality is a combination of both models: the transition zone is not an impermeable barrier but presents a flow resistance that affects the geometry of convection currents, resulting in a "partially coupled" convection system where subducting plates can traverse the transition zone but experience deceleration and angle change upon doing so, and mantle plumes can ascend through the entire mantle column but present geometry and temperature variations in the transition zone.

## Chapter 28: The Geological Carbon Cycle

### Silicates, Carbonates, and the Earth's Thermostat

The geological carbon cycle is the process by which carbon circulates between the atmosphere, oceans, lithosphere, and Earth's mantle over time scales of thousands to millions of years, controlling the concentration of atmospheric CO2 and, therefore, the planet's global temperature through the greenhouse effect. The principal mechanism of atmospheric CO2 regulation is the chemical weathering of silicates, a process in which CO2 dissolved in rainwater (forming carbonic acid, H2CO3) reacts with the silicate minerals of the Earth's crust, releasing calcium ions (Ca2+) and bicarbonate (HCO3-) that are transported by rivers to the oceans, where marine organisms build calcium carbonate (CaCO3) shells and skeletons that are deposited on the ocean floor as limestone sediments. The simplified chemical reaction of silicate weathering is: CaSiO3 + 2CO2 + H2O = Ca2+ + 2HCO3- + SiO2, a reaction that consumes two molecules of CO2 for each mole of silicate weathered, providing a mechanism for CO2 removal from the atmosphere that operates on geological time scales. Carbon removed from the atmosphere by silicate weathering is returned to the atmosphere by volcanic activity, which emits CO2 through the decomposition of carbonates during subduction and mantle melting, creating a negative feedback cycle that regulates global temperature over geological time. This feedback cycle, known as the "Urey thermostat" or "silicate thermostat," has maintained the Earth's global temperature within a range of 10-35 degrees Celsius over the last 4 billion years, despite the fact that the Sun's luminosity has increased by 30% since the formation of the solar system, a phenomenon known as the "faint young Sun paradox."

### Subduction and Carbon Recycling

The subduction of oceanic plates transports carbon from the Earth's surface toward the planet's interior, a process that has significant implications for the geological carbon cycle and the long-term regulation of atmospheric CO2. Subducted carbon is presented in multiple forms: organic carbonates (deposited in oceanic sediments as remains of marine organisms), inorganic carbonates (precipitated directly from seawater as limestone and dolomite), particulate organic carbon (deposited in sediments as organic matter), and carbon dissolved in fluids of altered plate. The total amount of carbon subducted annually is estimated at 0.5-2.0 x 10^13 kg of carbon per year, a figure that depends on the sedimentation rate, sediment composition, and subduction velocity at each boundary. During subduction, carbon undergoes a series of transformations: at depths of 100-200 km, carbonates and organic matter react with mantle minerals, releasing CO2 and methane that rise toward the surface through fluids and magma; at depths of 200-400 km, surviving carbonates can be transported into the lower mantle, where they stabilize as magnesite or as carbon in a reduced state (graphite or diamond). The final destination of subducted carbon depends on multiple factors, including the temperature of the subducting plate, the presence of fluids, the composition of the sediments, and the depth of the melting zone, and understanding it is crucial for predicting the evolution of atmospheric CO2 and climate on geological time scales.
## Chapter 29: The Formation of the Earth

### The Nebular Hypothesis and Planetary Accretion

The formation of the Earth occurred approximately 4.56 billion years ago through the accretion of planetesimals (rocky bodies 1-100 kilometers in diameter) orbiting in the protoplanetary disk around the young Sun, a process that took approximately 50-100 million years to complete and involved multiple collisions and mergers of these primitive bodies. The nebular hypothesis, originally proposed by Emanuel Swedenborg in the eighteenth century and developed by Immanuel Kant and Pierre-Simon Laplace in the nineteenth century, postulates that the solar system formed from the gravitational collapse of an interstellar gas and dust cloud (a molecular cloud), which rotated and flattened to form a protoplanetary disk whose center formed the Sun and whose edges formed the planets by accretion of planetesimals. The Earth formed in the inner zone of the protoplanetary disk, at a distance of 1 Astronomical Unit (150 million kilometers) from the Sun, where temperatures were high enough for rocky materials (silicates and metals) to condense, but low enough for water and other volatiles to remain in a solid or gaseous state. The chemical composition of the Earth, with an iron-nickel-rich core and a silicate-rich mantle and crust, is consistent with the composition of chondritic meteorites, which are considered the primitive materials of the solar system that have not experienced planetary differentiation, suggesting that the Earth formed from the accretion of these materials. The accretion rate of the Earth was approximately 10-20 kilometers per year in the early stages, but increased exponentially as the proto-planet's gravity attracted more material, reaching a maximum of 100-1,000 kilometers per year during the final accretion stage, when the Earth already had approximately 80-90% of its current mass.

### Differentiation and the Giant Impact

Earth differentiation is the process by which denser materials (iron and nickel) sank toward the center of the planet to form the core, while less dense materials (silicates) floated toward the surface to form the mantle and crust, a process that occurred during the first tens to hundreds of millions of years of Earth's history and was driven by the heat of accretion, radioactive decay, and planetesimal collisions. Evidence of differentiation is found in the chemical composition of the Earth, which presents a significant enrichment of iron in the core (approximately 85% of the planet's total iron) and a corresponding depletion in the mantle and crust, indicating that iron was extracted from the mantle and transported toward the center during differentiation. The heat generated during differentiation was enormous: the fall of iron from the surface to the center of the Earth (a distance of 6,371 km) releases gravitational potential energy of approximately 2 x 10^32 joules, an amount of energy sufficient to melt the entire mass of the Earth if dissipated locally, suggesting that the Earth may have experienced a state of "magma oceans" during its early history. The Giant Impact is a hypothetical event that occurred approximately 4.5 billion years ago, when a Mars-sized protoplanet (named Theia by Greek mythologists) collided with the proto-Earth in a cataclysmic impact that vaporized much of the crust and mantle of both bodies, creating a disk of orbital debris that eventually merged to form the Moon. ### Chronology of Earth's Formation

The chronology of the Earth's formation can be divided into several major stages, each with characteristic durations and events: initial accretion (4.56-4.50 billion years ago), core differentiation (4.50-4.40 billion years ago), the Giant Impact (4.50-4.40 billion years ago), the formation of the Moon (4.45 billion years ago), the cooling of the magma oceans (4.40-4.00 billion years ago), the formation of the first crust (4.00-3.80 billion years ago), and the onset of plate tectonics (3.00-2.50 billion years ago). Evidence of these stages is found in geological and geochemical records, including the ages of meteorites and lunar rocks, the isotopic ratios of radioactive elements (uranium-lead, samarium-neodymium, lutetium-hafnium), and the chemical composition of the planet's oldest minerals, such as zircon crystals from the Yilgarn Craton in Western Australia, which have ages of 4.404 billion years and provide the oldest direct evidence of solid continental crust. The primitive Earth was an extremely hot and violent planet, with surface temperatures of 1,000-2,000 degrees Celsius during the first 500 million years, a dense atmosphere of CO2, N2, H2O, and volatiles, and intense volcanic activity releasing gases from the mantle to the surface. The formation of the first continents occurred gradually through partial melting of the mantle and accumulation of granitic material, creating primitive continental nuclei such as the Pilbara Craton (Western Australia, 3.5 billion years ago) and the Kaapvaal Craton (South Africa, 3.6 billion years ago), which are the oldest surviving fragments of continental crust.

## Chapter 31: Modern Methods of Studying the Earth's Interior

### High-Resolution Seismology and Finite Elements

Modern methods of studying the Earth's interior have experienced revolutionary advances in recent decades, driven by the development of high-density seismic networks, high-capacity supercomputers, and sophisticated numerical algorithms that enable the construction of three-dimensional images of the planet's internal structure with unprecedented precision. High-resolution seismic tomography uses thousands of earthquakes recorded at thousands of seismographic stations distributed globally to invert the arrival times of P and S waves and construct three-dimensional seismic velocity models that reveal temperature, composition, and aggregation state anomalies of material in the Earth's interior. Finite-element and finite-difference methods solve the complete wave equation, including the reflection, refraction, and diffraction effects of seismic waves, enabling the construction of more precise images of complex structures such as mantle plumes, subducted plates, and heterogeneities in the transition zone. Broadband seismology, which uses surface waves and body waves with frequencies of 0.001-0.1 Hz, enables mapping the mantle structure at scales of 200-1,000 kilometers, while high-frequency seismology, which uses waves of 1-100 Hz, enables studying the structure of the crust and upper mantle at scales of 1-50 kilometers. The combination of multiple seismological methods with gravimetric, magnetic, geochemical, and geodetic data creates an integrated framework for studying the Earth's interior that provides an increasingly complete and detailed vision of the structure and dynamics of the planet.

### High-Pressure and High-Temperature Laboratories

High-pressure and high-temperature laboratories are scientific facilities that reproduce the extreme conditions of the Earth's interior, enabling the study of the physical and chemical properties of minerals and rocks at pressures of hundreds of gigapascals and temperatures of thousands of degrees Celsius, data that are essential for interpreting seismological observations and constructing models of the Earth's interior. The most widely used high-pressure devices are diamond-anvil cells (DACs), which use two diamonds cut with flat tips to compress a sample between them, reaching pressures of up to 400 GPa (sufficient to reproduce the conditions at the center of the Earth) with samples of only micrometers in size. Intermediate-pressure devices such as the multi-anvil and piston-cylinder can reach pressures of up to 25 GPa and 3 GPa respectively, with samples of several millimeters that allow more precise measurements of physical properties.
