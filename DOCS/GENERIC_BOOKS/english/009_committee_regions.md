# The European Committee of the Regions

## Chapter 1: Composition and function

### The members of the Committee

The European Committee of the Regions (CdR) is the advisory assembly of the European Union that represents regional and local authorities. It was created in 1994 by the Treaty of Maastricht and reinforced by the Treaties of Amsterdam and Lisbon, with the aim of ensuring that the voices of regions and municipalities are heard in the European decision-making process. The CdR is currently composed of 329 members, who are representatives of the regional and local authorities of the 27 Member States, appointed by national governments for a five-year term, renewable.

The members of the CdR are representatives of regional and local authorities, which include regional presidents, city mayors, councillors, and other elected officials at regional and local level. Members are appointed by national governments, but act independently of the governments that appointed them. This independence is essential to ensure that members represent the interests of regions and municipalities, and not the interests of national governments. The CdR also has observers from candidate countries, from the European Free Trade Association countries and from other associated countries, who participate in Committee meetings without voting rights.

The composition of the CdR reflects the diversity of regions and municipalities in the EU. Members are distributed among Member States on the basis of their population, which ensures equitable representation. Large countries, such as Germany, France and Italy, have the largest number of members, while smaller countries have a smaller number. The CdR also seeks to ensure equitable representation of women and of different political parties. The diversity of the CdR's members is an asset, as it allows the Committee to draw on a wide range of perspectives and experiences on matters affecting the regions and municipalities of the EU.

### The president and the Committee's bodies

The European Committee of the Regions is presided over by a president elected by the Committee's members for a two-and-a-half-year term, renewable once. The president chairs the Committee's plenary sessions, represents the CdR before other European institutions and oversees the administration of the institution. The president is assisted by a Bureau, which is the Committee's executive body and takes decisions on administrative organisation and political priorities of the CdR. The Bureau is composed of the president, 32 vice-presidents and the presidents of the political groups.

The CdR also has specialised committees that examine matters affecting regions and municipalities. There are currently six committees: the Commission for Regional Policy and EU Budget (COTRA), the Commission for Economic Policy (ECON), the Commission for Social Policy, Employment and Social Affairs (SEDEC), the Commission for the Environment, Climate Change and Energy (ENVE), the Commission for Education, Youth, Culture and Research (EDUC), and the Commission for Institutional Governance and Interinstitutional Affairs (GOV). The committees prepare the CdR's opinions on legislative proposals and examine matters assigned to them by the Bureau.

The CdR also has a General Secretariat that provides administrative and technical support to members and committees. The General Secretariat is composed of some 600 officials, organised into units specialised in different policy areas. The General Secretariat prepares documents for CdR meetings, records adopted opinions and provides technical assistance to members. The efficiency of the General Secretariat is essential for the functioning of the CdR, as it provides the administrative support necessary for the Committee's work.

## Chapter 3: The Committee's opinions

### The opinion procedure

The European Committee of the Regions issues opinions on the European Commission's legislative proposals and on other matters affecting regions and municipalities. The CdR's opinions are advisory, which means that they are not binding, but that the European Commission and the European Parliament are obliged to consult the CdR on legislative proposals that directly affect regional and local competences. The opinion procedure is one of the CdR's main instruments for influencing the European legislative process and for ensuring that the voices of regions and municipalities are heard.

The opinion procedure begins with the assignment of a legislative proposal to a CdR committee. The committee examines the proposal and prepares a draft opinion, which is debated and voted on by the committee. The draft opinion is then sent to the Bureau, which examines it and sends it to the CdR plenary for approval. The plenary debates and votes on the opinion, which is then transmitted to the European Commission, the European Parliament and the EU Council. The opinion procedure is rigorous and ensures that the CdR's opinions are the result of a democratic debate and a detailed analysis of legislative proposals.

The CdR's opinions may include recommendations for modifying legislative proposals, comments on the impact of proposals on regions and municipalities, and suggestions for improving the effectiveness of proposals. The CdR's opinions are an important instrument for the EU's democracy, as they ensure that the voices of regions and municipalities are heard in the decision-making process.

## Chapter 4: The Committee and subsidiarity

### The principle of subsidiarity

The principle of subsidiarity is a fundamental principle of European law, which establishes that decisions should be taken at the level closest to citizens, whenever possible. The principle of subsidiarity is enshrined in Article 5 of the Treaty on European Union, which establishes that the EU acts only in so far as the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, and that by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action. The principle of subsidiarity is essential for the functioning of the EU, as it ensures that regional and local competences are respected and that European legislation is effective and proportionate.

The principle of subsidiarity has two dimensions. The first dimension is competence, which establishes that the EU may only act in areas where the treaties confer competence. The second dimension is proportionality, which establishes that EU action must be appropriate to achieve the objectives pursued and must not go beyond what is necessary. The principle of subsidiarity applies to all areas of EU policy, including areas where the EU shares competence with the Member States, such as education, health, culture and environmental protection.

The principle of subsidiarity is especially important for regions and municipalities, as it ensures that regional and local competences are respected by European legislation. The CdR has defended that the principle of subsidiarity must be applied strictly, to ensure that the EU does not encroach on the competences of national, regional and local governments. The CdR has also defended that regional and local authorities must be consulted before adopting legislative proposals that affect their competences, to ensure that legislation is appropriate to the needs and realities of regions and municipalities.

### The subsidiarity check

The subsidiarity check is a mechanism that allows national parliaments and the European Committee of the Regions to monitor compliance with the principle of subsidiarity by the EU. National parliaments have the right to examine the European Commission's legislative proposals and to issue reasoned opinions if they consider that a proposal violates the principle of subsidiarity. The CdR may also issue opinions on compliance with the principle of subsidiarity, and may bring an action before the Court of Justice if it considers that an EU law violates the principle of subsidiarity.

The subsidiarity check is an important mechanism for the EU's democracy, as it ensures that decisions are taken at the level closest to citizens. The CdR has used this mechanism to defend regional and local competences, and has brought actions before the Court of Justice when it considered that European legislation violated the principle of subsidiarity. The CdR has also issued opinions on compliance with the principle of subsidiarity in various legislative proposals, and has proposed measures to improve the application of the principle.

The principle of subsidiarity and its check are especially important in a context of expansion of EU competences, as European integration has led the EU to assume new competences in areas that were traditionally the competence of national, regional and local governments. The CdR has defended that the expansion of EU competences must be compensated by strengthening the principle of subsidiarity, to ensure that regional and local competences are respected. The CdR has also proposed that the European Commission carry out a regional impact analysis before adopting legislative proposals, to ensure that proposals take into account the needs and realities of regions and municipalities.
