# The Court of Justice of the EU

## Chapter 1: Composition of the Court

### The Judges and the Advocates General

The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) is the supreme judicial institution of the European Union, responsible for ensuring the uniform interpretation and application of EU law. It is composed of a General Court and a Court of Justice proper, known as the "Little Court". The Court of Justice is currently composed of 27 judges, one from each Member State, and 11 Advocates General. The judges are appointed by the governments of the Member States by common agreement, for a renewable six-year term. The judges elect from among themselves a President, who directs the Court and presides over hearings.

The judges of the Court of Justice are selected from among jurists of recognised standing who meet the qualifications required for the exercise of judicial functions in their respective countries. Candidates must be independent, impartial, and have significant experience in EU law. The appointment of judges is a political process, as national governments seek candidates who share their legal views and who are capable of defending the interests of their countries before the Court. However, once appointed, the judges are independent and cannot receive instructions from national governments. This independence is essential to guarantee the impartiality of the Court's decisions.

The Advocates General are appointed by the governments of the Member States, after consulting the Court of Justice. The Advocates General are jurists of recognised standing whose function is to deliver reasoned opinions in the cases assigned to them by the Court. The opinions of the Advocates General are not binding, but the Court usually takes them into account when adopting its decisions. The Advocates General play an important role in the development of EU law, as their opinions analyse the legal questions raised and propose solutions that the Court may follow or reject. The function of Advocate General is one of the most prestigious in the field of EU law.

### The Structure of the Court

The Court of Justice of the EU is composed of two jurisdictions: the Court of Justice proper and the General Court. The Court of Justice is the superior jurisdiction, and its decisions are appealable before the Court itself in exceptional cases. The General Court is a lower jurisdiction that was created in 1989 to relieve the workload of the Court of Justice and to provide citizens and businesses with more direct access to EU justice. The General Court is currently composed of 47 judges, who sit in chambers of three or five judges.

The Court of Justice sits in plenary session, in chambers of three or five judges, or in a Grand Chamber of 15 judges, depending on the importance and complexity of the cases. The plenary session of the Court convenes for the most important cases, such as cases of Member State infringement and preliminary references that raise fundamental legal questions. The chambers of three or five judges handle less complex cases, and the Grand Chambers handle cases that require a more detailed examination. The Court of Justice has its seat in Luxembourg, and its hearings are held in the Justice Building, a modern and functional building that houses the hearing rooms, the judges' offices and the administrative premises.

The Court of Justice also has a Registrar, a Secretary General and administrative staff who support the work of the judges and the Advocates General. The Registrar is a senior official who assists the Court on legal and procedural matters. The Secretary General manages the administration of the Court and coordinates the work of the staff. The Court's administrative staff includes jurists, translators, librarians and other professionals who provide technical and administrative support. The efficiency of the Court depends, to a large extent, on the quality and dedication of its staff.

## Chapter 2: The General Court

### The Jurisdiction of the General Court

The General Court is a jurisdiction of the Court of Justice of the EU that was created in 1989 to relieve the workload of the Court of Justice and to provide citizens and businesses with more direct access to EU justice. The General Court is currently composed of 47 judges, who sit in chambers of three or five judges. The General Court has jurisdiction to hear actions brought by citizens, businesses and Member States against decisions of EU institutions, as well as disputes between EU institutions and their officials.

The General Court has jurisdiction to hear actions for annulment brought against acts of EU institutions, such as regulations, directives and decisions. Actions for annulment may be brought by Member States, by EU institutions or by any natural or legal person who demonstrates that the act directly and individually affects them. The General Court examines whether the contested act is compatible with the Treaties and the general principles of EU law, and may annul the act if it considers it to be illegal. The General Court also has jurisdiction to hear actions for damages, which are brought by persons who have suffered damage as a result of an illegal act of an EU institution.

The General Court also has jurisdiction to hear competition cases, which are brought by businesses against decisions of the European Commission in the field of competition policy. These cases are particularly complex, as they require a detailed analysis of markets and commercial practices. The General Court has developed significant case law in the field of competition, which has contributed to the development of EU law in this area. The General Court also has jurisdiction to hear cases relating to intellectual property, international trade and the environment, among others.

### Access to Justice

Access to justice is a fundamental principle of EU law, and the General Court has played an important role in its promotion. Citizens, businesses and Member States have the right to challenge acts of EU institutions before the General Court, provided they demonstrate that the act directly and individually affects them. This requirement of direct and individual effect has been interpreted by the General Court in a flexible manner, which has allowed an increasing number of persons to access EU justice. The General Court has also reduced the costs and time limits for bringing actions, facilitating access to justice for citizens and businesses of all Member States.

The General Court has also implemented measures to improve the efficiency of its procedures. The Court has introduced expedited procedures for urgent cases, and has created a registry office that assists litigants in bringing their actions. The Court has also implemented measures to reduce the duration of proceedings, including electronic case management and the holding of hearings by videoconference. These reforms aim to improve access to justice and ensure that citizens and businesses receive a swift and effective response to their complaints.

Access to justice in the General Court also has limitations. Representation requirements and court costs can be prohibitive for some citizens and businesses, particularly those from the smaller Member States. The complexity of EU law can also make it difficult to understand the procedures and legal requirements. Despite these limitations, the General Court has demonstrated a commitment to improving access to justice, and has implemented various reforms to make EU justice more accessible and equitable for all.

## Chapter 3: Preliminary Rulings

### The Preliminary Ruling Procedure

The preliminary ruling procedure is one of the most important mechanisms in EU law, as it allows national courts to refer questions to the Court of Justice concerning the interpretation and application of EU law. When a national court is faced with a question on EU law that is necessary to resolve a dispute, it may or must (depending on whether it is a court of last instance or not) refer the question to the Court of Justice for a ruling. The Court of Justice answers the preliminary question by means of a judgment, which is binding on the national court that referred it and on all the courts of the Member States. The preliminary ruling procedure is essential to ensure the uniform interpretation and application of EU law in all Member States.

The preliminary ruling procedure has two main phases. In the first phase, the national court refers the preliminary question to the Court of Justice, setting out the facts of the dispute and the reasons why it considers that an interpretation of EU law is necessary. In the second phase, the Court of Justice examines the question and delivers a judgment interpreting the applicable EU law. The judgment of the Court of Justice is binding on the national court that referred it, which must apply the interpretation provided to the case it is deciding. The judgment is also relevant for all other courts of the Member States, as it establishes a uniform interpretation of EU law that must be followed in similar cases.

The preliminary ruling procedure is a unique mechanism in international law, as it allows national courts to refer questions directly to a supranational court concerning the interpretation of legal rules. This mechanism reflects the special nature of EU law, which is directly applicable in all Member States and which prevails over national law in the event of conflict. The preliminary ruling procedure is also an instrument of dialogue between the Court of Justice and the national courts, as it allows national judges to participate in the development of EU law and to better understand their obligations under the Treaties.

### Preliminary Case Law

The preliminary case law of the Court of Justice is one of the most important sources of EU law, as it interprets the Treaties, regulations and directives, and establishes legal principles that guide the application of EU law in the Member States. Over the decades, the Court of Justice has delivered thousands of preliminary rulings that have had a significant impact on the lives of European citizens. These rulings have interpreted fundamental rights, the free movement of persons, goods, services and capital, competition policy, environmental protection and many other areas of EU law.

The preliminary case law of the Court of Justice has established fundamental principles of EU law, such as the principle of supremacy of EU law over national law, the principle of direct effect of regulations and directives, and the principle of sincere cooperation between the Member States and the EU institutions. These principles are essential for the functioning of the single market and for the guarantee of the rights of European citizens. The Court of Justice has also developed significant case law in the field of fundamental rights, interpreting the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU and establishing standards for the protection of human dignity, freedom, equality and other rights.

The preliminary case law of the Court of Justice also has an impact on national legal systems. National courts are obliged to follow the interpretations of the Court of Justice, which may require them to amend their previous case law or to adapt their procedures to the requirements of EU law. The Court of Justice also has the power to impose sanctions on Member States that fail to comply with its judgments, including fines. These sanctions are essential to ensure that the Court's judgments are respected and that EU law is applied uniformly in all Member States.

## Chapter 4: Infringement Proceedings

### The Infringement Procedure

Infringement proceedings are a legal procedure that allows the Court of Justice to determine whether a Member State has failed to fulfil its obligations under the EU Treaties. The proceedings may be initiated by the European Commission, acting as guardian of EU law, or by another Member State. The proceedings begin with a pre-litigation phase, in which the Commission sends a letter of formal notice to the Member State, informing it of the reasons for its concern and inviting it to submit observations. If the Member State does not remedy the infringement, the Commission may refer the matter to the Court of Justice, which may deliver a judgment finding infringement.

The infringement procedure is an essential instrument to ensure that Member States comply with their obligations under the EU Treaties. EU law is directly applicable in all Member States, and national courts are obliged to interpret and apply EU law uniformly. When a Member State fails to comply with this obligation, the infringement procedure allows the European Commission and the Court of Justice to enforce EU law. The procedure is also an instrument for protecting the rights of citizens, as it ensures that Member States respect the legal rules that protect their rights and interests.

The infringement procedure has several phases. In the first phase, the Commission investigates the infringement and sends a letter of formal notice to the Member State. In the second phase, the Member State submits observations and, if necessary, adopts corrective measures. In the third phase, the Commission evaluates whether the corrective measures are sufficient and, if not, delivers a reasoned opinion setting out the reasons why it considers that the Member State has failed to fulfil its obligations. In the fourth phase, if the Member State does not comply with the reasoned opinion, the Commission may refer the matter to the Court of Justice, which may deliver a judgment finding infringement. The infringement procedure may last several years, depending on the complexity of the case and the willingness of the Member State to remedy the infringement.

### judgments Finding Infringement

When the Court of Justice delivers a judgment finding infringement in infringement proceedings, the condemned Member State is obliged to adopt the necessary measures to comply with the judgment. Compliance with the judgments is supervised by the European Commission, which may verify whether the Member State has adopted the necessary corrective measures. If the Member State does not comply with the judgment, the Commission may refer the matter again to the Court of Justice, which may impose fines on the Member State. The fines may be lump-sum or periodic, depending on the severity of the infringement and the duration of the non-compliance.

The judgments finding infringement of the Court of Justice have a significant impact on the legal and political systems of the Member States. When a Member State is condemned for infringement, it usually adopts swift measures to remedy the infringement, as the fines can be substantial and the political pressure is significant. The judgments finding infringement also have a deterrent effect, as other Member States may see the example and adopt preventive measures to avoid being condemned. The infringement procedure and the judgments finding infringement are therefore essential instruments to ensure that EU law is respected and applied uniformly in all Member States.

Compliance with the judgments of the Court of Justice is a process that can be prolonged and complex. Some Member States may take several years to adopt the necessary corrective measures, especially if the infringements require significant legislative or administrative reforms. The European Commission closely monitors compliance with the judgments, and may adopt additional measures to pressure the Member States. The Court of Justice may also impose staggered fines, which increase over time if the Member State does not comply. These compliance mechanisms are essential to ensure that the Court's judgments are effective and that EU law is respected in practice.

## Chapter 5: Landmark Judgments

### Landmark Cases

The Court of Justice of the EU has delivered numerous judgments that have had a significant impact on the development of EU law and on the lives of European citizens. Some of the most important judgments include the case of Van Gend en Loos (1963), which established the principle of direct effect of EU law, the case of Costa v ENEL (1964), which established the principle of supremacy of EU law over national law, and the case of Cassis de Dijon (1979), which established the principle of mutual recognition in the single market.

The case of Van Gend en Loos was a preliminary ruling that raised the question of whether the regulations of the Treaty of Rome had direct effect in national legal systems. The Court of Justice responded that the regulations are directly applicable in all Member States, which means that citizens may invoke them before national courts without the need for a national implementing provision. This principle of direct effect was revolutionary, as it allowed European citizens to invoke EU law before national courts and to protect their rights directly.

The case of Costa v ENEL was a preliminary ruling that raised the question of whether EU law prevails over national law in the event of conflict. The Court of Justice responded that EU law has primacy over national law, even over the constitutional provisions of the Member States. This principle of supremacy is fundamental to the functioning of the single market, as it ensures that EU law is applied uniformly in all Member States, without national courts being able to invoke their national law to avoid applying EU law.

The case of Cassis de Dijon was a preliminary ruling that raised the question of whether a Member State may prevent the import of a product lawfully manufactured in another Member State. The Court of Justice responded that Member States may not prevent the free movement of goods, unless there are overriding reasons of general interest that justify the restriction. This principle of mutual recognition is fundamental to the single market, as it ensures that products lawfully manufactured in one Member State may be sold in any other Member State without additional restrictions.

### Fundamental Rights

The Court of Justice has played a crucial role in the protection of fundamental rights in the European Union. Although the original Treaties did not include a charter of fundamental rights, the Court of Justice recognised from early on the existence of general principles of law that protect fundamental rights. In 1969, in the Stauder case, the Court recognised that fundamental rights are part of the EU legal order. In 1970, in the Internationale Handelsgesellschaft case, the Court confirmed that fundamental rights are general principles of EU law that must be respected by the institutions and by the Member States.

The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, signed in 2000 and with legally binding force since 2009, codified the fundamental rights recognised by the Court of Justice. The Charter includes civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights, and establishes the principles of human dignity, freedom, equality, solidarity, citizenship and justice. The Court of Justice has used the Charter as the basis for its judgments in the field of fundamental rights, interpreting its provisions and establishing standards for their protection. The Charter has strengthened the protection of fundamental rights in the EU, as it provides a clear and binding legal framework for their protection.

The Court of Justice has also protected fundamental rights against the actions of the Member States. In several cases, the Court has annulled national measures that violated fundamental rights, such as the freedom of movement, equal treatment and the protection of privacy. The Court has also established principles for the protection of fundamental rights in the context of police and judicial cooperation, such as the need to ensure respect for human dignity and privacy in criminal investigations. The case law of the Court of Justice in the field of fundamental rights is one of the most important areas of EU law, and has contributed significantly to the protection of the rights of European citizens.

## Chapter 6: The Court and the Rule of Law

### The Principle of Legality

The principle of legality is a fundamental principle of EU law, which establishes that all actions of EU institutions and of the Member States must be based on law and respect the procedures established by the Treaties. The Court of Justice is the guardian of this principle, as it supervises the legality of the acts of EU institutions and of the actions of the Member States. The Court may annul the acts of EU institutions that it considers to be illegal, and may condemn Member States that fail to comply with their obligations under the Treaties.

The principle of legality is manifested in various areas. In the area of EU institutions, the Court supervises the legality of the acts of the Commission, the Council and the Parliament, and may annul the acts it considers to be illegal. In the area of the Member States, the Court supervises compliance with the obligations of the States under the Treaties, and may impose sanctions for non-compliance. The principle of legality also applies to national public authorities, which must act within the framework of EU law and respect the rights of citizens. The Court of Justice is the guarantor that all actions of the institutions and of the Member States are within the legal framework established by the Treaties.

The principle of legality is essential for the protection of the rights of citizens, as it ensures that public authorities act in a predictable manner and respect the rights established by the Treaties. The Court of Justice has developed significant case law in the field of legality, establishing principles for the protection of the rights of citizens against the actions of public authorities. The principle of legality is also fundamental for the trust of citizens in EU institutions, as it ensures that they act in a transparent and accountable manner.

### Protection of the Rule of Law

The Court of Justice plays a crucial role in the protection of the rule of law in the European Union. The rule of law is one of the fundamental values of the EU, established in Article 2 of the Treaty on European Union, and the Court has the responsibility of ensuring that these values are respected by all institutions and by all Member States. The Court has developed various mechanisms to protect the rule of law, including the supervision of compliance with democratic standards, the protection of judicial independence and the fight against corruption.

The Court of Justice has delivered important judgments in the field of the rule of law, such as the cases concerning the independence of judges in Poland and Hungary. In these cases, the Court has established that judicial independence is a fundamental principle of the rule of law, and that judicial reforms that undermine the independence of judges are contrary to EU law. The Court has also established that Member States have the obligation to guarantee the impartiality of their courts and to protect judges from political pressure. These judgments are essential for the protection of the rule of law in the EU, as they ensure that fundamental values are respected by all Member States.

The Court of Justice has also protected the rule of law by imposing sanctions on Member States that fail to comply with their obligations. The Court has imposed fines on several Member States for failing to transpose EU directives correctly, for failing to apply EU regulations and for failing to respect the rights of citizens. These sanctions are essential to ensure that EU law is respected and that the rule of law is upheld in all Member States. The Court of Justice is, in short, the guardian of the rule of law in the EU, and its work is fundamental to the legitimacy and stability of the European Union.
